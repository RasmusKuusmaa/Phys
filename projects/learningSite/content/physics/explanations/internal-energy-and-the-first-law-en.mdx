The first law of thermodynamics is nothing more exotic than energy
conservation, written for a system's internal energy: ΔU = Q − W, where Q is
heat added to the system and W is work done *by* the system on its
surroundings. Add heat and internal energy rises; have the system do work on
the outside world and internal energy falls, since that work had to come from
somewhere. Neither term alone determines the outcome — a gas compressed
(work done *on* it, negative W) while also losing heat can have its internal
energy rise, fall, or stay put depending on which effect wins, which is
exactly why the equation keeps both terms rather than assuming one dominates.

The crucial property that makes this bookkeeping work is that internal energy
is a *state function*: its value depends only on the system's current
state — its temperature, pressure, composition — and not at all on the history
of how it got there. Heat and work, by contrast, are *not* state functions;
they depend entirely on the path taken. Two different processes connecting the
same starting and ending states can involve wildly different amounts of heat
and work individually, yet ΔU = Q − W must come out identical for both,
because ΔU only cares about the endpoints. This is why measuring the change in
a state function is often far easier than tracking the process itself in
detail — you only need the beginning and the end.

Heat and work are not the same kind of quantity either, despite both being
forms of energy transfer. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature
difference, moving spontaneously from hot to cold; work is energy transferred
by an organised, directed force acting through a displacement. The first law
does not require distinguishing their microscopic mechanisms — both simply
add or remove energy from the system's internal energy — but conflating them
conceptually obscures why the second law of thermodynamics treats them so
differently.
