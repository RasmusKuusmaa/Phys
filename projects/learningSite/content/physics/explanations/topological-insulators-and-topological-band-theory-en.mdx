An ordinary band insulator has a full energy gap separating occupied and unoccupied electron
states throughout its entire bulk, and its surface, in general, is not required to conduct
electricity at all. A topological insulator's bulk is, in this respect, an entirely ordinary
band insulator — a full gap, no bulk conduction — but it possesses a property an ordinary
insulator's bulk band structure lacks: a nonzero topological invariant, a quantity computed
from how the electronic wavefunctions twist and wind across the material's Brillouin zone,
that can only take specific, discrete values and cannot change under any smooth deformation of
the band structure that keeps the energy gap open.

The bulk-boundary correspondence connects this abstract bulk property to something directly
measurable: wherever a topological insulator's bulk meets a region with a different value of
that invariant — most simply, ordinary vacuum or air, which is topologically trivial — the
energy gap is mathematically forced to close somewhere at that boundary, since the invariant
cannot jump discontinuously while the gap stays open. A closed gap at the boundary means
conducting states must exist there, so a topological insulator's edge or surface conducts even
though its bulk does not, a boundary conduction channel whose existence follows from
mathematics rather than from any specific chemical or structural detail of that particular
surface. This is precisely why these surface states are so robust: since their existence is
guaranteed by the bulk topology rather than by fine surface details, disorder, impurities, or
smooth changes to the surface's chemistry cannot remove them without also closing the bulk gap
elsewhere, a far more disruptive change than ordinary surface conduction requires to be
destroyed.

Topological insulators represent a genuinely new organizing principle for classifying phases of
matter, distinct from the traditional Landau paradigm, which classifies phases according to
which symmetries are spontaneously broken (a crystal breaking translational symmetry, a magnet
breaking rotational symmetry, and so on). A topological insulator and an ordinary insulator can
share exactly the same symmetries and yet be physically, measurably distinct — different surface
conductivity — purely because of a difference in bulk topology, a distinction the
symmetry-breaking framework has no vocabulary to describe at all. This realization, developed
substantially in the 2000s, opened an entire research programme classifying topological phases
of matter (including topological superconductors and other related states) as a genuinely
separate axis of classification alongside, not replacing, the older symmetry-breaking framework.
