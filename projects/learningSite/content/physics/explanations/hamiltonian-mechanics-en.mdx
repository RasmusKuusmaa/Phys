Lagrangian mechanics describes a system with position and velocity, producing
one second-order differential equation per coordinate — an equation involving
acceleration, which means solving it requires two pieces of initial
information (position and velocity) per coordinate. Hamiltonian mechanics
performs a change of variables, trading velocity for momentum, and the payoff
is that each single second-order equation splits into *two* first-order
equations: one saying how position changes with time, one saying how momentum
does. Nothing physical is different — a trajectory computed either way is
identical — but the mathematical structure becomes more symmetric and, for many
purposes, more tractable.

The momentum used here, called canonical or generalised momentum, is defined
directly from the Lagrangian rather than assumed in advance, and it does not
always reduce to the familiar mass times velocity. For a charged particle in
a magnetic field, for instance, the canonical momentum includes a contribution
from the field itself, not just from motion — a distinction that matters
enormously once quantum mechanics is built on this same foundation.

The central object, the Hamiltonian H, replaces the Lagrangian and for most
ordinary systems equals the total energy, kinetic plus potential. That
identification is not decoration: Hamilton's equations show that H is
literally the generator of time evolution — the function whose derivatives
tell every coordinate and momentum how to change next. This reframing, energy
as the engine driving the system forward in time rather than merely a
conserved bystander quantity, is what makes Hamiltonian mechanics the
formulation that carries over most directly into quantum mechanics, where the
Hamiltonian plays exactly the same generating role in the Schrödinger
equation.
