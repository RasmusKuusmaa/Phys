Connect a charged capacitor directly to an inductor and something happens with
no battery anywhere in the circuit: the capacitor discharges through the
inductor, building up a magnetic field as it does, and once the capacitor is
empty that field's collapse drives current onward, recharging the capacitor
with the opposite polarity. The process then reverses and repeats. This is
oscillation with no external driver needed, and it is the electrical exact
match of a mass on a spring: the capacitor's electric field storing energy the
way a stretched spring does, the inductor's magnetic field storing energy the
way a moving mass does, and the two trading that energy back and forth every
cycle. Nothing forces this rhythm; it is the natural, resonant behaviour of
the L and C connected together.

The resonant frequency follows from the same mathematics as the mechanical
oscillator, f = 1/(2π√(LC)). Both L and C sit under the square root in the
denominator, so increasing *either* one — not just one of them — slows the
oscillation and lowers the frequency, which surprises anyone expecting more
inductance to mean more "drive". A larger inductor opposes changes in current
more strongly, and a larger capacitor holds more charge at a given voltage;
both effects make each half-cycle take longer.

A real circuit always has some resistance, and resistance changes everything
about the story even though it appears in neither the formula for frequency
above nor in the LC circuit's own energy storage. Resistance dissipates energy
as heat every cycle, exactly as friction drains energy from a swinging
pendulum, so a real RLC circuit's oscillation amplitude decays over time —
damped oscillation, the electrical counterpart of a shock absorber. Push it
periodically at its own resonant frequency, as a radio receiver does when
tuning to a station, and the response grows sharply, the same resonance
behaviour met in mechanical driven oscillators.
