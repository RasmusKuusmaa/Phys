In introductory mechanics, moment of inertia is a single number: I = Sum(m r^2), computed
about one fixed axis, and torque and angular momentum obey the familiar scalar relation
L = I*omega, with L always pointing the same way as omega. That picture is only ever exactly
true for rotation about an axis of symmetry. For a rigid body spinning about an arbitrary
axis, the correct relationship is still L = I*omega, but now I is a 3x3 matrix — the inertia
tensor — and matrix multiplication doesn't generally point the output in the same direction
as the input.

The inertia tensor is built directly from the body's mass distribution: its diagonal entries
are the familiar moments of inertia about the x, y and z axes, and its off-diagonal entries
are products of inertia, which measure how mass is distributed asymmetrically between pairs
of axes. Because it is real and symmetric, linear algebra guarantees it can always be
diagonalized by an orthogonal change of axes — and the axes that do this are the principal
axes. In that rotated frame, the off-diagonal products of inertia vanish, and the three
diagonal entries left over are the principal moments of inertia.

Principal axes are why some spins are stable and others aren't. Only when a body rotates
about a principal axis is its angular momentum guaranteed to point along the same axis as its
angular velocity, staying fixed in the body and constant in the absence of torque. Spin it
about any other axis and, even with zero applied torque, the angular velocity vector sweeps
out a cone around the (still-constant) angular momentum vector — torque-free precession, the
wobble a thrown book or an American football exhibits when it isn't spun cleanly about one of
its own principal axes.

Every rigid body has three principal axes, whether or not it looks symmetric — a lumpy rock
has them just as much as a uniform sphere does; they're simply harder to find without doing
the diagonalization. A sphere's or a cube's principal axes happen to give equal principal
moments (a fully or partly degenerate inertia tensor), which is why spinning them about
almost any axis looks stable; that's a special case of the general theory, not an exception
to needing one.
