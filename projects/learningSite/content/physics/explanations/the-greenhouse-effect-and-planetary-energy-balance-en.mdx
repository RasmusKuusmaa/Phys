Every object that absorbs energy and does not otherwise heat up indefinitely
must radiate that energy away again, and for a planet warmed by its star,
this radiative balance sets its temperature. A planet with no atmosphere
absorbs sunlight, warms up, and radiates energy back to space as
electromagnetic radiation; because the planet is far cooler than its star,
this outgoing radiation is concentrated at much longer wavelengths — mostly
infrared rather than visible light — following the same blackbody radiation
principles that describe emission from any warm object. Equilibrium is
reached when the absorbed power exactly equals the radiated power, and this
balance alone, with no atmosphere involved, sets a "bare planet"
equilibrium temperature that can be calculated purely from the planet's
distance from its star and how much sunlight it reflects (its albedo).

An atmosphere containing greenhouse gases — carbon dioxide, water vapour,
methane, among others — changes this picture through a wavelength-dependent
effect. These gases are largely transparent to incoming visible sunlight, so
they do not significantly block sunlight from reaching and warming the
surface. But they strongly absorb the longer-wavelength infrared radiation
that the warmed surface itself emits, and having absorbed that energy, they
re-radiate it in all directions, including back down toward the surface.
This does not violate energy conservation — the planet as a whole still
radiates to space exactly as much power as it absorbs from the Sun once
overall equilibrium is reached — but the presence of the atmosphere means
the surface itself now receives additional downward radiation beyond direct
sunlight, so a higher surface temperature is needed before the system as a
whole reaches the same outgoing radiative balance. The atmosphere is not
acting like a physical blanket or barrier that blocks outgoing energy
entirely; it is acting as a partially absorbing, partially re-emitting
layer that shifts where in the atmosphere the effective radiating surface
sits, raising the surface temperature needed to maintain the planet's
overall energy balance.

This mechanism explains why planets with thick, greenhouse-gas-rich
atmospheres, such as Venus, have surface temperatures dramatically higher
than their bare-planet equilibrium value would predict, while planets with
thin or absent atmospheres, such as Mars or the Moon, have surface
temperatures much closer to their bare-planet prediction — and it is the
same underlying physics used to model how changes in atmospheric greenhouse
gas concentration on Earth shift the planet's own equilibrium surface
temperature.
