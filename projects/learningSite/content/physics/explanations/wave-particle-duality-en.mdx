Light behaves like a wave in some experiments — producing interference
patterns — and like a stream of particles in others, like the photoelectric
effect. Neither description alone is the full story; light is simply both,
depending on what you ask it to do.

The surprise runs the other way too: matter, not just light, has wave
properties. Louis de Broglie proposed that every particle, including
electrons, has an associated wavelength, related to its momentum — a
relationship strange enough that it was decades before diffraction
experiments confirmed electrons really do produce interference patterns,
just like light does.

This duality doesn't mean a particle is literally split into a wave half
and a particle half at the same time — it's a description that has both
behaviours available, showing whichever one an experiment is set up to
reveal. It's a genuinely strange feature of nature at small scales, not a
compromise or a contradiction to be smoothed away.
