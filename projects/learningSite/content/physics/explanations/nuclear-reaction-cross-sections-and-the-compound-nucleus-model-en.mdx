A nuclear reaction's cross-section quantifies its probability: formally, the reaction rate
equals the cross-section multiplied by the flux of incoming particles and the number of
target nuclei, so the cross-section behaves like an effective area the target presents for
that specific outcome. This is not a literal geometric area — it depends sensitively on the
incoming particle's energy, the specific reaction channel considered, and quantum mechanical
effects that can make it far larger or smaller than the nucleus's actual physical size,
particularly near a resonance, where the cross-section can spike sharply at specific
energies.

Niels Bohr's compound nucleus model explains many of these features. When a particle
(commonly a neutron) is captured by a target nucleus at moderate energy, rather than
interacting with just one or two nucleons and leaving quickly, its energy is often shared
among all the nucleons in the resulting nucleus through many internal collisions, producing a
compound nucleus: a highly excited, roughly thermalized state that persists for a
comparatively long time (long on nuclear timescales) before decaying. The central claim of
this picture, the "independence hypothesis," is that once formed, the compound nucleus's
subsequent decay — which particles it emits, and with what probabilities — depends only on
its excitation energy, angular momentum, and other conserved quantities, not on the specific
process that formed it, since the shared, thermalized energy has erased any detailed memory
of the formation.

This independence hypothesis is what allows compound-nucleus reactions to be treated with a
shared statistical framework regardless of the specific incoming and outgoing particles
involved, and it directly explains the sharp resonance peaks seen in many measured
cross-sections: at specific incoming energies that match a genuine quasi-bound excited state
of the compound nucleus, the reaction proceeds far more readily, producing the peak, while
away from resonance the cross-section is comparatively smooth and small. Not every reaction
proceeds this way, however — at higher energies, or for peripheral collisions where the
incoming particle only grazes the nucleus, direct reactions occur instead, where the incoming
particle interacts with only one or a few nucleons and an outgoing particle is emitted almost
immediately, without the energy ever being shared and thermalized across the whole nucleus,
a genuinely distinct and equally important reaction mechanism.
