A mass on a spring is the archetypal classical oscillator: it can have any
energy at all, set by how far it is pulled back before release, and it can
sit perfectly still at the bottom of its potential well with exactly zero
energy. Solve the Schrödinger equation for the same quadratic potential and
both of those classical facts turn out to be wrong at the quantum scale.

The allowed energies of a quantum harmonic oscillator form a discrete
ladder, En = (n + 1/2)ħω, where ω is the classical angular frequency of the
oscillator and n = 0, 1, 2, ... labels the level. Two features of this
formula are worth dwelling on. First, the levels are evenly spaced: moving
from any level to the next one up always costs exactly the same amount of
energy, ħω, no matter which level you start from — a strikingly different
pattern from, say, the hydrogen atom, whose levels bunch closer together at
higher energy. Second, and more surprising, even the lowest level n = 0 does
not have zero energy: it has E0 = ħω/2, called the zero-point energy. A
quantum oscillator can never be brought to perfect rest at the bottom of its
potential well; even in its ground state it retains an irreducible minimum of
both kinetic and potential energy, a direct consequence of the uncertainty
principle — pinning the particle to an exact position with exact zero
momentum would violate the trade-off between position and momentum
uncertainty.

This zero-point energy is not a mathematical curiosity; it is measurable and
physically consequential. It is part of why solids retain residual atomic
vibration even cooled arbitrarily close to absolute zero, and the same evenly
spaced-ladder structure appears throughout physics wherever a system is
approximately harmonic near a stable equilibrium — vibrating diatomic
molecules and normal modes of a crystal lattice are both, to good
approximation, collections of quantum harmonic oscillators, inheriting both
the even energy spacing and the unavoidable zero-point energy of the idealized
model.
