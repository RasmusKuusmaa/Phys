A galaxy cluster, the largest gravitationally bound structure the universe builds, contains anywhere from a few hundred to several thousand galaxies bound together by their mutual gravity. But those galaxies, however visually prominent, are not where most of the cluster's ordinary matter actually resides. As gas falls into a cluster's deep gravitational potential well, it is shock-heated to extraordinary temperatures -- tens of millions of kelvin -- becoming a diffuse, fully ionized plasma that fills the space between galaxies: the intracluster medium. This gas is hot enough to emit strongly in X-rays, which is how it is observed directly, and once its mass is added up, it substantially exceeds the combined mass of every galaxy in the cluster. The visible galaxies, in other words, are not even the dominant baryonic (ordinary-matter) component of a cluster, let alone its dominant mass component overall.

That last qualification matters, because both galaxies and hot gas together are still a small minority of a cluster's total mass. Gravitational lensing -- the bending of background light by a cluster's total gravitational potential -- provides an independent measurement of where a cluster's mass actually is, regardless of whether that mass is luminous, hot, or dark. These lensing measurements consistently show that dark matter dominates a cluster's total mass budget by a wide margin, with the hot intracluster gas a distant second and the visible galaxies a more distant third still. A cluster's overall mass ranking, from most to least, therefore runs: dark matter, then hot intracluster gas, then galaxies.

The clearest demonstration of this ordering comes from colliding clusters, most famously the Bullet Cluster. When two clusters collide, their galaxies and dark matter, both effectively collisionless on these scales, pass through each other largely unimpeded, continuing on their original trajectories. Their hot gas clouds, however, are not collisionless: gas from the two clusters collides electromagnetically, experiences genuine drag, and slows down, lagging behind and separating spatially from the galaxies and dark matter that sailed on ahead. X-ray observations directly image where the slowed, lagging hot gas ended up, while gravitational lensing maps show the cluster's total mass concentrated instead around the (unimpeded, still-leading) galaxies -- the two maps visibly displaced from one another. This spatial separation between where the ordinary gas sits and where most of the mass actually is, exactly as the collisionless-dark-matter picture predicts, stands as one of the most direct, least model-dependent pieces of observational evidence that dark matter exists as a physically real, separate mass component rather than a modification of gravity acting on ordinary matter alone.
