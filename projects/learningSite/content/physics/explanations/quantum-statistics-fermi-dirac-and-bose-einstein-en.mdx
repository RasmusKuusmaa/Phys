Ordinary statistical mechanics, developed for classical gas particles, treats
particles as distinguishable and allows any number of them to share the same
energy state. Quantum particles violate both assumptions: identical quantum
particles are fundamentally indistinguishable, and whether more than one of
them can occupy the same single-particle quantum state depends entirely on
which of two classes the particle belongs to.

Fermions — the class that includes electrons, protons, and neutrons — are
subject to the Pauli exclusion principle: no two identical fermions can
occupy the same quantum state. Building the statistical distribution that
respects this restriction produces the Fermi-Dirac distribution, which gives
the average occupation number of a state at a given energy and temperature,
capped so that it never exceeds one particle per state. This single
restriction has large consequences: it is the reason electrons in a solid or
in an atom fill states from lowest energy upward instead of all crowding into
the ground state, and the reason a Fermi gas at low temperature has most of
its particles locked into filled low-energy states with only those near the
topmost occupied level — the Fermi energy — free to respond to small
perturbations.

Bosons — the class that includes photons — face no such restriction: any
number of identical bosons can occupy the same state simultaneously. The
resulting Bose-Einstein distribution allows occupation numbers to grow
without the fermionic cap, and in fact makes states of the very lowest
energy increasingly favoured as temperature drops, culminating at low enough
temperature and high enough density in a macroscopic fraction of all
particles collapsing into the single lowest-energy state — a Bose-Einstein
condensate, a distinctly quantum phenomenon with no classical counterpart.

Both distributions converge on the classical Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution
in the same limit: whenever the number of accessible quantum states vastly
outnumbers the particles available to fill them, the probability of two
particles ever competing for the same state becomes negligible, so whether
or not multiple occupancy is technically permitted stops mattering. This is
why ordinary gases at everyday densities and temperatures are well described
by classical statistics even though every constituent particle is, in
principle, a fermion or a boson — quantum statistics is the general theory,
and the classical result is its dilute-gas limiting case.
