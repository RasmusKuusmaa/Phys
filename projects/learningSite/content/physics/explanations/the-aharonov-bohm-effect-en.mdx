Classical electromagnetism treats the scalar and vector potentials as convenient mathematical bookkeeping: only the electric and magnetic fields derived from them are supposed to have direct physical consequences, since the potentials themselves depend on an arbitrary gauge choice and can be changed without altering any field. The Aharonov-Bohm effect, proposed in 1959, showed this picture is incomplete once quantum mechanics is brought in. Consider an electron beam split into two paths that pass on opposite sides of a long, thin solenoid, then recombine to interfere. The solenoid confines its magnetic field entirely to its interior; both electron paths stay in a region where the magnetic field is exactly zero the whole way. Classically, nothing about the solenoid's field should be able to affect electrons that never encounter that field at all.

Quantum mechanically, each path's wavefunction acquires a phase that depends on the vector potential integrated along that specific path, and when the two paths recombine, their relative phase difference -- which determines where the interference pattern's bright and dark fringes land -- turns out to depend on the difference between the two paths' phase integrals. That difference is mathematically equal to the magnetic flux enclosed by the loop formed by the two paths together, which is exactly the flux confined inside the solenoid. So even though neither path ever passes through a nonzero field, the interference pattern shifts measurably as the solenoid's enclosed flux is changed -- a directly observable physical effect with no explanation in terms of local fields alone.

This does not mean the vector potential itself becomes directly, unambiguously measurable the way the fields are; it remains gauge-dependent, and different gauge choices still assign it different values at any given point. What the effect demonstrates instead is that a specific gauge-invariant quantity built from the vector potential -- the phase difference accumulated around a closed loop, equal to the enclosed flux -- has genuine, measurable physical reality, even in a region where every local field vanishes identically. The effect has since been confirmed experimentally multiple times, most conclusively using toroidal superconductor geometries engineered specifically to rule out any possible stray leakage field as an alternative explanation, cementing the vector potential's role as more than a mere calculational convenience in quantum theory.
