Just as electric charge is the source of the electromagnetic force, quarks carry a "colour"
charge (labelled red, green or blue, purely a naming convention with no connection to
visible colour) that is the source of the strong force, mediated by gluons in direct analogy
to how photons mediate the electromagnetic force. But there's a crucial structural
difference: the photon itself carries no electric charge and so doesn't interact with other
photons directly, while gluons do carry colour charge and interact with each other, and with
themselves, directly.

This self-interaction changes the force's behaviour completely: in QED, the electromagnetic
force gets weaker with distance, but in QCD, gluon self-interactions actually cause the
strong force between two quarks to grow stronger as they're pulled apart, rather than
weaker, the opposite of every other fundamental force's distance dependence. Pull two quarks
far enough apart and it eventually becomes energetically cheaper to spontaneously create a
new quark-antiquark pair out of the vacuum than to keep stretching the connection further,
snapping the original pair into two new, colour-neutral composite particles rather than ever
releasing an isolated quark.

This is colour confinement: quarks and gluons, despite being the fundamental constituents of
protons, neutrons and all other strongly-interacting particles, have never been directly
observed as free, isolated particles — they only ever appear bound together into
colour-neutral composites (three quarks for a proton or neutron, a quark-antiquark pair for
a meson). At very short distances, by contrast, the strong force actually becomes weak (a
property called asymptotic freedom, itself a direct consequence of the same gluon
self-interaction), which is why quarks inside a proton behave almost like free particles
when probed at very high energy, even though they can never be pulled out and observed
alone.
