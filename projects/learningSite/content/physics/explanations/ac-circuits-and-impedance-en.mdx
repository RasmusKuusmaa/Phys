On direct current a capacitor is a dead end: charge builds on the plates and
flow stops. On alternating current nothing gets to settle. The supply
reverses, the plates discharge and recharge the other way, and charge keeps
sloshing back and forth through the wires — a real, measurable current, even
though no charge crosses the gap. Raise the frequency and the reversals come
faster, so more charge moves per second and the capacitor opposes the current
*less*. An inductor does the opposite, opposing changes in current and so
obstructing high frequencies most.

This frequency-dependent opposition is called reactance, and it differs from
resistance in a way that matters. A resistor's voltage peaks exactly when its
current peaks. A capacitor's voltage lags a quarter of a cycle behind its
current, an inductor's leads by a quarter cycle. So when a resistor and a
reactive component share a circuit, their peak oppositions occur at different
moments and cannot simply be added up.

Impedance is the quantity that handles this properly, combining resistance
and reactance at right angles — for a series RL circuit, Z = √(R² + X_L²),
not R + X_L. The right-angle combination also produces a phase difference
between the circuit's overall current and voltage, which is why AC power
depends on a power factor rather than just volts times amps. And because
capacitive and inductive reactances move oppositely with frequency, they
cancel at one particular frequency: that is resonance, and it is what lets a
radio pick one station out of the whole band.
