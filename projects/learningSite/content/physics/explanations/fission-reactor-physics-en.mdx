A fission chain reaction is characterized by the effective multiplication factor k: the
average number of neutrons from one fission event that go on to cause a further fission.
When k equals exactly one, the reactor is critical — the reaction rate, and hence the power
output, is stable and self-sustaining without growing or dying out; k below one is subcritical
(the reaction dies out); k above one is supercritical (the reaction grows). A working power
reactor is deliberately engineered and operated to sit at criticality, adjusting conditions
continuously to keep k as close to exactly one as possible, a fundamentally different regime
from the deliberately, rapidly supercritical configuration a nuclear weapon requires.

Neutrons released directly by fission are fast, with energies in the mega-electronvolt range,
but uranium-235's fission cross-section is far larger for slow (thermal) neutrons than fast
ones. A moderator — commonly ordinary water, heavy water, or graphite — slows fission neutrons
down through repeated elastic collisions with light nuclei, without absorbing them
significantly, moving them into the energy range where they are far more likely to cause
another fission rather than simply scattering away or being captured without fissioning. This
is a distinct role from that of the control rods, made of strongly neutron-absorbing material
(such as boron or cadmium), which are inserted or withdrawn to remove neutrons from the
reaction directly and thereby tune k, the actual mechanism by which reactor power is raised,
lowered, or held steady.

Reactor control is only practically achievable because of delayed neutrons: while the vast
majority of fission neutrons are emitted essentially instantaneously ("prompt" neutrons, on a
timescale of microseconds), a small fraction (roughly 0.65% for uranium-235) are emitted
seconds later, as certain neutron-rich fission products undergo beta decay to an excited
daughter state that then emits a neutron. This delayed fraction slows the chain reaction's
overall effective response time from the prompt-neutron microsecond timescale to a timescale
of seconds, precisely matching what mechanically moved control rods can actually track and
respond to — without delayed neutrons, a reactor operating anywhere near criticality would be
essentially impossible to control by mechanical means, since the prompt-neutron chain reaction
alone would respond far too fast for any physical control system to follow.
