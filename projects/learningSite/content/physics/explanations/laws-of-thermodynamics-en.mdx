The first law of thermodynamics is conservation of energy dressed up for
gases and engines: a system's internal energy changes by exactly the heat
added to it minus the work the system does on its surroundings. Nothing
appears from nowhere, and nothing simply vanishes.

Heat and work are two different ways of transferring energy into or out of a
system, and the first law is precisely the statement that they're
interchangeable in their effect on internal energy — you can raise a gas's
internal energy by heating it, by compressing it with a piston, or with some
combination of both, and the law tracks exactly how much each contributes.

A classic trap is treating heat and work as though the first law can't
relate them — it's actually built entirely around relating them.
Compressing a gas quickly with no heat exchanged (an adiabatic process)
still raises its internal energy and its temperature, purely through the
work being done on it, which is exactly why a bicycle pump gets warm when
you use it.
