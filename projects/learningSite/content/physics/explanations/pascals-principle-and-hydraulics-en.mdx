Squeeze an enclosed liquid anywhere and the pressure increase appears
everywhere in it, undiminished. That is Pascal's principle, and it is the
whole basis of hydraulics. Since pressure is force divided by area, applying
the same pressure to a large piston yields a proportionally larger force: a
small effort on a narrow cylinder becomes a big push on a wide one, which is
how a car is lifted by a hand pump and stopped by a foot pedal.

The multiplication has to be paid for, and it is paid in distance. Push the
small piston down a long way and the large one rises only a little, in exactly
the ratio that keeps the volume of liquid constant. Force times distance —
work — comes out the same on both sides, minus friction. Nothing is created;
the lever principle has simply been rebuilt in fluid.

Two practical points. The ratio that matters is *area*, not diameter, and
area goes as the square of the radius — so doubling a piston's diameter
quadruples its force, a factor of four that is easy to miss. And the fluid
must be effectively incompressible for any of this to work. A liquid barely
changes volume under pressure and passes the push straight along; a gas
compresses instead, swallowing the movement and never delivering it. That is
exactly what a bubble of air in a brake line does, and why brake systems are
bled until no gas remains.
