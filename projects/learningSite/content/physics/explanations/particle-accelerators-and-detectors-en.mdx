Particle accelerators use electric fields to increase a charged particle's kinetic energy and
magnetic fields to steer it. A linear accelerator (linac) pushes particles down a straight
line through a sequence of oscillating electric fields timed to accelerate the particle at
each stage; a cyclotron instead bends particles into an outward spiral using a constant
magnetic field, accelerating them each time they cross a gap between electrodes, but this
simple design fails once particle speeds approach light speed, since relativistic mass
increase changes the particle's cyclotron orbital frequency, throwing it out of sync with a
fixed-frequency accelerating field. A synchrotron solves this by confining particles to a
fixed-radius ring and ramping the bending magnetic field's strength up in step with the
particles' rising momentum throughout acceleration, keeping them on the same circular path at
any energy — the design behind essentially every major modern high-energy accelerator.

Colliding two beams of particles head-on, rather than firing a single beam at a stationary
target, dramatically increases the energy actually available to create new particles. In a
fixed-target collision, momentum conservation requires the products to carry away much of the
beam's momentum as overall forward motion, and that bulk kinetic energy is unavailable for
creating new particle masses; only the energy available in the centre-of-momentum frame
actually counts toward particle creation, and for a fixed target that available energy grows
only slowly (as the square root of the beam energy) as beam energy increases. In a collider,
using two beams travelling in opposite directions with equal and opposite momentum, the net
momentum is already close to zero, so essentially none of the beam's energy needs to be
"wasted" on bulk recoil motion, and the available energy for particle creation instead grows
directly with the beam energy — which is why every major discovery machine built to reach the
highest possible particle-creation energies, from the LHC downward, is a collider rather than
a fixed-target accelerator.

What actually comes out of a collision is reconstructed by layered detector systems, since no
single detection technology can measure every relevant particle property at once. Tracking
detectors, placed inside a strong magnetic field, record the curved paths of charged particles,
from which their momentum and charge sign can be reconstructed via the curvature radius.
Calorimeters, placed further out, are designed to fully absorb a particle's energy (through
electromagnetic or hadronic showers) and measure it via the resulting signal, destroying the
particle in the process but yielding a direct energy measurement. Muon detectors, placed
outermost, catch the rare particles (like muons) that pass through the calorimeters largely
unaffected. Combining the information from each layer — trajectory curvature, energy
deposition pattern, and penetration depth — lets physicists reconstruct the momentum, energy,
and probable identity of essentially everything produced in a collision, the experimental
foundation on which the modern Standard Model of particle physics, and much of modern nuclear
physics, has been built and continues to be tested.
