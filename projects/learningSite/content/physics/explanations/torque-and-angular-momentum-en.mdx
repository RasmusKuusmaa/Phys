A force alone does not tell you whether something will turn. Push a door in
the middle of its face and it swings; push the same door just as hard right
next to the hinges, or push straight along its surface towards them, and it
barely moves. What matters is the force, the distance from the pivot, and
the angle between them — combined in the torque, τ = rF sin θ. That sin θ is
the whole reason a wrench has a long handle and you pull it sideways rather
than along its length.

Angular momentum is what torque changes, exactly as ordinary force changes
ordinary momentum. It is not the same quantity as linear momentum wearing a
different hat: a wheel spinning in place has plenty of angular momentum and
zero linear momentum, and a block sliding in a straight line is the reverse.
They are separate bookkeeping, each with its own conservation law.

That conservation law is the useful part. With no net external torque,
angular momentum stays fixed — so if the moment of inertia goes down, the
angular velocity must go up to compensate. This is the spinning skater
pulling her arms in, and nothing is being created when she speeds up: she
changes only how her mass is distributed, and the product of inertia and
spin rate holds. The same bookkeeping keeps a planet's orbit sweeping equal
areas in equal times, and keeps a falling cat's total angular momentum at
zero while it turns itself the right way up.
