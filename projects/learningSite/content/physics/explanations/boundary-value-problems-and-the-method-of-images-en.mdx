Uniqueness theorems for Laplace's equation guarantee that if you find any potential
satisfying the correct boundary conditions on a set of conductors — usually that each
conductor's surface is an equipotential — that potential is the only one that can be
correct, no matter how you found it. This licenses a shortcut: instead of solving the
differential equation directly, guess a configuration of fictitious charges outside the real
region of interest that happens to reproduce the right boundary condition, and the resulting
potential inside the real region is guaranteed to be the true one.

For a point charge held near an infinite grounded conducting plane, the trick is to remove
the plane entirely and replace it with a single fictitious "image" charge of the opposite
sign, placed at the mirror-image position behind where the plane was. The real charge plus
this image charge reproduce exactly the zero-potential boundary condition on the plane's
original location, so — by uniqueness — the field on the real side of the plane, in the
region that actually exists, is identical to what these two point charges alone would
produce, even though the image charge isn't really there.

The real induced surface charge on the actual conductor is not the fictitious image charge —
it's a genuine, physical distribution of charge on the conductor's real surface, and it can
be recovered from the boundary-condition solution as the discontinuity in the field's normal
component there. The method generalizes to a grounded sphere (an image charge at a specific
position inside the sphere, of a specific magnitude, not simply the mirror position) and to
a handful of other symmetric geometries, but it is a trick for specific shapes, not a general
algorithm — most real conductor geometries still require solving Laplace's equation
directly, typically by separation of variables in a coordinate system matched to the
geometry's symmetry.
