Newton's second law, F = ma, is exactly true only in inertial frames — those
moving at constant velocity, with no acceleration of their own. An observer
who is themselves accelerating, like a passenger in a braking car, or
rotating, like someone standing on a carousel, finds that objects around them
appear to accelerate in ways F = ma cannot explain using only the real forces
present. Rather than abandon Newton's law, physics keeps the equation and adds
a correction term — a fictitious force, equal to minus the frame's own mass
times its acceleration, that exists purely as a bookkeeping device for
describing motion from within an accelerating viewpoint.

"Fictitious" describes where the force comes from, not whether its effects are
real. A passenger thrown against their seatbelt when a car brakes suddenly
experiences a completely real deceleration; the seatbelt exerts a completely
real force. What is fictitious is attributing that to some force pushing the
passenger forward — from the road's inertial frame, nothing pushes the
passenger at all. Their body's inertia simply keeps it moving at the car's
original speed while the car (and the seatbelt) decelerates around them, and
the seatbelt's very real force is what then decelerates the passenger too.

The centrifugal force on a spinning carousel is the same story. From the
ground, the actual physics is straightforward: friction (or a handrail)
supplies a real, inward centripetal force that keeps the rider moving in a
circle, precisely as centripetal force always works. From the rider's own
rotating viewpoint, there is no such inward force apparent — they feel
stationary — so an outward centrifugal force has to be invented to explain
why they still feel pressed against the outer edge. Both descriptions predict
the same physical outcome; they differ only in which frame does the
accounting, and switching frames is exactly what makes the "extra" force
appear or vanish.
