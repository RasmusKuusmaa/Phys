Heat moves from hotter to cooler by three genuinely different mechanisms, and
telling them apart is what makes a heat-transfer problem tractable rather than
a single vague notion of "heat flows".

Conduction transfers heat through direct contact, molecule to molecule,
without any bulk motion of the material itself — a hot pan handle heats your
hand because vibrating atoms in the metal pass their energy along
neighbour to neighbour. How fast this happens depends enormously on the
material: metals conduct heat far more readily than wood or plastic, which is
exactly why a metal spoon feels colder than a wooden one even though both sit
at the same room temperature. Nothing about their actual temperature differs;
what differs is how quickly each pulls heat away from your significantly
warmer hand, and metal simply does it faster.

Convection transfers heat by the bulk motion of a fluid — warmer, less dense
fluid rises and is replaced by cooler fluid sinking in, carrying thermal
energy along with the moving material itself. This is why convection needs a
fluid that can flow; it does not occur in rigid solids, where the material
itself cannot circulate. A radiator warms a room mostly by setting up
convection currents in the air, not primarily by direct contact with everyone
in the room.

Radiation is the odd one out: it transfers energy via electromagnetic waves,
which need no medium at all to propagate, and it is the only one of the three
mechanisms that works across a vacuum. This is why sunlight warms the Earth
across 150 million kilometres of empty space, and why radiation is the
dominant heat-loss mechanism from any warm object even in the absence of
surrounding air. In most everyday situations all three mechanisms operate
simultaneously, and solving a real heat-transfer problem starts with
identifying which one dominates.
