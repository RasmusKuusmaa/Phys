Think of a crystal as a stack of parallel, equally spaced planes of atoms, and
send in a beam of X-rays. A little of the beam scatters from the first plane, a
little more from the second, and so on down through thousands of planes. The
wave from the second plane has travelled further than the wave from the first,
by an amount that depends on the angle, and unless that extra path is a whole
number of wavelengths the contributions from successive planes fall out of step
and cancel. Only at the special angles where they stay in step does anything
reach the detector, and the resulting pattern is a set of sharp peaks against
almost nothing.

Writing the extra path out gives Bragg's law: it equals twice the plane spacing
times the sine of the angle between the beam and the planes, and it must equal a
whole number of wavelengths. Two conventions catch people out. The angle is
measured from the planes, not from the normal as in ordinary reflection, so a
detector sitting at twice theta corresponds to a Bragg angle of theta. And
because a sine cannot exceed one, no diffraction happens at all unless the
wavelength is shorter than twice the spacing — with atomic planes a few tenths of
a nanometre apart, that rules out visible light entirely and is why the technique
waited for X-rays.

Turning peaks into a structure has two halves. The peak positions give the set of
plane spacings, and those spacings, with their directions, fix the size and shape
of the unit cell. Where the atoms sit inside the cell is a separate question,
answered by the relative intensities: a heavy atom scatters more strongly, and
atoms arranged so that their scattered waves cancel can extinguish a reflection
that the lattice alone would allow. The awkward part is that a detector measures
intensity and so loses the phase of each reflection — the phase problem, which
made early protein structures years of work and which modern methods sidestep
rather than solve. The same physics with neutrons or electrons in place of
X-rays is the everyday toolkit of materials science, and it is what produced the
photograph from which the double helix was read.
