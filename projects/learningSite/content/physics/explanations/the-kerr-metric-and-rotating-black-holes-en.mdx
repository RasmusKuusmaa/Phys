The Schwarzschild solution to the Einstein field equations describes a black hole with mass but
exactly zero angular momentum, a perfectly non-rotating, spherically symmetric idealization.
Essentially no real astrophysical black hole actually satisfies this idealization: a black hole
formed from the collapse of a rotating star inherits substantial angular momentum, and even a
black hole formed with little spin typically accretes matter carrying angular momentum
afterward, spinning it up over time. The Kerr metric, found by Roy Kerr in 1963, extends the
solution to the physically realistic case of a rotating black hole, and the resulting spacetime
has qualitatively new structure entirely absent from the Schwarzschild case, not merely a small
correction to it.

A rotating black hole drags spacetime itself around with it, an effect called frame-dragging: any
object near the black hole, even one attempting to hold a fixed position relative to a distant
observer using arbitrarily powerful propulsion, is forced to co-rotate to some degree simply by
the geometry of spacetime itself. This effect becomes so extreme within a region called the
ergosphere — which lies entirely outside the event horizon and touches it only at the poles —
that no object whatsoever can remain stationary relative to a distant observer while inside it,
regardless of how it tries to resist. Crucially, the ergosphere is not the same boundary as the
event horizon: an object within the ergosphere, while forced to co-rotate, can still in principle
escape back out across the ergosphere's boundary, unlike the event horizon itself, which remains
the genuine point of no return.

The ergosphere's existence makes possible the Penrose process, a theoretical mechanism (proposed
by Roger Penrose in 1969) for extracting usable energy from a rotating black hole. An object
entering the ergosphere can be split into two fragments in just the right way that one fragment
falls across the event horizon carrying negative energy as measured by a distant observer (a
possibility that exists only within the ergosphere's peculiar geometry), while the other fragment
escapes back out carrying away more energy than the original object possessed. The black hole's
mass and spin both decrease slightly as a result, so the process draws down the black hole's
rotational energy specifically, respecting overall energy conservation rather than violating it —
a rotating black hole's spin is, in this sense, a genuine, extractable energy reservoir, not
merely a passive property.
