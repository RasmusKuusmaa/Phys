A giant molecular cloud is held up against its own self-gravity by the thermal pressure of the cold gas it contains. Whether a given region of the cloud is stable or collapses depends on a direct competition between two energies: the gravitational binding energy trying to pull the region inward, and the thermal kinetic energy of its gas molecules resisting compression. Sir James Jeans worked out the critical mass above which gravity wins -- the Jeans mass -- and it depends sensitively on the cloud's temperature and density: colder, denser gas has a smaller Jeans mass (easier to collapse), while warmer, more diffuse gas has a larger one (harder to collapse). A region below its local Jeans mass sits in genuine, stable hydrostatic equilibrium; only a region above it is gravitationally unstable and begins to collapse once perturbed.

Once collapse begins, something important happens: as the gas contracts and grows denser, the Jeans mass itself keeps falling. A region that started out barely above the Jeans mass for its original density soon finds that its own interior contains many separate sub-regions, each individually above the new, much smaller local Jeans mass. Each of these sub-regions can then collapse on its own, largely independently of its neighbors -- a process called fragmentation. This repeats hierarchically as the gas keeps compressing, at least until the gas becomes dense and opaque enough that the heat released by compression can no longer radiate away efficiently (the opacity limit), at which point further fragmentation halts and the remaining pieces continue collapsing individually into protostellar cores.

This fragmentation cascade, not a single cloud collapsing into a single star, is the physical reason molecular clouds are observed to produce entire clusters of stars in one star-forming event, with a wide range of individual stellar masses rather than one uniform result. Regions like the Orion Nebula are observed doing exactly this: infrared surveys reveal hundreds of embedded protostars forming together out of one giant molecular cloud complex, each descended from its own local fragment of the original, much larger unstable region.
