A star's interior is so opaque that a photon produced by fusion in the core does not travel outward in a straight line at all; it is absorbed and re-emitted an enormous number of times, random-walking outward so slowly that it takes on the order of tens of thousands of years to reach the surface of a star like the Sun, even though light itself moves at the speed of light. What an outside observer actually sees is set not by the core but by the photosphere: a thin layer, only a few hundred kilometers deep in the Sun, where the gas finally becomes transparent enough (optically thin) that photons emitted there have a good chance of escaping to space without being reabsorbed first. The photosphere's temperature is what sets the star's observed spectrum and, to good approximation, its blackbody-like continuum.

The equation of radiative transfer formalizes this picture by tracking a beam of light's specific intensity as it travels through gas that both absorbs and emits along the path. Two competing terms determine how intensity changes with distance: an absorption term that removes photons from the beam, and an emission term (sourced by the gas's own temperature) that adds new photons into it. Deep inside a star, where the gas is highly opaque and in local thermal equilibrium, absorption and emission very nearly balance at every point, and the escaping radiation asymptotically approaches a perfect blackbody spectrum at the local temperature. Moving outward through the atmosphere, the gas becomes progressively less opaque, and the balance shifts until, at the photosphere, the emergent light finally decouples from the gas and travels to the observer largely unchanged.

This same physics explains limb darkening: a line of sight aimed at the center of a star's disk looks straight down into the star, penetrating to hotter, deeper photospheric layers before the gas becomes opaque enough to block further view; a line of sight aimed near the limb (edge) is instead sharply inclined, and reaches optical thickness after passing through only the atmosphere's shallower, cooler outer layers. Since hotter gas radiates more intensity, the center of the disk appears brighter than the limb -- a purely geometric consequence of viewing the same spherically symmetric temperature structure from different angles, not evidence that the star's edge is intrinsically cooler.
