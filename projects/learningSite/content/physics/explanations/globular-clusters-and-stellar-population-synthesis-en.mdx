A globular cluster packs up to a million stars into a dense, gravitationally bound ball, and crucially, essentially all of them formed together, from one collapsing cloud, in one relatively brief star-formation episode. That shared origin means every star in a given cluster started out with the same age and the same initial chemical composition, differing from its neighbors along only one axis: the initial mass each star happened to be assigned by the initial mass function. This makes a globular cluster an unusually clean natural laboratory -- a "coeval population" -- for studying how stars of different masses evolve, since age and composition are held fixed and mass is the only thing varying.

Plotting a globular cluster's stars on a Hertzsprung-Russell diagram exploits this directly. More massive main-sequence stars burn through their core hydrogen supply faster and evolve off the main sequence sooner, so as a coeval population ages, stars are lost from the main sequence starting from the highest masses first, working steadily down to lower and lower masses over time. The specific stellar mass at which stars are just now exhausting their core hydrogen and turning off the main sequence -- the main-sequence turnoff point, clearly visible as a break in the observed diagram -- therefore sits at a mass that depends only on how long the population has existed. Comparing the turnoff point's observed location against stellar-evolution models converts that single feature into a precise, direct measurement of the cluster's age, and globular clusters measured this way turn out to be among the oldest structures in the galaxy, dating back to shortly after the universe's earliest star-forming epochs.

Stellar population synthesis takes this same logic and runs it in the direction astronomers actually need for distant galaxies, which cannot be resolved into individual stars at all. Rather than reading an age off a resolved population's diagram, the technique starts from an assumed initial mass function and star-formation history, builds up a predicted combined spectrum by summing the individual light contributions of a full population of model stars of different masses and ages, and compares that predicted integrated spectrum against a galaxy's actual observed integrated light. Adjusting the assumed age and star-formation history until the predicted and observed spectra match lets astronomers infer a galaxy's stellar age and formation history purely from its unresolved total light, extending the same physics used inside a single, nearby globular cluster out to galaxies far too distant to ever see as anything but a single point of combined light.
