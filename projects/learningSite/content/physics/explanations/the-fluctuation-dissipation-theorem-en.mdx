A resistor at a fixed nonzero temperature generates a small, random, fluctuating voltage
across its terminals even with no current source connected at all — Johnson-Nyquist noise,
a direct consequence of the thermal motion of its charge carriers. The same resistor's
resistance is also exactly what determines how it dissipates energy (as heat) when a real
current is driven through it by an external voltage. These look like two unrelated facts —
one about spontaneous equilibrium fluctuations, one about the response to being driven — but
they are not independent.

The fluctuation-dissipation theorem shows they're the same physics viewed two ways: both the
equilibrium noise and the dissipative response come from the identical underlying
microscopic collisions (electrons scattering off the lattice, in the resistor's case), so
the size of the spontaneous fluctuations and the strength of the dissipative response are
locked together by a precise, quantitative relationship involving only the temperature — not
two independent properties of the material that could vary separately.

This connection was first derived by Einstein for Brownian motion, relating the diffusion
constant of a jostled particle (a fluctuation phenomenon) directly to its mobility under an
applied force (a dissipative, frictional response) through the temperature alone — the
Stokes-Einstein relation is a special case of the general fluctuation-dissipation theorem.
The same underlying idea now underpins Johnson-Nyquist electrical noise, the linewidth of
atomic transitions perturbed by their environment, and quite generally any system close
enough to thermal equilibrium that its response to a small perturbation can be treated as
linear.
