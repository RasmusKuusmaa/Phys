Tidal force is the difference in gravitational pull an extended object feels between its near side and its far side, and it grows stronger the closer an object gets to whatever it is orbiting and the more compact that central mass is. When a star wanders close enough to a supermassive black hole, this differential pull across the star's own diameter can grow strong enough to overwhelm the star's self-gravity -- the same gravity that normally holds the star together as one coherent body -- once the star crosses a specific distance called the tidal radius. Beyond that point, the star is torn apart entirely into a long, elongated stream of debris well before any of it reaches the black hole's event horizon, an event called tidal disruption.

The disrupted debris does not all share the same fate. Roughly half of it, on the side of the encounter closer to the black hole, ends up bound to the black hole on highly elongated orbits, while the other half, having gained enough energy from the encounter's geometry, is placed on unbound trajectories and escapes the system permanently, lost to interstellar space. The bound half returns over time (the exact timing spread out because debris on different parts of the elongated stream falls back at different rates) and gradually circularizes, through the same physics that shapes any accretion flow, into a temporary accretion disk around the black hole.

It is this newly formed accretion disk, not any nuclear process, that powers a tidal disruption event's observed flare: gravitational potential energy released as the bound debris settles into the disk is converted into an intense burst of radiation, rising over days as debris accumulates and circularizes, then fading over months to years as the disk's fuel supply is gradually exhausted. This distinctive rise-and-fade light curve, together with its characteristic spectral signature, lets astronomers identify a tidal disruption event and distinguish it from an ordinary supernova occurring by coincidence near a galaxy's center. Because most supermassive black holes are otherwise completely dormant, radiating essentially nothing between such events, tidal disruption events offer astronomers one of the only direct ways to catch an inactive supermassive black hole in the act, briefly announcing its presence through the violent consumption of a single unlucky star.
