Ordinary ("first-quantized") quantum mechanics describes a fixed number of particles, each
with its own wavefunction, with the correct symmetrization under particle exchange imposed
by hand afterward. Second quantization instead builds the symmetry in from the start by
switching description entirely: instead of asking "where is particle 1, where is particle
2," it asks "how many particles occupy single-particle state 1, how many occupy state 2,"
and so on — a state is completely specified by this list of occupation numbers, one per
available single-particle state.

Creation and annihilation operators, a-dagger and a, add or remove exactly one particle from
a specified single-particle state, and the entire many-particle Hilbert space — called Fock
space — is built up by applying creation operators to a reference "vacuum" state with zero
particles in every mode. For bosons, any occupation number is allowed and the
creation/annihilation operators obey the same commutation relations as the quantum harmonic
oscillator's ladder operators; for fermions, the operators instead anticommute, and this
anticommutation automatically forces occupation numbers to be either 0 or 1 — the Pauli
exclusion principle falls straight out of the algebra, rather than needing to be imposed as
a separate rule.

This formalism is essential whenever the particle number genuinely changes — a photon being
emitted or absorbed, particle creation and annihilation in high-energy collisions, or
phonons appearing and disappearing in a solid — situations first-quantized quantum
mechanics, with its fixed particle count, simply has no natural way to describe. It's also
the natural language for many-body quantum systems in condensed matter and nuclear physics,
where tracking each of an enormous number of identical particles individually would be
hopeless, but tracking occupation numbers of the available states remains entirely
tractable.
