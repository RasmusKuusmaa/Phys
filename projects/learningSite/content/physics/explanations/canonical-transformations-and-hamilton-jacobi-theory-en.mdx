Hamiltonian mechanics already buys you a lot of freedom in how you describe
a system, but not every change of position and momentum variables is
allowed. A canonical transformation is one that keeps Hamilton's equations
looking exactly like Hamilton's equations in the new coordinates — it
preserves the underlying structure (the Poisson brackets), even though the
new coordinates might look nothing like position and momentum in any
familiar sense.

That freedom turns out to be enormous. Among all the canonical
transformations available for a given system, there is (locally) one that
makes the new Hamiltonian identically zero everywhere. If the new
Hamiltonian is zero, Hamilton's equations say the new coordinates don't
change in time at all — they're constants, fixed by the system's initial
conditions. Finding the generating function for that specific
transformation is the content of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, a single
first-order partial differential equation in the old coordinates and time.

This reframes the entire problem. Instead of solving a system of coupled
ordinary differential equations for how position and momentum evolve, you
solve one partial differential equation for a single function, and the
motion falls out algebraically from its derivatives — no further
integration needed. It's rarely the easiest path for a generic problem, but
for systems with enough symmetry it separates beautifully into one ordinary
equation per coordinate, and it's the route that carries most directly into
the WKB approximation and, from there, into quantum mechanics.

The one thing to hold onto: making the Hamiltonian zero is a specific,
maximally ambitious goal for one particular use of canonical
transformations. The transformations themselves are used far more broadly —
simplifying coordinates, or moving to variables suited to periodic motion —
without needing to drive the Hamiltonian all the way to zero every time.
