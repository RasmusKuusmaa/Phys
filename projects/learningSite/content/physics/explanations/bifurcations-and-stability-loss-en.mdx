Fix a nonlinear system's equations and vary one parameter in them — the strength
of the drive, the temperature difference across a fluid layer, the load on a
column. For almost every value of that parameter, changing it slightly does
nothing interesting: the fixed points slide a little and keep whatever stability
they had. A bifurcation is one of the isolated values where that fails, and the
qualitative structure of the solutions changes.

Three of them cover most of what a first course meets. In a saddle-node
bifurcation a stable fixed point and an unstable one approach each other, merge
and disappear, which is how a system can run out of a steady state entirely and
be forced somewhere else. In a pitchfork, a symmetric state stays put but loses
its stability to two new states related by the system's own symmetry — a
compressed column is straight until the critical load and bows left or right
above it, and which side it picks is decided by whatever imperfection is present,
not by the equations. In a Hopf bifurcation the departing stability goes to an
oscillation instead: the fixed point is still there, but the system now circles
it on a small closed loop whose amplitude grows from zero as the parameter
increases. Heart rhythms, chemical oscillators and flutter in an aircraft wing
all start this way.

The distinction that matters in practice is supercritical versus subcritical. In
the supercritical case the new state grows continuously out of the old one, so
crossing the threshold is undramatic and reversible — back the parameter off and
the system returns the way it came. In the subcritical case the branch that takes
over is far away, so the system jumps, and on the way back it stays on the new
branch past the point where it left the old one: hysteresis, and a threshold that
depends on which direction you approached it from. Reading which case you are in
off the diagram is what makes the difference between a design that degrades
gracefully and one that fails all at once.
