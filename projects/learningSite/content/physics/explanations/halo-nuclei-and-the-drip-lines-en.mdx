Starting from any stable isotope and adding more and more neutrons while keeping the proton number fixed eventually reaches a boundary called the neutron drip line: beyond it, the nuclear force can no longer bind one more neutron at all, and any additional neutron separates from the nucleus essentially instantly rather than being retained even briefly. This is a qualitatively different, more extreme boundary than ordinary radioactive instability -- a nucleus well inside the drip line can be unstable and decay (by beta decay, for instance) while still being a genuinely bound system that persists for a measurable time; crossing the drip line itself means there is no bound state left to persist in at all. An analogous proton drip line exists on the proton-rich side.

Right at the edge of the neutron drip line, some light nuclei develop an unusual structure called a halo, first identified in lithium-11. An ordinary nucleus's physical size grows only modestly with its total nucleon number (roughly as the cube root of that number), reflecting nucleons packed together at a roughly constant nuclear density. In a halo nucleus, by contrast, one or two of the most weakly bound valence neutrons are so loosely attached that quantum mechanics allows their wavefunction to extend, with substantial probability, far beyond the compact, ordinary-density core formed by the rest of the nucleons. The result is a nucleus whose overall physical size is dramatically larger than its small nucleon count would predict under ordinary scaling -- lithium-11, with only eleven nucleons, has a physical extent comparable to a nucleus many times heavier, because its two halo neutrons spend so much of their existence orbiting far outside the core.

Halo nuclei matter beyond their own exotic structure because they expose genuine limits of the standard nuclear shell model, which was built primarily on data from stable, ordinary-density nuclei. Near the drip lines, shell gaps can shift, weaken, or vanish entirely, and new "magic numbers" -- values of proton or neutron number associated with especially stable configurations -- have been found that do not match the ordinary magic numbers seen near stability. Studying halo nuclei and the broader physics of the drip lines is therefore a direct experimental probe of how nuclear structure theory needs to be extended or modified at the most extreme, neutron-rich or proton-rich limits of nuclear existence, rather than a routine confirmation of physics already well understood from stable nuclei alone.
