Temperature is often introduced as "the average kinetic energy of the
molecules", and the word doing the quiet work there is *average*. At any
instant a gas contains molecules crawling along and molecules moving several
times faster than typical, constantly trading energy through collisions. The
Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution is the stable shape that this traffic settles
into: a curve rising from zero, peaking near a typical speed, and falling
away with a long tail towards high speeds.

Heating the gas does not slide that curve sideways. The peak moves right, but
the curve also flattens and the tail stretches, because the distribution must
still enclose the same total number of molecules. The consequence matters
more than the shape: the number of molecules above any given speed threshold
grows far faster than the average speed does. Chemical reactions with an
activation barrier speed up dramatically on modest heating for exactly this
reason — it is the tail, not the average, that gets over the barrier.

Mass sets the other axis of the story. Thermal equilibrium equalises average
kinetic energy, not speed, so at the same temperature a light molecule must
move faster than a heavy one. Helium at room temperature has a distribution
reaching far higher speeds than nitrogen does, which is why helium leaks
through small openings more quickly and why Earth's gravity has retained
nitrogen and oxygen while losing almost all of its hydrogen and helium to
space.
