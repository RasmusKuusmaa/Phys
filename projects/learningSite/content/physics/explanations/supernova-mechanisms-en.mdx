A core-collapse supernova happens at the natural end point of a massive star's life: once
its core has fused its way to iron, no further fusion can release energy to support it
against gravity, and the core collapses in barely a second, from roughly the size of the
Earth down to a neutron star a few kilometres across. That collapse halts abruptly when the
core's matter reaches nuclear density and the strong force's short-range repulsion takes
over, sending a shock wave back outward through the still-infalling outer layers of the
star, which — helped enormously by an intense burst of neutrinos released by the
collapse — blows the star's outer layers off entirely in the visible explosion.

A Type Ia supernova is an entirely different physical process with no collapsing
massive-star core at all: it happens when a white dwarf — the compact, degenerate remnant
of a lower-mass star, supported against gravity by electron degeneracy pressure rather than
fusion — accretes additional mass from a companion star (or merges with another white
dwarf) and is pushed close to the Chandrasekhar mass limit, roughly 1.4 solar masses, the
maximum a white dwarf's degeneracy pressure can support. Near that limit, the white dwarf's
core ignites explosive carbon fusion that runs away and disrupts the entire star in a
thermonuclear explosion, leaving no compact remnant behind at all.

Because a Type Ia supernova always detonates at essentially the same mass (the Chandrasekhar
limit), it releases a remarkably consistent amount of energy and reaches a strikingly
similar peak brightness across nearly every occurrence — which is exactly what makes them
useful as "standard candles" for measuring cosmic distances, since a known intrinsic
brightness compared against an observed apparent brightness gives a distance directly.
Core-collapse supernovae show no such consistency, since they depend on the original star's
mass and structure, which varies considerably from star to star, and are instead identified
observationally by the presence of hydrogen lines in their spectra (generally absent in a
Type Ia) and by the compact remnant — a neutron star or black hole — they typically leave
behind.
