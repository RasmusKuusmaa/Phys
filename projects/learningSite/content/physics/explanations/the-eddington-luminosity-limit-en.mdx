Radiation carries momentum, and light streaming outward from a luminous object exerts a genuine outward force on any charged particles in its path, primarily through photons scattering off free electrons in ionized gas (electrons, being far lighter than ions, feel this push far more strongly and drag the ions along with them electrostatically). At the same time, gravity pulls that same gas inward toward the object. As long as gravity wins, the gas stays confined, held in hydrostatic-like equilibrium around the object. But as an object's luminosity rises, so does the outward radiation force, and once luminosity crosses a critical value, the outward push exceeds gravity's inward pull entirely, and the gas is driven away rather than held in place.

This critical value is the Eddington luminosity, named for Arthur Eddington, who worked out the balance in the context of stellar structure. Both the radiation force and the gravitational force on a shell of gas at some radius from the object fall off as the inverse square of that radius, so in the standard derivation (spherical symmetry, fully ionized gas, opacity dominated by electron scattering), the radius cancels out of the balance entirely, leaving a limit that depends only on the object's mass. This is precisely why the same simple formula, with the same simple mass-dependence, applies equally to a hypergiant star and to matter accreting onto a black hole, despite the two situations differing enormously in physical scale.

The Eddington limit shows up across a wide range of astrophysical contexts. The most massive, most luminous stars known sit close to their own Eddington limit and are observed undergoing dramatic, unstable mass-loss episodes as a direct consequence -- radiation pressure periodically winning outright and blowing off outer layers in violent eruptions rather than settling into placid equilibrium. In accretion, matter falling onto a compact object (a neutron star or black hole) releases gravitational energy as radiation, and the accretion rate at which the resulting luminosity reaches the Eddington limit defines the Eddington accretion rate, a natural theoretical ceiling on how fast a compact object can steadily grow by accretion -- highly luminous quasars and X-ray binaries are often observed radiating at a significant fraction of this same limit, evidence that the same radiation-pressure-versus-gravity balance regulates growth from stellar to supermassive-black-hole scales alike.
