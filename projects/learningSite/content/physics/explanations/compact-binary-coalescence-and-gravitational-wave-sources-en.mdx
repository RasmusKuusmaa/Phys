General relativity predicts that any mass distribution with a time-varying quadrupole moment
radiates gravitational waves, carrying away energy and angular momentum from the system. Two
compact objects — black holes, neutron stars, or a mix — orbiting one another satisfy this
condition continuously, so a compact binary is never in a truly static orbit: it steadily loses
energy and angular momentum to gravitational radiation, causing the orbital separation to shrink
gradually and the orbital (and hence gravitational-wave) frequency to rise. This orbital decay
was confirmed indirectly decades before gravitational waves were directly detected, through
precise timing of the Hulse-Taylor binary pulsar, whose orbital period was observed shrinking at
exactly the rate general relativity predicted.

As the two objects spiral closer together, this energy-loss process accelerates: a smaller
orbital separation radiates more strongly, which shrinks the separation faster still, in a
runaway process that culminates, in the final fraction of a second before merger, in the objects
colliding and merging into a single object while emitting a strong, rapidly evolving burst of
gravitational radiation. The resulting signal's frequency and amplitude both rise together
throughout this process, producing the characteristic rising-pitch "chirp" waveform that detectors
like LIGO and Virgo search for — and because the rate at which the frequency rises is directly
determined by the system's total mass and mass ratio (through well-understood general
relativistic equations), the detected waveform's shape alone reveals detailed physical properties
of the merging objects, including their individual masses and spins, without needing any
independent electromagnetic observation of the same event.

Gravitational-wave detectors work on an entirely different principle from any telescope: rather
than collecting electromagnetic radiation, laser interferometers like LIGO measure the tiny,
characteristic stretching and squeezing of spacetime itself that a passing gravitational wave
produces, detecting length changes in kilometers-long detector arms vastly smaller than an
atomic nucleus. This is why gravitational-wave astronomy is often described as a genuinely new
observational sense, complementary to traditional electromagnetic astronomy rather than merely a
more sensitive telescope, and why "multi-messenger" observations — combining a gravitational-wave
detection with simultaneous electromagnetic or neutrino observations of the same event, as
achieved for a neutron star merger in 2017 — are especially valuable, since they combine two
genuinely independent physical channels of information about the same astrophysical event.
