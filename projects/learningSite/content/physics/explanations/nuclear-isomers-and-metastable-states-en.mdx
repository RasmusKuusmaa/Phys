Most excited nuclear states decay to lower-energy states extremely quickly, typically within a
nanosecond or far less, by emitting a gamma-ray photon carrying away the energy difference. But
certain specific excited states, called nuclear isomers, remain trapped for dramatically longer —
anywhere from microseconds to years, and in a handful of known cases, far longer still. An
isomer has exactly the same number of protons and neutrons as the corresponding ground-state
nucleus and is therefore chemically identical to it in every respect that a chemist could
detect; the only difference lies entirely within the nucleus itself, in its internal excitation
and structure.

The dominant reason an isomeric state's decay is suppressed is a mismatch in angular momentum
between the isomeric state and the lower-energy state (often the ground state) it must eventually
transition to. Gamma-ray emission strongly favors transitions involving only a small change in
angular momentum; the probability of a gamma transition drops off steeply as the required change
in angular momentum increases, essentially independent of how much energy is actually available
to be released. An isomeric state that happens to have a very different angular momentum from the
nearby lower-energy states it could transition to therefore has its gamma-decay pathway strongly
suppressed, even if the energy available for the transition is substantial — this angular
momentum mismatch, rather than a small energy gap, is the primary reason isomeric states persist
so much longer than typical excited nuclear states. A less common but related mechanism involves
a large mismatch in nuclear shape (for instance, a strongly deformed isomeric state transitioning
to a much less deformed lower state), which similarly suppresses the transition probability.

Nuclear isomers have practical significance beyond nuclear structure physics itself. Certain
isomers are directly useful as diagnostic tracers in nuclear medicine, chosen specifically because
their intermediate half-life (long enough to administer and image, short enough to limit
radiation exposure) and characteristic gamma-ray energy make them well suited to imaging
techniques like SPECT. Isomers with an unusually large amount of trapped excitation energy have
also motivated speculative proposals for extremely high energy-density storage, since the trapped
isomeric energy could in principle be released on demand — though no practical, controllable
mechanism for triggering isomer decay on demand has yet been demonstrated, and this application
remains firmly in the research and speculative stage rather than practical use.
