An inductor is the electrical mirror image of a capacitor: where a capacitor
opposes changes in *voltage* across it, an inductor opposes changes in
*current* through it, via the electromagnetic induction of the changing
magnetic field its own current creates. Try to change the current suddenly and
the inductor generates a back-voltage fighting the change — not opposing
current itself, since a steady current produces no changing flux and so no
opposition at all.

Close a switch on a battery, resistor and inductor in series and this
opposition governs the whole approach to steady state. The current cannot
jump to its final value instantly, because that would require an infinite
rate of change and therefore an infinite back-voltage. Instead it climbs
exponentially, exactly the shape RC charging takes, with a time constant
τ = L/R rather than RC. A larger inductance means a stronger opposition to
change and so a slower climb; a larger resistance limits the final current but
also, less obviously, makes the *approach* faster, since R appears in the
denominator.

This is the precise inverse of the RC story: there, resistance delayed
voltage; here, resistance (together with inductance) delays *current*. The
inductor's stored energy sits in its magnetic field rather than an electric
one, released as current decays when the source is removed rather than as
voltage decays. Real inductors — solenoid windings, transformer coils, motor
windings — all inherit this current-smoothing behaviour, which is exactly why
inductors are used to suppress sudden current spikes in power supplies.
