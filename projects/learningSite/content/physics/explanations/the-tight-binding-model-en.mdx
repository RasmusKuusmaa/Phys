An isolated atom has sharp, discrete energy levels — an electron in a given orbital has one
specific, definite energy. Bring many identical atoms together into a periodic lattice and
each atom's orbital overlaps slightly with its neighbours', allowing an electron some small
probability of tunneling from one atom's orbital to the next; the tight-binding model builds
the solid's actual electronic states from exactly this picture, treating the lattice as a
periodic array of atoms whose orbitals are "tightly bound" but not perfectly isolated.

That small overlap is enough to split what would be N degenerate copies of the same atomic
energy level (one per atom, if they were truly isolated) into N closely-spaced but distinct
levels once the atoms are brought together, which for a macroscopic number of atoms merge
into a quasi-continuous energy band. The width of that band is set directly by the strength
of the overlap — the "hopping" matrix element between neighbouring orbitals — not by
anything about the original atomic level itself: strongly overlapping orbitals (from closely
spaced atoms, or from wide, diffuse orbitals) give a wide band, weakly overlapping orbitals
give a narrow one.

This is the natural complementary picture to the free-electron model, which starts from
delocalized, nearly-free electrons weakly perturbed by the lattice potential — the two
approaches are opposite starting points that both correctly produce a band structure, and
which one is more useful depends on the material: tight-binding works especially well for
materials with localized, strongly correlated orbitals (transition-metal d-electrons, for
instance), where the free-electron picture's assumption of nearly-free electrons is a poor
starting approximation.
