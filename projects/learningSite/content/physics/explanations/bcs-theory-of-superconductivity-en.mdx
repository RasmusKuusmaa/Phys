Two electrons repel each other directly through the Coulomb force, so a net attraction
between them seems impossible — but in a crystal lattice, an electron moving through the
lattice slightly attracts the nearby positive ions, distorting the lattice around it; that
distortion persists for a short time after the first electron has moved on, and a second
electron passing through the same region is attracted to the resulting slight excess of
positive charge. Mediated by this lattice distortion — equivalently, by the exchange of a
virtual phonon — the net effect between the two electrons can be a weak attraction, even
though their direct Coulomb interaction is repulsive.

This attraction, however weak, is enough to bind pairs of electrons with opposite momentum
and opposite spin into Cooper pairs. Crucially, a Cooper pair, being a bound state of two
fermions, behaves as a composite boson — and unlike the individual electrons that make it
up, which are barred by the Pauli exclusion principle from sharing a state, an unlimited
number of Cooper pairs can condense into the same single collective ground state, in a way
directly analogous to Bose-Einstein condensation.

This collective, coherent ground state of paired electrons is what makes superconductivity
possible: scattering a single electron out of this state (which is what causes ordinary
electrical resistance) would require breaking a Cooper pair, which costs a finite amount of
energy — the superconducting energy gap — so at low enough temperature, with not enough
thermal energy available to break pairs, the current-carrying condensate simply cannot be
scattered and flows with exactly zero resistance. BCS theory correctly explains
conventional, phonon-mediated superconductivity in simple metals, though some
higher-temperature superconductors involve pairing mechanisms not yet fully understood,
showing the theory's explanatory reach has limits.
