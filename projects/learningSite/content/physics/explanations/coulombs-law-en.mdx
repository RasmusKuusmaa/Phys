Coulomb's law describes the electric force between two point charges with the
same mathematical shape as Newton's law of gravitation: proportional to the
product of the two sources, inversely proportional to the square of the
distance between them. F = kq₁q₂/r², with k the Coulomb constant. The
resemblance is not superficial — both are inverse-square laws because both
describe an influence spreading out from a point through three-dimensional
space, so intensity dilutes as the surface area of an expanding sphere, which
grows as r².

The inverse-square part is where intuition needs correcting. Tripling the
distance does not divide the force by three; it divides it by nine, because
the force depends on 1/r², not 1/r. Small changes in separation therefore
produce large changes in force, which is why charged particles feel almost
nothing at a distance and violently strong forces up close.

Unlike gravity, which only attracts, the Coulomb force can attract or repel,
and the rule is simple: like charges repel, opposite charges attract. It is
worth resisting the instinct to read "like" as "belongs together" — two
positive charges are alike in sign and push each other away precisely because
they are alike. And exactly as with gravity, the force is mutual: whatever
force charge A exerts on charge B, charge B exerts back on A with equal
magnitude and opposite direction, Newton's third law applying here just as it
does to any other force.
