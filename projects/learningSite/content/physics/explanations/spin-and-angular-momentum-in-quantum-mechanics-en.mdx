Electrons carry angular momentum that has nothing to do with orbiting
anything. It is intrinsic — as much a fixed property of an electron as its
charge or mass — and it is called spin. The name invites a picture of a
small ball rotating, and that picture fails immediately: an electron has no
measured size to rotate, and reproducing the observed angular momentum with
a classical sphere would need its surface moving faster than light. Spin is
its own thing, with no classical counterpart.

What makes it strange is that it is quantised in half-integer units and,
for an electron, has only two possible measurement outcomes. Choose any axis
at all, measure the spin along it, and you get +ħ/2 or −ħ/2 — never anything
between, and never a value that depends on how the electron was prepared.
The Stern–Gerlach experiment shows this directly: a beam of silver atoms
sent through a non-uniform magnetic field splits into exactly two beams,
where a classical magnetic moment would have smeared into a continuous band.

Spin is what completes the account of atomic structure. It supplies a fourth
quantum number alongside n, l and m, and the Pauli exclusion principle says
no two electrons in an atom may share all four. That does not forbid sharing
an orbital — it forbids sharing a full quantum state. So each orbital holds
exactly two electrons, one of each spin, and no more. Fill the orbitals under
that rule and the periodic table's row lengths, the chemistry of the noble
gases and the reason matter takes up space all follow.
