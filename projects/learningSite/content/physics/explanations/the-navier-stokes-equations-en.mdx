Every specific fluid-flow result met earlier -- Bernoulli's equation, Poiseuille flow through a pipe, even the qualitative onset of turbulence tracked by the Reynolds number -- is a particular solution or a particular regime of one single, much more general underlying law: the Navier-Stokes equations. These equations are nothing more exotic than Newton's second law, force equals mass times acceleration, applied to an infinitesimally small parcel of fluid rather than to a rigid body. The forces balanced are the pressure gradient (fluid pushed from high pressure toward low pressure), viscous stress (internal friction resisting relative sliding motion between neighbouring layers of fluid moving at different speeds), and any external body force such as gravity, with the parcel's resulting acceleration on the other side of the equation. Bernoulli's equation falls out as the special case where viscosity is neglected and the flow is steady; Poiseuille flow falls out as the special case of steady viscous flow confined to a cylindrical pipe under a constant pressure gradient. Different named results throughout fluid mechanics are not separate laws but different simplifying assumptions applied to this one equation.

Despite this conceptual simplicity, the Navier-Stokes equations are notoriously difficult mathematically, because the fluid's own velocity appears nonlinearly in the equations (the fluid's motion advecting, or carrying along, its own momentum), and this nonlinearity is precisely the feature responsible for turbulence's famously chaotic, difficult-to-predict behavior. Numerically, this difficulty is entirely manageable in practice: given a specific starting condition, computers can integrate the equations forward in time to extraordinary accuracy over any finite time window that matters practically, which is exactly how aircraft are designed and storms are forecast days in advance.

But numerically approximating one specific case is a fundamentally different question from proving, in full mathematical generality, that the equations behave well at all. Specifically: starting from any smooth, well-behaved initial fluid velocity field, does a smooth solution to the three-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations always exist for all future time, or can the mathematics itself "blow up," developing an actual infinite value in finite time? Despite the equations' universal practical success, no one has ever proven the answer in general, in either direction -- and this single question, the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, is significant enough to stand as one of the seven Clay Mathematics Institute Millennium Prize Problems, with a million-dollar prize for its resolution. The same equations solved routinely, successfully, and numerically every day thus remain, viewed purely mathematically, one of the deepest open problems in all of applied mathematics.
