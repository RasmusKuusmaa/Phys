A gas has several variables — pressure, volume, temperature — and a
thermodynamic process is what happens when it moves between states. Naming a
process by which variable it holds fixed is not a formality: that single
constraint, combined with the first law, completely determines how heat, work
and internal energy trade off for that process.

An isothermal process holds temperature fixed. Since an ideal gas's internal
energy depends on temperature alone, ΔU = 0 throughout, so by the first law
every joule of heat added must leave again as work done, and vice versa — heat
and work are in lockstep. An isochoric (constant-volume) process holds volume
fixed, and since work in this context is force through a *changing* volume, a
process with no volume change does zero work regardless of how much the
pressure or temperature shifts; every joule of heat added goes directly into
internal energy. An isobaric process holds pressure fixed, letting volume and
temperature move together, with heat splitting between raising the internal
energy and doing work.

An adiabatic process holds *no* thermodynamic variable fixed — what it fixes
is the exchange of heat, Q = 0, not temperature. This is the misconception to
watch for: "adiabatic" and "isothermal" are opposite constraints, not
synonyms. With Q = 0, the first law reads ΔU = −W directly: if the gas does
work by expanding, that work can only be paid for out of its own internal
energy, and since internal energy is tied to temperature, an adiabatically
expanding gas necessarily cools — no heat leaves, but the gas still gets
colder, because energy has to leave through work even when it cannot leave as
heat. This is exactly why a bicycle pump body warms during rapid compression
(adiabatic, work done *on* the gas raises its internal energy) and why gas
released rapidly from a canister feels cold (adiabatic expansion, the gas
does work at its own internal energy's expense).
