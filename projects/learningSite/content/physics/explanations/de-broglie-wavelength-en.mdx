Light shows both wave and particle behaviour — interference patterns on one
hand, photons with definite momentum on the other. De Broglie's proposal, made
before any direct evidence existed for it, was that this duality is not
special to light: *every* particle with momentum has an associated wavelength,
given by the same relation photons already obeyed, λ = h/p. A moving electron
is exactly as much a wave as a photon is, tied to its motion through
momentum rather than through anything unique to electromagnetism.

Because Planck's constant h is extraordinarily small, this wavelength is tiny
for anything with everyday momentum. A thrown baseball's de Broglie
wavelength is dozens of orders of magnitude smaller than the size of a proton
— utterly unmeasurable and physically irrelevant to any experiment ever
performed on it, which is exactly why baseballs never visibly diffract or
interfere. An electron, by contrast, has such a small mass that even modest
momenta give it a wavelength comparable to the spacing between atoms in a
crystal, which is large enough to produce genuinely observable diffraction
patterns when a beam of electrons passes through a crystalline sample — the
direct experimental confirmation of de Broglie's proposal, and today the
working principle behind electron microscopy.

The inverse relationship between wavelength and momentum is worth sitting
with, because it runs opposite to the everyday intuition that "faster should
mean a bigger wave." Higher momentum means a *shorter* wavelength, not a
longer one — accelerating a particle compresses its associated wave rather
than stretching it, exactly the reverse of what raising a wave's amplitude or
speed does to an ordinary mechanical wave.
