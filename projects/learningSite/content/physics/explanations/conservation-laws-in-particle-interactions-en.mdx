Not every reaction that conserves energy and momentum is actually allowed to
happen. Particle physics imposes a further set of conservation laws that any
proposed reaction must satisfy simultaneously, and a reaction violating even
one of them is strictly forbidden, regardless of how much energy is
available to drive it — these laws function as a checklist that a proposed
process must pass in full before it can be taken seriously as physically
possible.

Some of these conservation laws are familiar from earlier physics: total
energy and total momentum must both be conserved in every particle
interaction, exactly as in classical mechanics, now applied relativistically.
Electric charge conservation is similarly strict — the total electric charge
before a reaction must exactly equal the total charge after, with no
exceptions observed in any interaction ever recorded.

Beyond these familiar quantities, particle physics introduces additional
conserved quantum numbers specific to the particle world. Baryon number
counts baryons (particles like protons and neutrons, built from three
quarks) as +1 each and antibaryons as −1, and this total must be conserved
in every known interaction. This law has a strikingly direct experimental
consequence: since a proton is the lightest baryon, and baryon number
conservation forbids it from decaying into any combination of lighter,
non-baryonic particles without violating the baryon count, the proton is
observed to be extraordinarily stable — experiments have searched for proton
decay and found none, placing an enormous lower bound on the proton's
lifetime, consistent with baryon number being an exactly conserved quantity
in the Standard Model. Lepton number plays an analogous role for leptons
(electrons, muons, taus and their neutrinos), similarly forbidding reactions
that would change the total lepton count.

Together, these conservation laws are what make it possible to predict,
before ever running an experiment, which particle reactions can occur and
which cannot — a proposed decay or collision product is ruled out instantly
if it fails to conserve even one of energy, momentum, charge, baryon number,
or lepton number, regardless of how energetically favourable it might
otherwise appear.
