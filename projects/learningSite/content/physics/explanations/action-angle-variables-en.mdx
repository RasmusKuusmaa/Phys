For a one-dimensional system oscillating periodically between two turning points, define the
action J = (1/2*pi) times the closed integral of p dq over one full period of the motion.
Geometrically, J is proportional to the area enclosed by the system's closed orbit in phase
space (position vs. momentum) — a fixed number for a periodic orbit, and different orbits
(different energies) trace out different areas.

J is chosen specifically so that its conjugate variable, the angle variable theta, increases
by exactly 1 (or 2*pi, depending on convention) over one full period, growing uniformly in
time regardless of how the actual position and momentum move within that period — fast near
the turning points, slow near the middle, for a pendulum. Because J and theta are canonically
conjugate and J is conserved (the Hamiltonian expressed in these variables depends only on
J), Hamilton's equations make theta's rate of change a constant: the system's angular
frequency.

This gives an extremely direct route to a periodic system's frequency: omega = dH/dJ,
without ever writing down or solving the equations of motion for position and momentum as
functions of time. For a simple harmonic oscillator this reproduces the ordinary frequency;
the real value shows up in more complicated periodic systems — an anharmonic oscillator, a
particle in a more general potential well — where solving for x(t) directly would be far
harder than computing one integral for J.

Action-angle variables also set up the historically important — if formally superseded — old
quantum theory: Bohr and Sommerfeld's postulate that only orbits with J equal to an integer
multiple of Planck's constant are allowed reproduces, for several exactly solvable systems,
the same energy levels full quantum mechanics gives. Action-angle variables are also the
natural starting point for the WKB approximation, which recovers that same quantization
condition as a genuine semiclassical limit of the Schrodinger equation.
