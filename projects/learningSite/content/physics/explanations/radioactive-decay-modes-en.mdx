An unstable nucleus rids itself of excess energy through radioactive decay,
and different nuclei do this in different characteristic ways. Distinguishing
these decay modes matters because each changes the nucleus differently and
involves a different underlying force.

Alpha decay ejects an alpha particle — a helium-4 nucleus consisting of two
protons and two neutrons bound tightly together — from the parent nucleus.
This reduces the mass number by 4 and the atomic number by 2, transforming
the nucleus into a different element two places lower on the periodic table.
Alpha decay occurs mainly in heavy nuclei, where ejecting a tightly bound
alpha particle is energetically favourable compared to the alternative
configurations.

Beta decay operates through the weak nuclear force rather than the strong
force that governs alpha decay, and converts one type of nucleon into
another inside the nucleus. In beta-minus decay, a neutron converts into a
proton, an electron, and an antineutrino, with the electron and antineutrino
ejected from the nucleus; the atomic number increases by 1 while the mass
number stays the same, since a neutron has simply become a proton. Beta-plus
decay is the mirror process: a proton converts into a neutron, a positron,
and a neutrino, decreasing the atomic number by 1. In neither case is the
emitted electron or positron a pre-existing particle that was "inside" the
nucleus beforehand — it is created at the moment of decay, along with its
accompanying neutrino, out of the energy released by the conversion.

Gamma decay is different in kind from both. It does not transform the
nucleus's identity at all — no protons or neutrons are gained, lost, or
converted. Instead, it occurs when a nucleus is left in an excited energy
state, typically as an aftermath of a prior alpha or beta decay that left
the daughter nucleus with excess internal energy, and that excited nucleus
drops to a lower energy state by emitting a high-energy photon (a gamma
ray) — directly analogous to how an excited atomic electron drops to a
lower orbital by emitting a lower-energy photon, but here the transition
occurs between energy levels of the nucleus itself rather than of an
orbiting electron. Because neither the mass number nor the atomic number
changes, gamma decay leaves the nucleus as the same isotope, just at a
lower, more stable internal energy.
