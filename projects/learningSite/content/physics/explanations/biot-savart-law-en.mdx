Coulomb's law gives the electric field from a point charge; the Biot–Savart
law is its magnetic counterpart, giving the magnetic field produced by a
small element of current-carrying wire. It shares the inverse-square falloff
with distance — both describe an influence spreading through three-dimensional
space from a source — but the direction rule is completely different, and that
difference is the entire content worth understanding.

A point charge's field points radially, straight out along the line from
charge to observation point. A current element's field is perpendicular to
*two* directions at once: the direction of the current itself, and the
direction from the element to the observation point. The field magnitude
involves sin θ between those two directions, exactly as it did for the
Lorentz force, and for the same underlying reason — cross products govern
magnetic effects throughout electromagnetism. Point the current element
straight at the observation point (θ = 0) and the field there is zero,
however large the current; the field is largest directly out to the side of
the wire, where θ = 90°.

Building the total field of a real wire means summing the Biot–Savart
contribution from every infinitesimal segment along its length — an integral,
not a single evaluation, since each segment sits a different distance and
angle from the observation point. This is more laborious than Ampère's law,
but it works for any current geometry, symmetric or not, where Ampère's law
only simplifies calculations when enough symmetry exists to choose a
convenient loop. The two are not competing laws; Biot–Savart is the general
tool and Ampère's law is the shortcut available in the symmetric cases —
current in an infinite straight wire, a long solenoid, a toroid — where it
applies.
