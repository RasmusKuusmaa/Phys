A plane wave in open space can have any frequency and travel in any direction, but confine
it inside a hollow conducting pipe and the boundary condition — no tangential electric field
allowed at a perfect conductor's surface — becomes a constraint the wave's transverse field
pattern must satisfy at every point on the pipe's inner wall. Only a discrete, countable set
of transverse patterns, the waveguide's modes, satisfy that constraint for a given
cross-sectional shape, in exact analogy to how only discrete standing-wave patterns fit a
guitar string pinned at both ends.

Each mode has its own cutoff frequency, set by the pipe's cross-sectional dimensions: below
cutoff, that mode's wave simply cannot propagate down the guide at all — the fields decay
exponentially with distance instead of oscillating, a purely evanescent, non-propagating
disturbance. Above cutoff, the mode propagates, but at a phase velocity that is actually
faster than the speed of light in vacuum (with no contradiction with relativity, since phase
velocity carries no information; the group velocity, which does, stays below c) and depends
on frequency — waveguide propagation is inherently dispersive, unlike a plane wave in free
space.

Close the waveguide off entirely at both ends with conducting walls and it becomes a cavity
resonator, supporting only a discrete set of standing-wave resonant frequencies rather than a
continuous propagating spectrum — the same relationship a fully open string (any frequency
propagates) has to a string clamped at both ends (only discrete standing waves survive). This
is the operating principle behind microwave cavities in particle accelerators and klystrons,
and behind why a microwave oven's cavity, and not just its magnetron, has to be built to the
right dimensions to sustain the frequency it's designed to use.
