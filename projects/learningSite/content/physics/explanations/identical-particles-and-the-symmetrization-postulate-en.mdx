Two electrons, or two photons of the same energy and polarization, are not merely similar —
quantum mechanically, they are genuinely indistinguishable: there is no experiment, even in
principle, that can track "which electron is which" through an interaction. Since swapping
the labels of two truly identical particles can't change any measurable prediction, the
total probability density (the wavefunction's absolute value squared) must stay exactly the
same under that swap.

That requirement alone still allows the wavefunction itself to either stay exactly the same
(symmetric) or flip sign (antisymmetric) under the exchange, since both leave the squared
magnitude unchanged — and it's an experimental fact, not something derivable from this
argument alone, that every particle species falls into exactly one of these two camps and
stays there: bosons (integer spin — photons, gluons, helium-4 atoms) are always symmetric
under exchange, fermions (half-integer spin — electrons, protons, quarks) are always
antisymmetric, and this spin-statistics connection itself is a deep result of relativistic
quantum field theory.

The antisymmetry requirement for fermions is exactly what produces the Pauli exclusion
principle: if two fermions occupied literally the same single-particle quantum state,
swapping their labels would leave the wavefunction completely unchanged, which is only
consistent with antisymmetry if that wavefunction is identically zero — so no two fermions
can ever share a state. Bosons carry no such restriction, which is why an arbitrarily large
number of them can pile into the exact same single-particle state, the phenomenon behind
both the laser (many photons in one mode) and Bose-Einstein condensation.
