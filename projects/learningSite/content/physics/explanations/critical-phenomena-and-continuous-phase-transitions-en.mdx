Boiling and melting are first-order transitions: the two phases briefly coexist, and turning liquid into gas costs a fixed amount of latent heat even though the temperature doesn't change while it happens. A continuous transition looks nothing like that. A ferromagnet losing its magnetisation as it warms through the Curie point, or helium becoming a superfluid as it cools, both slide smoothly from one phase into the other — there's no latent heat and no two phases coexisting, just an order parameter (magnetisation, in the ferromagnet's case) shrinking continuously to zero.

What makes the critical point interesting isn't the order parameter itself but how sharply the system responds to a small nudge right at that temperature. The heat capacity, the magnetic susceptibility, the correlation length — all of them diverge there, and they do it following power laws: order parameter ~ (Tc − T)^β below Tc, susceptibility ~ |T − Tc|^−γ on either side. β, γ and the other critical exponents are measurable numbers, not free parameters you fit for convenience.

The remarkable part is universality: a fluid at its liquid-gas critical point and a magnet at its Curie point can have the *same* critical exponents, even though one is about density and the other about spin alignment. What decides the exponents turns out to be only the dimensionality of space and the symmetry of the order parameter — everything about the specific atoms or forces involved washes out near the critical point. That's why critical phenomena get studied as their own subject, independent of which material happens to be transitioning.
