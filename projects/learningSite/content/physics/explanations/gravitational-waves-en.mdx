Linearizing the Einstein field equations around flat spacetime — treating a small
perturbation to an otherwise flat background as a genuine physical wave — shows that
disturbances in spacetime curvature propagate outward at exactly the speed of light,
obeying a wave equation directly analogous to the one governing electromagnetic waves.
Unlike electromagnetic radiation, which requires an accelerating charge (and, because charge
is always monopole-symmetric or better, has no monopole radiation), gravitational radiation
requires a time-varying mass quadrupole moment — a perfectly spherically symmetric or purely
rotating mass distribution radiates nothing at all; the source needs to be genuinely,
changingly asymmetric, such as two massive objects orbiting each other.

A passing gravitational wave stretches space in one transverse direction while squeezing it
in the perpendicular transverse direction, then reverses, oscillating as the wave passes —
an effect that is extraordinarily small for any realistic astrophysical source observed from
Earth, changing the length of a kilometre-scale detector by a small fraction of a proton's
diameter, which is exactly why gravitational waves, though predicted in 1916, took a full
century of technological advancement to detect directly.

LIGO's first direct detection in September 2015, of the characteristic "chirp" signal from
two merging black holes roughly 1.3 billion light-years away, confirmed the existence of
gravitational waves exactly as general relativity predicted and opened an entirely new
observational channel for astronomy — one that doesn't rely on light or any other
electromagnetic radiation at all, and can directly probe events like black hole mergers that
emit essentially no light whatsoever, complementing rather than replacing every other form
of astronomical observation.
