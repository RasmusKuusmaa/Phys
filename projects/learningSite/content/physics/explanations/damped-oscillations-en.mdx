An idealised mass on a spring oscillates forever, but no real oscillator does,
because friction, air resistance or some other drag continuously drains
energy from the motion. That loss shows up as a shrinking amplitude: the
oscillation keeps its rhythm while each swing reaches a little less far than
the one before, tracing an envelope that decays exponentially in time rather
than losing a fixed amount every cycle. Because energy is proportional to
amplitude squared, a decaying amplitude means the system's total energy is
also draining away smoothly, exactly as it should for a dissipative process.

How much damping is present changes the character of the motion, not just its
speed of decay, and three distinct regimes result. With light damping, the
system is *underdamped*: it swings back and forth many times, each swing
smaller than the last, ringing down gradually — a plucked guitar string or a
lightly damped pendulum. Push the damping high enough and the system becomes
*overdamped*: it creeps back toward equilibrium slowly with no oscillation at
all, like a door closer set too strong, sluggishly returning the door rather
than swinging it shut. Between these sits *critical damping*, the precise
threshold that returns the system to equilibrium in the shortest possible time
with no overshoot and no oscillation — which is exactly why car suspensions
and analytical balances are engineered to sit as close to critical damping as
possible.

The common intuition that "more damping means faster return" holds only up to
critical damping and then reverses: an overdamped system with very strong
damping is actually *sluggish*, taking longer to settle than the critically
damped case, because the same drag that kills oscillation also directly
resists the return motion itself.
