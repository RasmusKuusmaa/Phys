More than half of all stars are members of binary or higher-order multiple systems, gravitationally bound to at least one companion. As long as both stars remain comfortably within their own Roche lobes -- the teardrop-shaped region surrounding each star within which gas is gravitationally bound to that star rather than to its companion -- each star evolves essentially as an isolated star of its own mass would. The Roche lobes of the two stars meet at the inner Lagrange point, the saddle point in the combined gravitational-plus-centrifugal potential where the pull toward each star exactly balances.

Stars evolve off the main sequence and expand as they age, and the more massive star in a binary, having burned through its core hydrogen fastest, expands into a giant first. If that expansion is enough to fill and then overflow its Roche lobe, gas spills through the inner Lagrange point onto the companion. This mass transfer can proceed steadily or violently depending on the details, and it carries away not just mass but often angular momentum, reshaping the orbit itself as it happens. Crucially, this can transfer enough of the donor star's envelope onto the companion that the two stars' relative masses reverse: the original donor, having lost most of its outer layers, can end up as the less massive star in the system, while the companion, having gained that material, becomes the more massive one.

This mass-reversal is the resolution to the historical "Algol paradox," named for the eclipsing binary Algol, in which the less massive star observed today is, paradoxically, the more evolved subgiant -- exactly backwards from what single-star evolution alone would predict, until mass transfer is taken into account. Beyond Algol-type systems, binary mass transfer onto a compact companion (a white dwarf, neutron star, or black hole) powers X-ray binaries and cataclysmic variables, and mass transfer onto a white dwarf that pushes it toward the Chandrasekhar limit is one of the two leading progenitor channels proposed for type Ia supernovae -- making binary interaction, not solitary stellar evolution, the actual origin story behind many of astrophysics' most energetic and observationally important phenomena.
