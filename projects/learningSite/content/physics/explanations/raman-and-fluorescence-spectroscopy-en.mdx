Shine a laser on almost any sample and the overwhelming majority of the light that bounces back comes back completely unchanged in wavelength — ordinary Rayleigh scattering, the same elastic process that colours the sky. But look closely at the light that comes back, and a tiny fraction of it — often less than one photon in a million — arrives at wavelengths shifted slightly away from the laser's own. That shifted light is Raman scattering, and it happens because a small number of photons don't just bounce off the molecule unchanged; they briefly interact with one of its vibrational modes and either give up or pick up exactly the amount of energy that mode requires to be excited or de-excited, coming out shifted by precisely that much in wavelength.

Because each vibrational mode has its own characteristic energy, and a molecule typically has several modes (bond stretches, bends, twists), the Raman-shifted light doesn't come out at just one shifted wavelength — it comes out as several distinct shifted lines, each one a signature of a specific vibration. Measuring the pattern of shifts is effectively reading off which vibrations the molecule has, which is why Raman spectroscopy is used to identify materials and molecular structure without touching or destroying the sample at all.

Fluorescence looks superficially similar — light goes in at one wavelength, comes out at another — but the mechanism is entirely different, and the two are easy to confuse if you only look at the wavelength shift. In fluorescence, the photon isn't scattered at all; it's absorbed completely, promoting an electron to a real excited energy level. That electron then loses some of its energy through fast, non-radiative processes (collisions, vibrational relaxation) before eventually dropping back down and emitting a new photon — necessarily at lower energy, and therefore longer wavelength, than the one absorbed. That shift toward longer wavelengths is called the Stokes shift, and unlike Raman scattering, which happens almost instantaneously, fluorescence typically takes nanoseconds — an eternity by comparison — because the electron spends real time sitting in the excited state before it drops back down.
