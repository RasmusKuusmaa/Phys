Stand in a windowless, accelerating rocket in deep space and every experiment you can
perform gives exactly the same result as standing at rest on the surface of a planet with
the matching gravitational field — objects you drop accelerate "downward" relative to you at
the same rate, and there is no local experiment that can tell the two situations apart. This
is the equivalence principle: locally, acceleration and gravity are physically
indistinguishable, which is a very different, much stronger statement than simply saying
gravitational and inertial mass happen to be numerically equal.

Taken seriously, the equivalence principle undermines the very idea of gravity as a force
acting between masses: if being in a gravitational field is locally the same as
accelerating, then a freely falling observer — one experiencing gravity alone, with nothing
else pushing on them — should feel exactly as if no force acts on them at all, the same way
an astronaut in orbit feels weightless despite Earth's gravity clearly acting on the
spacecraft. General relativity takes this at face value: a freely falling object isn't being
pushed or pulled by anything; it's simply moving along the straightest possible path
available to it.

That straightest possible path through curved spacetime is called a geodesic — the direct
generalization of a straight line in flat space to a curved geometry, found from the metric
tensor by minimizing (or more precisely, extremizing) the path's total spacetime interval.
What looks like a gravitational "force" bending an object's path in the older Newtonian
picture is, in general relativity, simply the object moving along a geodesic that happens to
look curved when viewed against a flat background — the object itself is doing the simplest
possible thing, moving as "straight" as the curved geometry it's embedded in allows.
