Classically, a particle with energy less than a barrier's height simply
cannot enter the barrier region at all — it reflects with certainty. Solve
the Schrödinger equation for the same situation and a different picture
emerges. Inside the classically forbidden region, the wavefunction does not
suddenly vanish; it satisfies an equation whose solution is a decaying
exponential, not zero. The wavefunction, and the probability of finding the
particle, both fall off smoothly and continuously with depth into the
barrier rather than stopping dead at the boundary. This same decaying-tail
behaviour is the mechanism behind quantum tunneling: if the barrier is thin
enough that the exponential tail has not yet decayed to nothing by the far
side, there is a nonzero probability that the particle is found beyond the
barrier, on the classically inaccessible side.

A finite-depth potential well — a region of low potential energy bounded on
both sides by finite (not infinite) barriers — shows a related contrast with
the idealized infinite well. An infinitely deep well forces the wavefunction
to be exactly zero at its walls, producing an infinite ladder of allowed
energy levels, evenly related by the same boundary condition at every level.
A finite well relaxes that boundary condition: the wavefunction is allowed to
leak into the walls as a decaying exponential, similar to the tunneling case,
which changes the allowed energies and — crucially — limits how many bound
states can exist at all. Above a certain energy set by the well's depth, a
particle is no longer bound: it is a scattering state, free to leave the well
region entirely, and the discrete ladder of bound levels simply ends. A
shallow or narrow enough well may support only a single bound state, or even
none.

The general lesson is that quantum boundaries are never perfectly sharp: the
allowed wavefunction always extends somewhat beyond a real physical barrier,
and how far it extends, and how many discrete bound states a given well can
hold, both depend on the barrier's finite height and width — not just on its
existence.
