A system held only slightly away from equilibrium relaxes back through linear-response transport: each thermodynamic flux -- a heat current, an electric current, a diffusive particle flow -- is proportional, to good approximation, to the thermodynamic forces present (temperature gradients, voltage gradients, concentration gradients). The simplest picture pairs each flux with its own single matching force: Fourier's law relates heat current to a temperature gradient, Ohm's law relates electric current to a voltage gradient, Fick's law relates particle diffusion to a concentration gradient. But real materials generally show genuine cross-coupling as well: a temperature gradient in a conducting material can itself drive an electric current (the Seebeck effect, the basis of thermocouples), and conversely, driving an electric current through a junction between two different materials can itself pump heat (the Peltier effect, used in solid-state refrigeration). These are not separate, unrelated phenomena but two faces of the same underlying cross-coupling between the heat flux and the electric flux.

In 1931, Lars Onsager proved a strikingly general constraint on exactly this kind of cross-coupling. Writing the linear relationship between all the fluxes present and all the forces present as a matrix of transport coefficients, the diagonal entries relate each flux to its own natural force, while the off-diagonal entries capture the cross-coupling terms (such as the Seebeck and Peltier coefficients). Onsager showed that this matrix of cross-coupling coefficients must be exactly symmetric -- the coefficient describing how force number two drives flux number one must equal the coefficient describing how force number one drives flux number two. This symmetry is not a mathematical given: an arbitrary matrix of linear-response coefficients has no reason to be symmetric at all, and demonstrating that real transport coefficients genuinely satisfy this constraint (the measured Seebeck and Peltier coefficients in a given material are indeed related exactly as the symmetry predicts) was a substantial, non-trivial result.

Crucially, Onsager's proof rested on nothing more exotic than the time-reversal symmetry of the underlying microscopic equations of motion, combined with the principle of detailed balance describing how a system near equilibrium fluctuates and relaxes. The reciprocal relations are therefore a genuine bridge between microscopic reversibility and macroscopic, dissipative transport behavior, in much the same spirit as the fluctuation-dissipation theorem connects microscopic fluctuations to macroscopic dissipation. This work, recognized with the 1968 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, now forms the foundational symmetry principle underlying the entire framework of linear irreversible (non-equilibrium) thermodynamics, constraining the transport coefficients of any system displaced only slightly from equilibrium.
