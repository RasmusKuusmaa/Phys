Most constraints you meet early in Lagrangian mechanics are the easy kind:
a bead stuck on a wire, a pendulum bob at a fixed distance from its pivot.
These can be written as a plain relation between coordinates — the bead's
height is a function of how far along the wire it's travelled — so you can
just substitute that relation in and end up with one fewer coordinate to
solve for. This kind of constraint is called holonomic, and it disappears
cleanly.

A rolling wheel is different. Rolling without slipping says the contact
point's velocity is zero, which relates the wheel's linear velocity to its
angular velocity. That's a relation between velocities, and in general it
cannot be integrated back into a single relation between the position
coordinates alone — there's no function of position whose derivative gives
you that condition for every possible path the wheel might take. A
constraint like this is nonholonomic, and it can't be used to quietly
eliminate a coordinate.

The fix is to keep the constraint explicit and add it to the problem with an
extra unknown: a Lagrange multiplier, one per constraint. You end up with
the ordinary Euler–Lagrange equations plus a multiplier term, plus the
constraint equation itself, and you solve them all together. The reward for
this extra bookkeeping is not just correctness — the multiplier you solve
for turns out to equal the actual constraint force (the friction force
enforcing the rolling condition, say) up to a geometric factor you can read
off directly. Ordinary unconstrained Lagrangian mechanics throws constraint
forces away as irrelevant; this method hands them back.

The traps are procedural. Plugging the constraint in early, the way you
would for a holonomic one, gives equations of motion that are simply wrong
for a nonholonomic constraint — the order of operations (differentiate
first, constrain after, via the multiplier) is not optional here the way it
is for the holonomic case.
