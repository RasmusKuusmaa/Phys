Conduction electrons in a metal obey the Pauli exclusion principle, so at absolute zero they
don't all sit in the lowest-energy state — they fill up every available momentum state one
by one, from lowest energy to highest, until all the electrons are accommodated. The
boundary of this filled region in momentum space, separating occupied from unoccupied
states, is the Fermi surface; for a simple free-electron metal it's a sphere (the Fermi
sphere), but for a real metal, shaped by the actual periodic lattice potential, it can be a
complex, faceted shape reflecting the material's specific band structure and crystal
symmetry.

Deep inside the filled Fermi sea, every nearby momentum state is already occupied, so the
Pauli exclusion principle forbids an electron there from being scattered into a neighbouring
state at all — there's nowhere for it to go. Only electrons with momenta right at the Fermi
surface have empty states immediately available nearby, so only they can actually be
scattered by an impurity, a phonon, or an applied field and contribute to a current; deep
electrons are effectively frozen out of any transport process, however energetic they might
individually be.

This is why the Fermi surface, not the full electron distribution, is what actually
determines nearly all of a metal's transport and many other properties: electrical and
thermal conductivity, the response to a magnetic field (which bends the Fermi surface's
electrons along cyclotron orbits, measurable via quantum oscillation experiments that map
the Fermi surface's actual shape directly), and even whether a material behaves as a metal,
semimetal, or insulator in the first place, all trace back to the geometry of this one
surface in momentum space — its shape is arguably the single most important piece of
information about a metal's electronic structure.
