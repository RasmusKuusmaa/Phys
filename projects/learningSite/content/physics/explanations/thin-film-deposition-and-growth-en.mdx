Almost every optical coating, hard coating, solar cell and integrated circuit is
a stack of layers between a few nanometres and a few micrometres thick, and they
are all made by delivering material to a surface one atom at a time. The two
families differ in how the material is delivered. Physical vapour deposition
turns a solid source into vapour — heating it until it evaporates, or knocking
atoms off it with energetic ions in sputtering — and lets the vapour travel
through vacuum to the substrate. Chemical vapour deposition instead flows a
reactive gas over a hot substrate, where it decomposes and leaves the solid
behind while the by-products are pumped away.

The vacuum is not just about keeping contamination out. In physical deposition
the arriving atoms have to reach the substrate without colliding on the way, so
the mean free path must be longer than the source-to-substrate distance; at
higher pressures the flux arrives from every direction rather than in a line,
which changes how the film covers steps and how dense it turns out. Chemical
deposition is the opposite case: it *relies* on gas-phase transport, so it runs
at pressures where evaporation would be scattered into uselessness, and it coats
complicated shapes evenly for exactly that reason.

What decides the film's character is what happens after an atom lands. It
diffuses across the surface until it finds a step, a defect or another atom to
bond to, and the competition between how strongly it binds to the substrate and
how strongly to its own kind sets the growth mode: layer-by-layer wetting when
the substrate wins, isolated islands that only later merge when its own kind
wins, and layers first then islands in the intermediate case. That is why an
extremely thin metal film can be optically visible but electrically
discontinuous. If the substrate is crystalline, the lattice constants match
closely and the atoms are mobile enough to find lattice sites before being
buried, the film grows as a single crystal continuous with the substrate —
epitaxy — and any residual mismatch is stored as elastic strain until it relaxes
into dislocations at a critical thickness.
