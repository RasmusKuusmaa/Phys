The Einstein field equations relate spacetime curvature to the distribution of matter and
energy for any spacetime whatsoever, but solving them in full generality is extraordinarily
difficult. Cosmology makes the problem tractable by assuming, on sufficiently large scales, that
the universe is homogeneous (the same average density everywhere) and isotropic (looking the
same in every direction) — the cosmological principle, well supported observationally once
averaged over scales large enough to smooth out individual galaxies and clusters. A spacetime
with exactly this symmetry is described by the Friedmann-Lemaitre-Robertson-Walker (FLRW)
metric, built from a single time-dependent function, the scale factor, describing how the
overall separation between any two comoving points grows (or shrinks) over cosmic time, together
with a constant describing the universe's overall spatial curvature (flat, positively curved, or
negatively curved).

Substituting the FLRW metric into the Einstein field equations and simplifying using the
symmetry assumptions collapses the full set of tensor equations down to two comparatively
simple ordinary differential equations for the scale factor: the Friedmann equations. The first
relates the expansion rate (the scale factor's rate of change relative to its own value, which
is exactly the Hubble parameter) to the universe's total energy density and its spatial
curvature; the second relates the expansion's acceleration to the total energy density and
pressure of everything filling the universe. These are not separate cosmological postulates —
they are a direct, purely mathematical consequence of applying general relativity itself to a
homogeneous, isotropic spacetime, turning cosmology into calculable predictions from a theory
originally developed to explain much smaller-scale phenomena like planetary orbits and light
bending near the sun.

The second Friedmann equation reveals something the naive Newtonian intuition of universally
attractive gravity misses: the expansion's acceleration depends not just on energy density (which
is always positive and always decelerating) but also on pressure, and a component with
sufficiently negative pressure — most notably a cosmological constant, or dark energy more
generally — contributes a genuinely repulsive term, actively driving the expansion to accelerate
rather than merely slowing its deceleration. Ordinary matter and radiation, with positive
pressure or none, decelerate the expansion exactly as intuition suggests; it is the presence of a
dark-energy-like component with negative pressure that explains the accelerating expansion
directly observed in distant supernova surveys, one of the most striking confirmations that the
Friedmann equations, and the general relativity underlying them, correctly describe the
universe's actual large-scale dynamics.
