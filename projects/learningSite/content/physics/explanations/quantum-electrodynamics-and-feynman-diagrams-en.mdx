In QED, two charged particles don't interact via an instantaneous force reaching across
space; they interact by exchanging virtual photons — photons that briefly violate the
ordinary energy-momentum relation in a way genuine, observable photons never do, existing
only fleetingly as an intermediate step in the calculation, never directly detected. A
Feynman diagram is a precise picture of one specific way this exchange can happen: straight
lines for charged particles (electrons, say), wavy lines for photons, and vertices where
lines meet representing a specific interaction.

Each diagram isn't just an illustration — it corresponds to a specific mathematical
expression via a fixed set of rules (Feynman rules), and the full quantum-mechanical
amplitude for a process is the sum over every diagram that could contribute, including
arbitrarily complicated ones with more internal photon exchanges, particle-antiparticle
loops, and so on. Because each additional vertex in a diagram brings down a factor of the
electromagnetic coupling constant (a small number, roughly 1/137), diagrams with more
vertices contribute progressively smaller corrections, and truncating the sum after a
manageable number of increasingly complex diagrams still gives extraordinarily accurate
predictions.

This perturbative approach, done carefully (accounting for and systematically removing
certain infinities that appear in the naive calculation, a procedure called
renormalization), makes QED the most precisely verified theory in the history of physics:
its prediction for the electron's magnetic moment agrees with experiment to better than one
part in a trillion, an agreement unmatched by any other physical theory. The same
diagrammatic technique, with different particles and vertices, extends to the weak and
strong interactions, making Feynman diagrams the standard calculational and conceptual
language across essentially all of particle physics, not just QED.
