Placing a two-dimensional electron gas in a strong perpendicular magnetic field collapses its
allowed energy states into discrete, highly degenerate Landau levels. At sufficiently low
temperature and high field, the Hall conductance measured across such a system, rather than
rising smoothly as the field or electron density changes, locks onto a sequence of exactly flat
plateaus, with the conductance at each plateau quantized to an integer multiple of a single
combination of fundamental constants (the elementary charge squared divided by Planck's
constant). This quantization is extraordinarily precise — reproducible to better than one part in
a billion across different samples, materials, and laboratories — which is exactly why it now
serves as the international metrological standard defining the practical realization of the
resistance unit.

Naively, sample disorder and impurities, always present in any real material, might be expected
to degrade this precision, the same way disorder generally degrades other electronic transport
measurements. In fact, disorder plays an essential, constructive role in producing the observed
flat plateaus in the first place: without any disorder, the Hall conductance would jump abruptly
between quantized values only at isolated, exact field strengths, producing no observable plateau
at all. Disorder broadens the sharp Landau levels and localizes many of the resulting electron
states (meaning these localized states don't contribute to conduction), so that as the field is
tuned across a range, the conductance remains pinned at the same quantized value until enough
extended (non-localized) states are filled to push it to the next plateau. The plateaus' exact
quantized values are protected by a topological invariant (the first Chern number, related to the
same broad family of topological ideas underlying topological insulators), a quantity that is
fundamentally insensitive to smooth changes in the material's specific disorder, geometry, or
composition, exactly why the effect is universal across different real, imperfect samples rather
than being a fragile idealization only achievable in a perfectly clean system.

The integer quantum Hall effect is well explained entirely within a picture of independent,
non-interacting electrons filling Landau levels one at a time, with the topological protection
arising already at the single-particle level. This is a genuinely different underlying mechanism
from the fractional quantum Hall effect, which occurs at even lower temperatures and higher
fields and requires strong electron-electron interactions and genuine many-body correlation that
the independent-electron picture used for the integer effect cannot produce at all — the two
effects share a family resemblance (both are Hall-conductance plateaus in a two-dimensional
electron gas) but rest on qualitatively distinct physics.
