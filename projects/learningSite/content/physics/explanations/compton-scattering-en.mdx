By the early twentieth century, the photoelectric effect had already shown
that light interacts with matter in discrete energy packets — photons — but
some physicists still hoped a purely classical wave picture might eventually
account for it. Compton scattering closed that door. When X-ray photons are
directed at a target containing loosely bound or effectively free electrons,
the scattered X-rays come back with a longer wavelength than the incoming
beam, and the size of that wavelength shift depends on the scattering angle
in a way that a classical wave picture cannot reproduce at all — classical
electromagnetic theory predicts the scattered radiation should keep the same
wavelength as the incident wave, just redistributed in direction.

The effect is explained cleanly by treating the interaction as a
particle-like collision between a photon and an electron, each carrying
momentum as well as energy. Conservation of both energy and momentum in this
photon-electron "collision" — using the photon's momentum p = h/λ, familiar
from the de Broglie relation and applied in reverse here — predicts exactly
the observed relationship between the wavelength shift and the scattering
angle: the photon loses some momentum and energy to the recoiling electron,
and by the same p = h/λ relation, a photon that has lost momentum must have
gained wavelength. The larger the scattering angle, the more momentum is
transferred and the larger the wavelength shift, up to a maximum at
backscattering.

Compton scattering was decisive evidence, alongside the photoelectric
effect, that light must be treated as discrete, momentum-carrying photons
in this regime, not merely a wave. It remains an important interaction
mechanism today: it is one of the main ways gamma rays and X-rays lose energy
when passing through matter, relevant to radiation shielding, medical
imaging, and astrophysical observations of high-energy radiation.
