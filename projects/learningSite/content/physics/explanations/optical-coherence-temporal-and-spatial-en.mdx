A sharp, high-contrast interference pattern only appears when the combined light waves keep a stable, predictable phase relationship over the relevant time delay and path difference; when that phase relationship instead drifts and randomizes, the bright and dark fringes wash out into a uniform average. Coherence is the property measuring how well that stable phase relationship is maintained, and it comes in two genuinely distinct varieties with different physical origins.

Temporal coherence measures how long a source's own phase stays predictable as time passes, and it is governed directly by the source's spectral bandwidth. A source emitting exactly one frequency has a phase that evolves perfectly predictably forever; a source emitting a broad range of frequencies simultaneously has each frequency component's phase advancing at a slightly different rate, so the combined phase relationship scrambles after only a short coherence time (equivalently, over only a short coherence length). This is why an ordinary incandescent bulb, radiating a broad continuous thermal spectrum, has extremely poor temporal coherence, while a stabilized single-frequency laser, with its extraordinarily narrow linewidth, can maintain temporal coherence over enormous path differences -- brightness and coherence are unrelated properties, since a source can be intense yet spectrally broad, or dim yet spectrally pure.

Spatial coherence is a separate matter entirely: it measures how well the phase stays correlated across different points on the source's own physical extent, rather than across time at one point. A genuinely point-like source has perfect spatial coherence by definition, since there is only one point for the phase to be defined at; a physically extended source is effectively many independent point emitters superposed, each contributing its own uncorrelated phase, and the resulting light has correspondingly degraded spatial coherence set by the source's angular size as seen from the observation point. This is precisely why classic interference demonstrations using ordinary extended sources (sunlight, an incandescent bulb) first restrict the light through a narrow slit or pinhole -- selecting light that effectively originates from a much smaller, more nearly point-like region restores usable spatial coherence over the smaller region actually being illuminated, even though the original source itself remains spatially incoherent overall. Lasers achieve both excellent temporal coherence (from stimulated emission's inherently narrow linewidth) and excellent spatial coherence (from the cavity mode structure selecting a single, well-defined spatial pattern) simultaneously, which is exactly why interference and diffraction experiments that work effortlessly with laser light require this kind of careful engineering to work at all with ordinary thermal light sources.
