Weigh a nucleus carefully and compare it with the sum of the masses of its
individual protons and neutrons measured separately, and the nucleus always
comes up slightly lighter. This missing mass — the mass defect — is not lost;
by mass-energy equivalence, E = mc², it has been converted into the energy
that holds the nucleus together, called the binding energy. Equivalently,
the binding energy is exactly the amount of energy that would need to be
supplied to pull the nucleus apart into its separate, free nucleons — a
tightly bound nucleus therefore has both a large binding energy and a
correspondingly large mass deficit relative to its separated parts.

Dividing binding energy by the number of nucleons gives binding energy per
nucleon, a measure of how tightly, on average, each nucleon is held. Plotted
against atomic mass number, this quantity rises steeply for the lightest
nuclei, peaks around iron and nickel (mass numbers near 56-62), and then
declines slowly for heavier nuclei. This single curve is the key to
understanding both major forms of nuclear energy release. Fusing light
nuclei together — hydrogen into helium, for instance — moves the product
further up the rising part of the curve, so the product nucleus has more
binding energy per nucleon than the reactants; the difference is released as
energy, which is why fusion powers stars. Splitting a heavy nucleus into two
medium-mass fragments moves both fragments up toward the peak from the
declining side of the curve, again increasing total binding energy per
nucleon and releasing the difference; this is why fission of heavy nuclei
such as uranium also releases energy. Nuclei already near the peak, such as
iron, release no net energy from either fusing further or splitting apart —
which is exactly why iron is the endpoint of energy-releasing fusion inside
stars, and why nuclei near the peak of the curve are typically the most
stable nuclei found in nature.
