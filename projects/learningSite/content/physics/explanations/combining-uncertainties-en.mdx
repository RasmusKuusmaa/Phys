When quantities with their own uncertainties get combined, the rule for
combining the uncertainties depends on what you did with the quantities.
Adding or subtracting two measured lengths adds their absolute
uncertainties directly: (10.0 ± 0.1) cm + (5.0 ± 0.1) cm gives 15.0 ± 0.2 cm.

Multiplying or dividing works differently — there, it's the relative (or
percentage) uncertainties that add. A length known to ±2% multiplied by a
length known to ±3% gives an area known to about ±5%, not ±2% or ±3%
individually.

The two rules are easy to mix up, and mixing them up is one of the most
common mistakes in this area — subtracting instead of adding uncertainties,
for example, would suggest a calculation somehow got more certain by
combining two measurements, which is never how it works. Uncertainties only
ever grow (or, at best, stay comparable) when quantities are combined;
they never shrink.
