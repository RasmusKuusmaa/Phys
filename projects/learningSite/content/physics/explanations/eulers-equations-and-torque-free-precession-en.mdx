Newton's second law for rotation, torque = dL/dt, is simplest to write in an inertial frame,
but for a spinning rigid body it's far easier to solve in the frame that rotates along with
the body — the one in which the inertia tensor is diagonal and constant, since the principal
axes are fixed in the body. Translating dL/dt into that rotating frame introduces an extra
term for the frame's own rotation, and the result is Euler's equations: three coupled
equations, one per principal axis, each relating the time derivative of one component of
angular velocity to the other two components and the corresponding torque.

With zero external torque, Euler's equations still couple the three components of omega to
each other through the differences between the principal moments of inertia — a body doesn't
just keep spinning about whatever axis it started on unless that axis is exactly one of the
three principal axes. Linearizing the equations around steady rotation about each principal
axis reveals which of the three is actually stable: rotation about the axis of largest or
smallest principal moment of inertia relaxes back toward steady spin after a small
perturbation, while rotation about the intermediate axis grows away from it.

This is the intermediate-axis theorem, and it is easy to demonstrate with an ordinary object:
a rectangular block (a tennis racket, a smartphone, a book) thrown spinning cleanly about its
longest or shortest axis keeps spinning cleanly, but thrown spinning about its middle-length
axis it tumbles unpredictably in flight, flipping through a half-turn before settling back —
the same instability Euler's equations predict for any asymmetric rigid body, not a fluke of
any particular object.
