Given a set of measured data points that are expected, according to theory,
to lie on a straight line, the practical problem is finding the specific
line — the specific slope and intercept — that best represents the data.
Drawing a line "by eye" is subjective: different people, given the same
scatter of points, will draw slightly different lines, and there is no way
to attach a meaningful uncertainty to a line chosen this way.

The least-squares method replaces this subjective judgement with an
objective mathematical criterion. For any candidate line, the vertical
distance (the residual) between each data point and the line can be
calculated; the least-squares criterion selects the specific slope and
intercept that minimise the sum of the squares of all these residuals.
Squaring the residuals before summing serves two purposes: it makes every
term positive, so that positive and negative deviations do not cancel out
and hide a poor fit, and it penalises large deviations more heavily than
small ones, so the fit is especially sensitive to points that lie far from
the general trend. This minimisation problem has a closed-form algebraic
solution for a straight line, so the best-fit slope and intercept can be
calculated directly from the data rather than found by trial and error.

Because the least-squares procedure is a well-defined calculation rather
than a subjective judgement, it produces the same result every time it is
applied to the same data, and — importantly — it also yields formulas for
the uncertainty in the fitted slope and intercept themselves, based on how
scattered the data points are around the best-fit line. A tight cluster of
points close to the fitted line gives small uncertainties in the fit
parameters; a wide scatter gives large ones. This is what allows an
experimenter to report not just "the slope is approximately such-and-such"
but a quantitative, defensible value with its own uncertainty, and to assess
quantitatively — via how well the fit actually matches the data — whether
the assumed linear relationship (often obtained via linearisation of a
nonlinear physical law) is actually supported by the measurements.
