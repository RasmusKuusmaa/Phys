Many nuclei carry nonzero intrinsic spin, and because they are charged, that spin gives rise
to a magnetic moment, in direct analogy with an electron's spin magnetic moment. Nuclei with
even numbers of both protons and neutrons, such as carbon-12 and oxygen-16, have zero total
spin because of the same pairing effect that boosts their binding energy (the nuclear shell
model's spin-pairing tendency), but many common nuclei do not: hydrogen-1 (a bare proton),
carbon-13, and nitrogen-15 all carry nonzero spin and a corresponding magnetic moment, making
them accessible to magnetic resonance techniques.

Placed in an external magnetic field, a spin's different orientations correspond to distinct
energy levels (Zeeman splitting), with the energy gap between adjacent levels proportional to
the field strength and the nucleus's magnetic moment. Nuclear magnetic resonance drives
transitions between these levels using radio-frequency electromagnetic radiation tuned to
exactly match that energy gap — resonance occurs only when the photon energy equals the level
splitting, producing a sharp, sensitive signal at a well-defined frequency for a given nucleus
and field strength.

Critically, that resonance frequency is not determined solely by the nucleus and the external
field: it is also slightly shifted by the local magnetic field generated by surrounding
electrons (which partially shield the nucleus from the external field, an effect that depends
on the electron density and bonding around that specific nucleus) and by nearby nuclear spins
(spin-spin coupling). This "chemical shift" is small but precisely measurable, and it means
the same type of nucleus (say, a hydrogen atom) produces a distinguishably different resonance
frequency depending on its specific chemical environment within a molecule — a hydrogen bonded
to oxygen resonates at a different frequency than one bonded to carbon. This sensitivity to
local chemical environment is what makes NMR spectroscopy one of the most powerful tools
available for determining molecular structure, rather than merely confirming which isotopes
are present.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a direct medical application of this same underlying
physics: it detects the resonance signal predominantly from hydrogen nuclei in water and fat
throughout the body, and by applying magnetic field gradients that vary smoothly across space,
encodes spatial position into the resonance frequency and phase of the detected signal,
allowing that signal to be reconstructed into a detailed image of the body's internal soft
tissue structure without using any ionizing radiation.
