A single hydrogen atom's Schrödinger equation is already hard enough; a molecule's is worse, because it couples the motion of every electron to the motion of every nucleus at once. The trick that makes it tractable is the Born-Oppenheimer approximation, and it rests on one fact: nuclei are thousands of times heavier than electrons, so electrons zip around and settle into their arrangement essentially instantly compared to any nuclear motion. That lets you solve the electronic problem separately for each fixed nuclear geometry, then stitch the results together into a potential energy surface — a landscape, over every possible bond length and angle, of how much energy the electrons contribute at that geometry.

The nuclei then move on that landscape according to ordinary quantum mechanics, but with a crucial twist: the motion is quantized. Vibration along a bond behaves like a quantum harmonic oscillator, restricted to an evenly spaced ladder of discrete energies; rotation of the whole molecule behaves like a quantum rigid rotor, with its own ladder of energy levels. Neither can take just any energy — which is exactly why a molecule's infrared and microwave spectra show sharp lines at specific frequencies rather than a smooth continuous glow.

This two-step structure — solve the electrons first for a fixed geometry, then quantize the nuclear motion on the resulting surface — is the conceptual backbone underneath essentially every molecular calculation in chemistry and physics, from working out a diatomic molecule's bond length by hand to running a full quantum-chemistry simulation on a computer.
