Classical mechanics has one central equation of motion, F = ma, that turns a
description of the forces acting on an object into a prediction of exactly
how its position changes over time. Quantum mechanics has an analogous
central equation, but for the wavefunction rather than a trajectory: the
Schrödinger equation, which takes the potential energy a particle experiences
and determines exactly how its wavefunction evolves in time. The two
equations play parallel structural roles — each is the engine that turns
"here are the forces" into "here is how the state changes" — but what they
each act on is fundamentally different: a definite position and velocity in
one case, a probability-carrying wavefunction in the other.

Solving the equation for a particular physical situation — a particle
confined to a box, an electron bound to a proton, a mass on a spring —
typically does not yield one wavefunction with one energy, but a whole family
of solutions, each pairing an allowed wavefunction with its own energy value.
This is not the equation malfunctioning; it is exactly how quantisation
emerges within this framework. The particle-in-a-box's discrete energy ladder
and the hydrogen atom's discrete spectral lines are both directly consequences
of the Schrödinger equation admitting only certain wavefunctions for a given
confining potential, precisely the same way a guitar string clamped at both
ends admits only certain standing-wave patterns.

Time-independent versions of the equation, used for situations where the
system's energy does not change, are what produce these discrete energy
levels directly; the full time-dependent equation is needed whenever the
wavefunction itself changes shape over time — a wave packet spreading,
interfering, or tunnelling through a barrier. Both versions apply to any
non-relativistic quantum system once its potential energy is specified, not
merely to hydrogen or any other single example; the equation is general, and
each different physical situation is simply a different choice of potential
fed into the same governing structure.
