For roughly the first few minutes after the Big Bang, the universe as a whole was hot and
dense enough (billions of degrees, comparable to the centre of a star) for nuclear fusion to
proceed everywhere in space simultaneously, starting from free protons and neutrons and
building up light nuclei — deuterium, helium-3, helium-4, and a small amount of lithium-7.
This window closed quickly: as the universe expanded, both its temperature and its density
dropped rapidly, and within about twenty minutes the conditions needed for fusion no longer
existed anywhere, freezing the light-element abundances in place.

The predicted primordial abundances depend essentially on just one free parameter — the
universe's baryon-to-photon ratio, how many protons and neutrons exist for every photon —
making Big Bang nucleosynthesis an unusually sharp, precisely testable prediction: measure
that one ratio (independently determined from the cosmic microwave background's detailed
structure) and the theory predicts specific abundances for hydrogen, helium and lithium that
can be directly checked against what's actually observed in the oldest, most pristine gas
clouds and stars available, ones least contaminated by nuclear processing inside stars since
then.

The predicted and observed abundances of hydrogen and helium agree to remarkable precision,
one of the most successful and precise quantitative tests the entire Big Bang model has
passed — roughly three-quarters hydrogen and one-quarter helium by mass, matching
observation closely, is a result stellar nucleosynthesis alone could never produce, since
stars have only converted a small fraction of the universe's hydrogen into helium over its
entire history. The lithium-7 abundance is the one persistent exception: the predicted
amount is measurably higher than what's actually observed in old stars, the "lithium
problem," a genuine unresolved discrepancy that remains an active area of research rather
than a settled result.
