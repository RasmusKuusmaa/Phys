Two or more quantum particles are entangled when the combined system's quantum state cannot be
written as a simple product of independent states for each individual particle — the particles'
properties are correlated in a way that has no classical analogue. A commonly used example is a
pair of particles prepared so that measuring one particle's spin along some axis always yields a
result perfectly correlated (or anticorrelated) with a measurement of the other particle's spin
along the same axis, however far apart the two particles are separated when the measurements
occur.

Before Bell's theorem, it seemed plausible that this correlation could be explained the same way
an everyday, classical correlation would be: perhaps each particle carries some definite, if
unknown, "hidden variable" determining the measurement outcome in advance, the same way two
gloves shipped to different cities are each definitely left- or right-handed the whole time, even
before either box is opened — no faster-than-light influence would be needed, since the
correlation would simply reflect a shared, predetermined property. John Bell showed
mathematically in 1964 that this intuitive picture cannot be right if it is required to be both
"local" (no influence propagates faster than light) and "realist" (particles possess definite
values for their properties independent of measurement): any theory satisfying both assumptions
together must obey a specific statistical inequality relating the correlations measured along
different combinations of axes. Quantum mechanics predicts correlations that violate this
inequality, and repeated, increasingly rigorous experiments (closing various experimental
loopholes over decades) have confirmed violations matching quantum mechanics' predictions to high
precision, actively ruling out the entire class of local hidden-variable theories rather than
merely lacking evidence for them.

Despite instantaneously affecting what outcomes become correlated at the other particle's
location, entanglement cannot be used to send information faster than light. The specific outcome
obtained when measuring the first particle is genuinely random from the experimenter's
perspective — there is no way to control or bias which result appears — so nothing meaningful can
be communicated by the act of measurement alone. Recognizing that a correlation exists at all
still requires comparing the two sets of measurement results afterward, which necessarily
requires an ordinary classical communication channel limited by the speed of light, which is
exactly why entanglement, despite its genuinely non-classical correlations, does not violate
special relativity's prohibition on faster-than-light signaling.
