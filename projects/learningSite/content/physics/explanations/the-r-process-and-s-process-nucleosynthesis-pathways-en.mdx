Nuclear fusion releases energy only up to the binding-energy-per-nucleon peak around iron; fusing
elements together beyond that point costs energy rather than releasing it, so ordinary stellar
fusion cannot be responsible for building elements substantially heavier than iron. Instead,
essentially all such elements are built through neutron capture: a nucleus absorbs a free neutron
without needing to overcome any Coulomb repulsion (since neutrons are uncharged), becoming a
heavier isotope of the same element, which may then undergo beta decay, converting a neutron into
a proton and moving the nucleus to the next element up.

The s-process (slow neutron capture) occurs where the ambient neutron flux is low enough that the
typical time between successive neutron captures on a given nucleus is longer than the timescale
for beta decay. Consequently, whenever a neutron capture produces an unstable, neutron-rich
isotope, that nucleus generally has time to beta-decay back toward a stable configuration before
capturing another neutron, so the s-process pathway hugs the valley of nuclear stability closely,
building heavy elements gradually within evolved, lower-mass stars over thousands of years, one
capture and decay at a time.

The r-process (rapid neutron capture) requires an extraordinarily higher neutron flux, so high
that successive neutron captures occur far faster than beta decay can respond, driving nuclei
deep into extremely neutron-rich, highly unstable territory well away from the valley of
stability — piling up many extra neutrons onto a seed nucleus before it has any chance to beta
decay at all. Only once the neutron flux drops (as the environment producing it dilutes or
subsides) do these extremely neutron-rich nuclei finally beta-decay in a cascade back toward
stability, producing a characteristically different set of stable heavy isotopes than the
s-process reaches, including essentially all of the very heaviest naturally occurring elements.
Because it requires such an extreme, transient neutron flux, the r-process cannot occur gradually
inside an ordinary star; it requires explosive, neutron-dense astrophysical environments, most
directly confirmed for neutron star mergers through the 2017 multi-messenger observation linking
a gravitational-wave detected merger to the characteristic electromagnetic afterglow (a
"kilonova") produced by r-process nucleosynthesis and the subsequent radioactive decay of its
freshly synthesized heavy elements. Together, the s-process and r-process are understood to
account for essentially the entire observed abundance of elements heavier than iron, with each
process responsible for a distinct, only partially overlapping subset of the heavy isotopes
found in nature.
