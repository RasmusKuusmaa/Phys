In ordinary flat-space physics, distance is given by the Pythagorean theorem, and Cartesian
coordinates make every calculation simple — but curved spacetime has no global Cartesian
coordinate system at all, so a theory of gravity built on curved geometry needs mathematical
objects whose physical meaning doesn't depend on which coordinates happen to be used to
describe them. A tensor is precisely such an object: a quantity with well-defined
transformation rules under any change of coordinates, guaranteeing that an equation written
entirely in terms of tensors, if true in one coordinate system, is automatically true in
every coordinate system.

The metric tensor g is the central tensor of general relativity: it generalizes the
Pythagorean theorem to curved space, giving the infinitesimal squared distance between two
nearby points as a sum over all pairs of coordinate directions, weighted by the metric's
components at that point. In flat spacetime described in ordinary coordinates, the metric
reduces to the simple, constant Minkowski form from special relativity; in curved spacetime,
or even in flat spacetime described in curved coordinates (like polar coordinates), the
metric's components vary from point to point, and it's precisely this coordinate-dependent
variation that tensor calculus is built to handle correctly.

Everything general relativity needs — the length of a path, the angle between two
directions, whether a given coordinate change represents genuine curvature or merely an
artifact of an awkward coordinate choice, and ultimately the curvature itself — is built up
systematically from the metric tensor and its derivatives using the rules of tensor
calculus. This is why general relativity is often summarized as "geometry is gravity":
specifying the metric tensor for a given spacetime is mathematically equivalent to
specifying its entire gravitational field.
