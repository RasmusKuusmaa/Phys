When a two-dimensional electron gas is placed in a strong perpendicular magnetic field, the
electrons' allowed energy states collapse into discrete, highly degenerate Landau levels, and
the integer quantum Hall effect — the Hall conductance rising in sharp, exactly integer
multiples of a fundamental unit as the field or electron density is tuned — emerges from this
picture straightforwardly, treating electrons as independent particles simply filling these
Landau levels one at a time. At sufficiently low temperatures and strong fields, however, an
additional set of plateaus appears at specific fractional multiples of that same unit — the
fractional quantum Hall effect — and no amount of refining the independent-electron picture can
produce it: it requires electron-electron interactions to be treated as essential rather than a
small correction, since the fractional plateaus arise from the electrons settling collectively
into a strongly correlated many-body quantum state, a qualitatively different phenomenon from
the integer effect rather than a smaller version of the same physics.

The low-energy excitations of this correlated state behave as well-defined quasiparticles, and
strikingly, they carry an electric charge that is a fraction of a single electron's charge
(commonly e/3 for the most prominent fractional plateau). This does not mean any individual
electron has physically split into pieces — the electron remains a genuinely indivisible
elementary particle — but rather that the emergent excitations of the whole correlated
many-body system behave, for practical purposes, as particles with that fractional charge, an
example of emergent behaviour in condensed matter that has no single-particle counterpart.

Even more strikingly, these quasiparticles obey exchange statistics that are neither fermionic
nor bosonic. In three-dimensional space, exchanging two identical particles twice is
topologically equivalent to not exchanging them at all (any path swapping them twice can be
continuously deformed away), which restricts the exchange phase to exactly two values,
producing the familiar bosonic (symmetric) and fermionic (antisymmetric) wavefunctions.
Confined to exactly two spatial dimensions, however, this topological argument fails: swapping
two particles once is genuinely, topologically distinct from swapping them twice, since a path
winding one particle around another in a plane cannot be continuously shrunk away without
leaving the plane, allowing an exchange phase anywhere between the bosonic and fermionic
extremes (abelian anyons), or, for certain fractional quantum Hall states, an even richer
non-abelian exchange behaviour where the order of exchanges genuinely matters. Anyonic
statistics exist only because of this two-dimensional confinement — there is no analogous
possibility in ordinary three-dimensional space — and non-abelian anyons in particular are
actively studied as a potential physical basis for topologically protected quantum computation.
