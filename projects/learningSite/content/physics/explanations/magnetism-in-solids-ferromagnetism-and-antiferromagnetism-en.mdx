The strong, persistent magnetism of a material like iron cannot be explained by ordinary classical magnetic dipole-dipole interactions between neighbouring atomic moments -- calculated directly, those interactions are far too weak, by several orders of magnitude, to survive thermal agitation at anywhere near room temperature. The real driving force is a purely quantum-mechanical effect called the exchange interaction, which has no classical analogue whatsoever. It arises from the interplay of two ingredients: the Pauli exclusion principle, which forbids two electrons from occupying the same quantum state, and the ordinary Coulomb repulsion between electrons. Together, these mean that two electrons with parallel spins (forced by Pauli exclusion into different spatial orbitals, keeping them further apart on average) experience less Coulomb repulsion than two electrons with antiparallel spins (which can occupy the same spatial orbital, on average sitting closer together) -- so the total energy of a pair of electrons genuinely depends on their relative spin orientation, even though the interaction driving that dependence is purely electrostatic.

Whether this exchange interaction favours parallel or antiparallel neighbouring spins depends on the specific sign it takes for a given material's electronic structure, and this sign, not merely the interaction's strength, decides what kind of magnetic order results. A positive exchange constant favours neighbouring spins aligning parallel to each other throughout the material, producing ferromagnetism: a strong, macroscopically observable net magnetization, as in iron, cobalt, and nickel. A negative exchange constant instead favours neighbouring spins aligning antiparallel, producing antiferromagnetism: the material still develops genuine, strong magnetic order at the microscopic level, but that order splits into two interpenetrating sublattices with opposite magnetization directions, which cancel exactly to give zero net magnetization overall despite the underlying order being just as real and just as strong.

In both cases, the resulting magnetic order is a genuine phase transition, not merely a gradual weakening with temperature. Below a sharp critical temperature -- called the Curie temperature for a ferromagnet, the Néel temperature for an antiferromagnet -- the exchange interaction's ordering tendency dominates over thermal fluctuations, and long-range magnetic order persists. Above that critical temperature, thermal fluctuations overwhelm the exchange interaction entirely, and the magnetic order disappears qualitatively rather than fading smoothly, leaving a disordered paramagnetic phase with zero long-range spin correlation, in the same spirit as the Ising model's own order-disorder transition applied specifically to real magnetic materials.
