In the early, hot, dense universe, matter existed as an ionized plasma — free electrons and
atomic nuclei, not bound into neutral atoms — and photons scattered constantly off the free
electrons, unable to travel far before being redirected, making the early universe opaque,
the same way a thick fog scatters light. As the universe expanded, it cooled, and about
380,000 years after the Big Bang, the temperature dropped low enough (roughly 3000 kelvin)
that electrons could bind permanently to nuclei without immediately being knocked loose
again by a photon — recombination, despite the name, is really the first time electrons and
nuclei combined at all into stable neutral atoms.

Once nearly all the free electrons were bound up in neutral atoms, photons stopped
scattering constantly and the universe became transparent almost overnight on cosmic
timescales — those photons have been travelling essentially unimpeded ever since. Stretched
by thirteen billion years of subsequent cosmic expansion, their wavelength has redshifted
from the visible/infrared light of a 3000-kelvin surface down to microwaves corresponding to
a present temperature of just 2.7 kelvin, observed today from every direction in the sky as
the cosmic microwave background — a direct, physical snapshot of what the universe looked
like at the single moment recombination happened.

The cosmic microwave background's spectrum matches a blackbody curve more precisely than
almost any laboratory measurement ever achieved, direct confirmation of the hot, dense early
universe the Big Bang model predicts. Superimposed on that nearly uniform temperature are
tiny fluctuations, only about one part in 100,000, that are not noise or measurement
error — they are the direct imprint of small density variations already present in the
early universe at the moment of recombination, the same density variations that gravity
subsequently amplified over billions of years into every star, galaxy, and galaxy cluster
observed today, making the cosmic microwave background's fluctuation pattern one of
cosmology's most precise available probes of the universe's early history and its overall
composition and geometry.
