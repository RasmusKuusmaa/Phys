Computing a Feynman diagram beyond the simplest (tree-level) approximation requires summing
over "loop" diagrams, which involve integrating over the momentum of virtual particles that can,
in principle, carry any momentum at all. For many interacting theories, these loop integrals
diverge — they produce formally infinite results as the integration is extended to arbitrarily
high momentum (equivalently, arbitrarily short distance). This is not, by itself, a sign that
the theory is broken: the parameters appearing in the original Lagrangian (the "bare" mass and
charge) are mathematical bookkeeping quantities, not the actual measured mass and charge of the
particle, since any real measurement of a particle's properties is already affected by its
interactions (including the same loop effects producing the divergence). Renormalization is
the systematic procedure of reorganizing the calculation so that the bare parameters, together
with the divergent loop contributions, combine into the finite, physically measured values —
predictions for actual physical observables (scattering cross-sections, decay rates) come out
completely finite despite the divergent intermediate steps, provided the theory is
renormalizable, meaning its infinities can all be absorbed into a finite number of parameters.

The modern, deeper understanding of why this works comes from the renormalization group: a
coupling constant's effective value is not truly a single fixed number but depends on the
energy scale (equivalently, the distance scale) at which it is measured or probed, an effect
called "running." Physically, this happens because a charged particle's effective charge, as
measured by a probe at some distance, is affected by virtual particle-antiparticle pairs
(vacuum polarization) that partially screen or anti-screen it, and a higher-energy probe that
gets closer sees through more of that screening. This running has been directly confirmed
experimentally: the electromagnetic coupling measured at very high energy (as accessible in
collider experiments) is measurably larger than the low-energy value familiar from everyday
electromagnetism, and the strong coupling famously runs the opposite way, weakening at high
energy (asymptotic freedom), which is why quarks behave as nearly free particles at very short
distances despite being permanently confined at ordinary distances.

Not every quantum field theory is renormalizable in this technical sense — only theories whose
divergences can all be absorbed into a finite set of parameters qualify, and this was
historically a serious, restrictive guide to model-building: the Standard Model's forces
(electromagnetism, the weak force, the strong force) are all renormalizable, but a naive
quantum treatment of gravity is famously not, requiring an ever-growing, effectively infinite
list of new parameters to absorb its divergences order by order, which is a central reason
constructing a consistent quantum theory of gravity remains an unsolved problem distinct in
kind from quantizing the other three fundamental forces.
