An ordinary conductor's electrical resistance decreases smoothly as it
cools, but never reaches exactly zero — even at very low temperature, some
residual scattering of electrons off lattice imperfections and vibrations
remains. Certain materials behave completely differently: cooled below a
sharply defined critical temperature, their electrical resistance drops
abruptly, discontinuously, to exactly zero. This is superconductivity, and
the transition is not a smooth extrapolation of ordinary conduction — it is
a genuine phase transition into a qualitatively different electronic state.

A second, independent hallmark distinguishes true superconductivity from a
hypothetical "perfect conductor" with merely zero resistance: below its
critical temperature, a superconductor actively expels magnetic field from
its interior, a phenomenon called the Meissner effect. A perfect conductor
with zero resistance alone would be expected to simply trap whatever
magnetic field was present at the moment it became resistance-free; a real
superconductor instead pushes field lines out entirely, regardless of the
field's history, which is a distinct and additional physical effect beyond
zero resistance and is why superconductivity is treated as a genuinely new
phase of matter rather than an extreme limit of ordinary conduction.

Conventional superconductivity, as explained by BCS theory (named for
Bardeen, Cooper, and Schrieffer), is a fundamentally quantum-mechanical
phenomenon. Below the critical temperature, electrons in the material form
weakly bound pairs, called Cooper pairs, mediated by interactions with the
vibrating crystal lattice — one electron slightly distorts the lattice as it
passes, and that distortion attracts a second electron, producing a net
effective attraction between the two electrons despite their mutual
electrostatic repulsion. Cooper pairs behave collectively as bosons rather
than as individual fermions, and can condense into a single coherent quantum
state that moves through the crystal lattice without scattering — since
scattering a single pair out of this collective state would require
disrupting the entire coherent condensate, which costs more energy than is
available at low temperature, ordinary resistive scattering simply cannot
occur, producing exactly zero resistance rather than merely a very small
one. Superconductivity today underlies technologies including MRI machines
and high-field magnets, and remains an active area of research, particularly
in the search for materials that superconduct at higher, more practically
achievable temperatures.
