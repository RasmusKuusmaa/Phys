Far from being empty, the space between stars is filled with gas and dust known collectively as the interstellar medium (ISM). By mass it is mostly hydrogen and helium left over from the Big Bang, enriched over cosmic time by heavier elements forged in stars and ejected by stellar winds and supernova explosions. Despite containing a substantial fraction of a galaxy's ordinary matter, the ISM's average density is astonishingly low -- typically far less than one atom per cubic centimeter -- yet it is directly detectable: absorption lines it imprints on the light of background stars, radio emission from atomic and molecular gas, and infrared glow from warmed dust grains all give astronomers direct, quantitative access to its composition, temperature, and motion.

The ISM is not one uniform substance but several distinct phases coexisting in rough pressure balance. Hot, extremely low-density ionized gas, heated to millions of kelvin by supernova shockwaves, occupies vast bubble-like cavities. Warm neutral and warm ionized atomic hydrogen fill much larger volumes at more modest temperatures. Cold neutral atomic clouds are denser and cooler still. At the extreme end sit molecular clouds: dense, cold regions where dust grains shield the interior from destructive ultraviolet starlight well enough that molecules -- including molecular hydrogen and, in denser cores, more complex species -- can form and survive. These phases have wildly different densities and temperatures, but multiply density by temperature and the resulting pressures come out comparable, which is precisely what lets such different environments sit side by side rather than one simply overwhelming the others.

Molecular clouds matter enormously beyond their own chemistry: they are exactly the cold, dense regions where the Jeans instability can push a region into gravitational collapse, making the ISM's densest phase the direct nursery of the next generation of stars. Interstellar dust, meanwhile, leaves its own fingerprint on virtually every observation of a distant star: it scatters and absorbs blue light more efficiently than red, so a star's light reaching Earth after crossing a dusty sightline is both dimmed (extinction) and reddened relative to its true, intrinsic brightness and color -- a correction astronomers must apply before inferring a star's actual distance, temperature, or luminosity.
