Just as Rutherford's early scattering experiments revealed the atom's nuclear substructure by firing alpha particles at gold foil and finding occasional large-angle scattering events impossible for a smooth, uniformly distributed charge, physicists in the late 1960s fired very high-energy electrons at protons at the Stanford Linear Accelerator, seeking to test whether the proton itself had internal structure. Ordinary elastic scattering (the proton recoiling as a whole, staying intact) falls off rapidly at high momentum transfer if the proton is treated as an extended, smoothly-distributed charge -- but the experiments instead measured deep inelastic scattering, where the struck proton breaks apart, and the results at high momentum transfer showed something surprising: the scattering cross-section stopped depending independently on both the energy transfer and momentum transfer separately, and instead depended only on one particular dimensionless combination of the two, a pattern named Bjorken scaling after James Bjorken, who predicted it theoretically.

This specific scaling behavior is exactly the signature expected if the electron is actually scattering off small, essentially free, pointlike constituents rattling around loosely inside the proton, rather than off one continuous charge distribution. Richard Feynman had already proposed, independently of any specific identification, that such pointlike constituents -- which he called partons -- might exist inside hadrons, without committing to what they physically were. The observed Bjorken scaling was direct, striking experimental confirmation that Feynman's phenomenological partons were real physical objects, not merely a convenient mathematical device.

The crucial next step was identifying these partons with the quarks and (electrically neutral, so invisible to electron scattering) gluons that quantum chromodynamics (QCD) had proposed as the fundamental constituents bound together by the strong force. Detailed features of the scattering data -- the fraction of the proton's momentum carried by charged partons, the specific way scaling was observed to be only approximate rather than perfectly exact (a subtle deviation later explained by QCD's own logarithmic running of the strong coupling) -- matched QCD's quantitative predictions closely. This match between deep inelastic scattering data and QCD's specific predictions for how quarks and gluons should behave was the crucial experimental evidence that elevated quantum chromodynamics from a promising but unconfirmed mathematical framework to an experimentally validated theory of the strong interaction.
