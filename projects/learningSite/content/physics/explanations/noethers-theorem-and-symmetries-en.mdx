Every conservation law in mechanics can look like a separate piece of luck:
energy happens to be conserved, momentum happens to be conserved, angular
momentum happens to be conserved. Noether's theorem shows they are not
separate at all — each one is the shadow of a symmetry the Lagrangian
already has.

A symmetry here means a continuous change to the coordinates that leaves the
Lagrangian's value unchanged. Shift every particle in a system by the same
distance in space, and if nothing about the physics depends on absolute
position, the Lagrangian is untouched — that symmetry is exactly what forces
linear momentum to be conserved. Let the whole experiment start a second
later with nothing else changed, and if the Lagrangian doesn't care what time
it is, energy is conserved. Rotate the system rigidly by any angle, and if
the Lagrangian is unchanged, angular momentum is conserved. The theorem turns
each of these "if" clauses into a hard guarantee: continuous symmetry in,
conserved quantity out, with no separate assumption needed.

The word "continuous" is doing real work. The symmetry has to come with a
parameter you can dial smoothly — an angle, a displacement, a time shift —
not a one-off flip like mirror reflection. Discrete symmetries are real and
useful, but they don't feed into this particular theorem; they show up in
physics through different machinery entirely.

It's also a symmetry of the Lagrangian specifically, not of the trajectory a
system actually follows. A ball thrown at an angle traces a lopsided parabola
with no rotational symmetry in the picture at all, yet the Lagrangian
governing it — kinetic minus potential energy, written as a function of
position and velocity — is exactly as time-translation-symmetric as any other
free-fall problem, which is why energy is still conserved along that ungainly
path.
