At the energies of ordinary chemistry, biology and everyday physics, the electromagnetic
force (mediated by the massless photon, with effectively infinite range) and the weak force
(mediated by the very massive W and Z bosons, with a range confined to roughly the size of a
nucleus) look like completely different forces with nothing in common — one long-range and
familiar, one short-range and exotic. Electroweak theory shows this apparent difference is a
low-energy illusion: at energies well above the mass of the W and Z bosons (roughly 100 GeV,
reached in modern particle colliders), the two forces are described by a single, unified
gauge symmetry and behave as one interaction.

The apparent difference at everyday energies is a direct consequence of the Higgs
mechanism: the underlying electroweak symmetry is spontaneously broken by the Higgs field's
nonzero background value, and this breaking is what splits the originally unified force
carriers into the massless photon on one hand and the very massive W and Z bosons on the
other. Below the energy scale set by the W and Z masses, the massive carriers' short range
makes the weak interaction look feeble and short-ranged compared to the still-massless
photon's electromagnetism, even though both trace back to the same underlying symmetry.

This unification was directly confirmed, not just theoretically elegant: electroweak theory
predicted the existence, masses and properties of the W and Z bosons before they were
directly observed, and the 1983 discovery of both particles at CERN, with masses matching
the theory's predictions, was decisive experimental confirmation. Electroweak unification is
also the template and inspiration for grand unified theories, which speculate — without yet
any direct experimental confirmation — that the strong force merges with the electroweak
force at even higher, currently inaccessible energies.
