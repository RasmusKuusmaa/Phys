Superheavy elements, the heaviest members of the periodic table, are not found in nature but
must be synthesized by fusing two lighter nuclei together in a particle accelerator, a process
with an extraordinarily low success probability that typically yields only a single atom at a
time, if it succeeds at all. Once produced, these atoms are extremely short-lived, decaying
within milliseconds to at most a few seconds through alpha decay or spontaneous fission, and
their identity and properties must be inferred entirely from the characteristic pattern of
decay products and energies detected as the single synthesized atom decays, a completely
different experimental regime from bulk chemistry.

The general trend among heavy nuclei, captured by the semi-empirical mass formula's Coulomb
term, is that stability decreases as atomic number increases, since electrostatic repulsion
between an ever-larger number of protons grows faster than the strong force's short-range
binding can compensate. But the nuclear shell model predicts a genuinely different effect
layered on top of this general decline: nuclei with proton or neutron numbers that fill
complete quantum shells ("magic numbers") are measurably more tightly bound and more stable than
their immediate neighbours, the same shell effect responsible for ordinary, well-known magic
numbers among lighter, stable nuclei. Shell model calculations predict that certain specific
proton and neutron numbers well beyond any element synthesized so far correspond to closed
shells for superheavy nuclei too, producing a hypothetical "island of stability" — a region where
nuclei could be dramatically longer-lived than their immediate lighter or heavier neighbours,
potentially by many orders of magnitude, rather than simply continuing the general downward
trend.

This prediction remains genuinely untested, however, because the specific neutron-rich isotopes
predicted to sit at the island's centre cannot yet be reached using the fusion reactions
currently available, which are limited by the neutron content of stable or sufficiently
long-lived target and projectile nuclei that exist to be fused together. The superheavy elements
synthesized so far sit on the approach to the predicted island rather than at its centre, and
some (elements in the flerovium region) do show modestly enhanced half-lives consistent with
approaching genuine shell closure, encouraging evidence that the predicted effect is real, but
confirming the full island of stability awaits either new production techniques capable of
reaching more neutron-rich superheavy isotopes or entirely new experimental approaches not yet
developed.
