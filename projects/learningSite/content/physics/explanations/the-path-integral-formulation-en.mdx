In the path integral formulation, the amplitude for a particle to go from one spacetime
point to another is a sum — formally, an integral — over every possible path connecting
them, no matter how wild or classically forbidden that path looks, each path contributing a
complex phase exp(i S[path]/hbar), where S is the classical action calculated along that
specific path. Unlike ordinary quantum mechanics's single, definite wavefunction obeying one
differential equation, this formulation never picks out one path as "the" trajectory; it
adds up the contributions of literally all of them.

For most paths, especially those far from the classical trajectory, the action S varies
rapidly from one nearby path to its neighbours, and their wildly different phases interfere
destructively, cancelling each other out almost completely. Right around the classical
path — the one extremizing the action, the path that satisfies the ordinary Euler-Lagrange
equations of classical mechanics — nearby paths have nearly the same action and their phases
interfere constructively instead, reinforcing each other. This is exactly why classical
mechanics emerges as the dominant, observed behaviour in the limit where the action is large
compared to hbar: it's not that other paths are literally forbidden, just that their
contributions wash out.

This is mathematically equivalent to solving the Schrodinger equation — the same transition
amplitudes come out either way, this is not a competing physical theory, just a different
but exactly equivalent calculational and conceptual route to them — but it generalizes far
more naturally to quantum field theory, where "summing over paths" becomes "summing over
field configurations," and it makes certain results (the connection between classical
mechanics and the quantum limit, some derivations in statistical mechanics via the formal
similarity between imaginary time and inverse temperature) far more transparent than the
operator formalism does.
