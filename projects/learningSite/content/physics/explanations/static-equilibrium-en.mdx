Newton's first law says an object with zero net force stays at constant
velocity, and for an object already at rest, "constant velocity" includes
staying put. But an object is not just a point — it has extent — and a rigid
body can spin even while its centre of mass sits perfectly still. Zero net
force is not the whole story for a real object; static equilibrium needs a
second, independent condition: zero net torque as well.

The two-forces-cancel trap shows exactly why. Push up on one end of a rod and
down with equal force on the other, both perpendicular to the rod. The forces
sum to zero — the rod does not accelerate sideways — but they do not act
along the same line, so together they form a couple that produces a genuine
net torque and spins the rod. Force balance alone missed this entirely,
because force balance says nothing about *where* along an object each force is
applied, only how big and which direction. Torque is what captures that missing
information: the same force applied far from a pivot produces more torque than
one applied close to it, which is exactly why door handles sit far from the
hinge.

A ladder leaning against a wall is the classic worked example precisely
because it needs both conditions simultaneously: the wall pushes horizontally,
the ground pushes both vertically and, via friction, horizontally, and gravity
pulls down at the ladder's centre of mass — three forces that must sum to
zero, and whose torques about any chosen pivot point must *also* sum to zero.
Checking force balance alone would miss the torque condition that actually
determines whether the ladder's foot is about to slip.
