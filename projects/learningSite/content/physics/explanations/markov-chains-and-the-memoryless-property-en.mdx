Write down everything you would need to know to compute the odds for what a
system does next. If the present state is enough — if knowing the route it took
to get there adds nothing — the process is Markovian. That is a strong
assumption, and it is not a claim that history is irrelevant: history is what
determined the current state. It says only that history acts through the present
and not around it.

The payoff is that the dynamics collapse into a table of transition
probabilities, one row per state, and everything else follows from applying that
table repeatedly. Most chains of practical interest then drift towards a
stationary distribution — the probabilities across states stop changing, even
though individual systems carry on hopping. That is equilibrium in the
probabilistic sense: the traffic into each state balances the traffic out, and
where the chain started has been forgotten. How long that forgetting takes is a
separate question, and for a system with a high barrier between two regions it
can be far longer than the simulation you were planning to run.

This is why Markov chains are so heavily used in physics rather than merely
mentioned. Radioactive decay is Markovian, which is exactly why an old nucleus is
statistically identical to a new one and why the decay law is exponential.
Thermally activated hopping between potential wells is Markovian, so the same
machinery handles diffusion in a crystal. And the whole Monte Carlo method runs
backwards from the stationary distribution: choose transition probabilities that
satisfy detailed balance with respect to the Boltzmann factor, run the chain, and
the states it visits are a sample of the thermal ensemble — which is how a
statistical-mechanics problem too hard to integrate becomes one you can simulate.
