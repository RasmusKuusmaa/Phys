A pure, undoped semiconductor such as silicon conducts electricity only
weakly at room temperature, since its small but nonzero band gap allows only
a modest number of electrons to be thermally excited from the filled
valence band into the empty conduction band. Practical semiconductor devices
rarely rely on pure material; instead they exploit doping — deliberately
introducing a small, carefully controlled concentration of impurity atoms
into the crystal lattice — to engineer the material's conductivity and
charge-carrier type on purpose.

Silicon has four valence electrons, each forming a covalent bond with a
neighbouring silicon atom in the crystal lattice. Replacing an occasional
silicon atom with a donor impurity from group V of the periodic table, such
as phosphorus (five valence electrons), leaves four electrons participating
in bonds just like silicon's, with one extra electron left over — loosely
bound to the donor atom and easily freed into the conduction band by
thermal energy at room temperature. This produces an n-type semiconductor,
named for the extra negative charge carriers (free electrons) it
contributes; the donor impurities themselves stay fixed and become
positively charged once they have donated their extra electron, but it is
the freed electrons, not the fixed ions, that carry current.

Replacing a silicon atom instead with an acceptor impurity from group III,
such as boron (three valence electrons), leaves one bond short an electron —
a vacancy called a hole. A hole is not a literal particle, but it behaves
like one: a nearby valence electron can hop into the vacancy, filling it but
creating a new vacancy at the electron's former position, so the hole
effectively migrates through the crystal exactly like a mobile positive
charge carrier, in a direction opposite the electrons that are actually
moving. This produces a p-type semiconductor, named for its extra positive
charge carriers.

Neither doping type by itself is especially remarkable; the real power of
semiconductor doping emerges when n-type and p-type regions are combined
within a single crystal. A junction between them — a p-n junction — allows
current to flow easily in one direction and blocks it in the other, forming
a diode, and more elaborate combinations of doped regions form transistors,
the switching elements at the heart of essentially all modern digital
electronics.
