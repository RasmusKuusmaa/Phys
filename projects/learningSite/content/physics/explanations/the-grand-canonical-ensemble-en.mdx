The canonical ensemble fixes a system's particle number N and lets only energy fluctuate,
exchanged with a heat bath at fixed temperature T. Many real systems — electrons in a metal
that can move in or out of a region, atoms in a fluid open to a larger container, photons
that can be created or absorbed — don't have a fixed particle number at all, so the grand
canonical ensemble instead lets both energy and particle number fluctuate, in contact with a
reservoir that fixes temperature T and a chemical potential mu, the energy cost of adding one
more particle.

The grand partition function sums the ordinary Boltzmann factor over every possible energy
state and every possible particle number simultaneously, with an extra factor
exp(mu N / kT) rewarding configurations with more particles when mu is positive (or
penalizing them when mu is negative). Every thermodynamic quantity — the average particle
number, the average energy, the pressure, the entropy — follows from this one grand
partition function by straightforward differentiation, exactly as the canonical ensemble's
ordinary partition function generates the canonical quantities.

Applied to a single quantum state that can hold 0 or 1 fermions (obeying the Pauli
exclusion principle), the grand canonical ensemble's average occupation number is exactly
the Fermi-Dirac distribution; applied to a state that can hold any number of bosons, it
gives exactly the Bose-Einstein distribution — both of these famous distributions are not
separate postulates but direct, mechanical consequences of grand canonical statistics
applied to the appropriate particle statistics. This is why the grand canonical ensemble,
not the canonical ensemble, is the natural starting point for quantum statistical mechanics
whenever particle number isn't fixed by hand.
