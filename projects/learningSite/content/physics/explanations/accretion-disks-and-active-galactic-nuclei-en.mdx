Matter falling toward a compact object such as a black hole, neutron star, or white dwarf
almost never falls straight in, because realistic infalling gas carries some angular momentum,
and angular momentum conservation prevents it from simply plunging radially inward. Instead, the
matter settles into a flattened, rotating accretion disk, orbiting the central object while
slowly spiraling inward as viscous friction (turbulence, magnetic stresses, and other
dissipative processes within the disk's gas) transfers angular momentum outward and converts
orbital gravitational potential energy into heat. That heat is radiated away, primarily as
thermal emission whose characteristic temperature and spectrum depend on the accretion rate and
the mass and nature of the central object, making the disk itself, rather than the compact
object, the source of the observed light.

Accretion is an extraordinarily efficient power source, considerably more so per unit mass
than nuclear fusion for accretion onto a sufficiently compact object like a black hole, since a
substantial fraction of the infalling matter's rest-mass energy can be converted to radiation
before it crosses the event horizon (compared with nuclear fusion's roughly one percent
efficiency converting mass to energy). This efficiency is exactly why accretion, rather than
fusion, powers the most luminous compact objects and phenomena in astrophysics: X-ray binaries
(a black hole or neutron star accreting from a companion star), and, at the far larger end,
active galactic nuclei.

An active galactic nucleus is powered by a supermassive black hole, typically millions to
billions of solar masses, sitting at a galaxy's centre and accreting surrounding gas and
disrupted stellar material. The black hole itself, by definition, emits no light at all; the
extraordinary luminosity — in the most extreme cases (quasars) outshining the black hole's
entire host galaxy — comes from the surrounding accretion disk, a hot corona of gas above the
disk emitting X-rays, and often powerful relativistic jets of material launched perpendicular to
the disk, all structures heated and accelerated by the black hole's gravity rather than emitting
light themselves. The particular combination of these structures visible from a given viewing
angle, along with the accretion rate relative to the black hole's mass, produces the diverse
observed range of active galactic nucleus types (Seyfert galaxies, quasars, blazars, and
others), which are now understood as largely the same underlying physical phenomenon viewed
under different conditions and orientations.
