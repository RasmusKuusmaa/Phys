The nuclear shell model, on its own, favors nuclei with closed proton and neutron shells (the
"magic numbers"), which tend to be close to spherical. But for nuclei with several valence
nucleons outside a closed shell, residual interactions between those valence nucleons can
compete with and overcome the shell model's spherical preference, driving the nucleus into a
permanently non-spherical equilibrium shape — typically elongated along one axis (prolate,
roughly football-shaped) or flattened (oblate, roughly disk-shaped) rather than spherical. This
is a genuine, static deformation of the nuclear ground state, not a transient fluctuation, and
it is common across large regions of the chart of nuclides, particularly for nuclei roughly
midway between closed shells.

Deformation has a direct, measurable consequence: a deformed nucleus, having a distinguishable
orientation for its long axis, can rotate collectively as a rigid body, producing a genuine
excited state with definite angular momentum, energy, and a real physical rotation, unlike a
spherical nucleus, where any "rotation" simply reproduces the identical, indistinguishable
configuration and therefore costs no energy at all — spherical nuclei have no meaningful
collective rotational excitations. This is exactly why rotational excited-state bands, sequences
of excited states connected by a common rotational structure, are a direct experimental
signature specifically of nuclear deformation, essentially absent in nuclei close to closed
shells.

Treating a deformed nucleus as a rigid quantum rotor predicts a simple, characteristic pattern
for the energies within a rotational band: energy proportional to the square of the total
angular momentum quantum number (properly, to J(J+1)), scaled by an overall constant set by the
nucleus's moment of inertia. This characteristic energy-spacing pattern is directly measurable
via the gamma-ray transitions between successive states in the band, and because the moment of
inertia depends directly on how elongated or flattened the nucleus actually is, measuring a
rotational band's energy spacing gives nuclear structure physicists a direct, quantitative
experimental probe of a nucleus's collective shape, complementing and cross-checking theoretical
predictions from nuclear structure models.
