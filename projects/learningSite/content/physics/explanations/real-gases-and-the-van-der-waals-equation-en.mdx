The ideal gas law, PV = nRT, rests on two simplifications: molecules are
treated as dimensionless points with no volume of their own, and they are
assumed never to interact except in perfectly elastic collisions. Real
molecules fail both assumptions — they do occupy actual space, and they do
attract each other weakly through intermolecular forces — and the van der
Waals equation is the ideal gas law with a correction term added for each.

The volume correction subtracts a term accounting for the space molecules
themselves take up, since the volume actually available for the gas to move
through is less than the container's total volume once real molecular size is
counted. The pressure correction adds a term accounting for the weak mutual
attraction between molecules, which pulls them slightly toward each other and
so reduces the force with which they strike the container walls compared to
what non-interacting point particles would produce — real pressure ends up
slightly *lower* than the ideal prediction for the same density, unless
corrected for.

Both effects are negligible when a gas is spread out and moving fast: at low
pressure, molecules are far apart on average, so their finite size barely
matters; at high temperature, they move quickly enough that the weak
attractive forces have little time to act before a collision separates them
again. It is precisely the opposite regime — high pressure, low
temperature — where molecules are forced close together and moving slowly,
that both corrections become significant, and not coincidentally, this is
also the regime approaching condensation into a liquid. The van der Waals
equation is in fact good enough to predict roughly where that transition
happens, something the ideal gas law, with no attraction between molecules at
all, cannot capture even qualitatively — an ideal gas can never condense,
because nothing in its model gives molecules any reason to stick together.
