The liquid-drop model treats a nucleus as a roughly incompressible fluid of protons and neutrons, and that picture predicts a specific kind of collective motion: the entire population of protons sloshing back and forth as one coherent body against the entire population of neutrons sloshing the opposite way, a bulk oscillation of the whole nuclear "liquid" rather than any individual nucleon moving on its own. When a nucleus is bombarded with gamma-ray photons across a range of energies, its probability of absorbing a photon shows exactly this behavior: a single, broad, dramatic peak at a specific characteristic energy, called the giant dipole resonance, where the photon's oscillating electric field drives this coherent proton-versus-neutron sloshing motion directly. The resonance's energy depends on how tightly the nucleus's restoring force binds this collective motion, typically appearing at somewhat higher energy in lighter nuclei and lower energy in heavier ones.

Because the giant dipole resonance so overwhelmingly dominates photon absorption in this energy range, it is the essential gateway to essentially every important photonuclear reaction: photon absorption exciting the nucleus enough to eject a neutron or proton, photofission (a photon-induced nuclear fission event), and related processes all proceed by first exciting this same collective resonance, which then decays by emitting a particle or splitting the nucleus rather than by re-emitting the photon directly. Understanding how a nucleus responds to electromagnetic radiation, as opposed to bombardment by particles, essentially means understanding this one resonance and its subsequent decay channels.

Photonuclear reactions built on the giant dipole resonance have genuine practical and scientific importance well beyond nuclear structure physics itself. Photoneutron sources exploit the resonance to generate free neutrons using photon beams rather than a reactor or particle accelerator's usual neutron sources. Nuclear security and safeguards technology uses photon-induced fission signatures to screen for special nuclear material. And in astrophysics, the resonance underlies the photodisintegration reactions relevant to the p-process, one of the nucleosynthesis pathways responsible for producing certain rare, proton-rich heavy isotopes that neither the s-process nor the r-process can reach. Far from being a narrow laboratory curiosity, the giant dipole resonance is one of the most universal and consequential collective phenomena in all of nuclear structure.
