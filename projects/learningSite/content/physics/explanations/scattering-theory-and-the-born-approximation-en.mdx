A particle beam scattering off a potential is described quantum mechanically by a
stationary-state wavefunction that looks, far from the scattering region, like an incoming
plane wave plus an outgoing spherical wave, psi approaches exp(i k z) + f(theta, phi) *
exp(i k r)/r. The function f(theta, phi), the scattering amplitude, contains everything about
how strongly the potential scatters into each direction, and the differential cross-section
is simply its squared magnitude, d(sigma)/d(Omega) = |f(theta, phi)|^2 — the quantum
replacement for the classical impact-parameter calculation.

Finding f exactly requires solving the full Schrodinger equation with the actual potential,
which is only tractable for a handful of special cases. The Born approximation sidesteps
this by treating the potential as a small perturbation on the free particle: to leading
order, the scattering amplitude becomes proportional to the potential's Fourier transform
evaluated at the momentum transfer between the incoming and outgoing directions — f(theta)
is proportional to the integral of V(r) exp(-i q . r) d^3r, where q is the momentum change.
This turns a differential-equation problem into a single, often analytically doable integral.

The Born approximation is valid specifically when the potential is weak and the particle's
energy is high enough that the incoming wave is only mildly distorted by the potential — a
condition that can be checked self-consistently after the fact, not assumed blindly.
Applied to a screened Coulomb potential, it reproduces the Rutherford scattering formula in
the appropriate limit, connecting the classical result directly to its quantum origin, and
applied more generally it's the standard first tool for estimating cross-sections in nuclear
and particle physics before resorting to exact or numerical methods.
