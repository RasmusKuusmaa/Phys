Every nuclear reaction has a Q-value: the total kinetic energy released, equal to the rest-mass energy of the reactants minus the rest-mass energy of the products. An exothermic reaction (Q positive) releases energy and can happen even with a projectile at rest. An endothermic reaction (Q negative) needs to be fed energy from somewhere, and that somewhere is the projectile's kinetic energy.

The naive expectation is that a projectile with kinetic energy equal to |Q| should just barely make an endothermic reaction happen. That is wrong, and the reason is momentum conservation. In the lab frame the target nucleus starts at rest, so the whole system carries whatever momentum the incoming projectile brought with it — momentum that has to still be there after the reaction, carried forward by the products. Building up that shared forward motion costs kinetic energy that never becomes available to pay for the reaction itself. The projectile therefore needs somewhat more than |Q|, and the exact excess depends on how the momentum has to be split: a light projectile hitting a much heavier target wastes very little of its energy on recoil, while a projectile close in mass to the target wastes a lot.

This is exactly why particle accelerators built to produce or study threshold reactions are specified well above the reaction's raw Q-value — the accelerator has to supply the recoil tax as well as the reaction's own energy deficit.
