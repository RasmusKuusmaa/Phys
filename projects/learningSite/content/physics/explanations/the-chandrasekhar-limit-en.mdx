A white dwarf is the remnant core of a low-to-intermediate-mass star that has exhausted its
nuclear fuel, and with no fusion left to generate outward pressure, it must be supported
against gravitational collapse by something else entirely: electron degeneracy pressure. The
Pauli exclusion principle forbids two electrons from occupying the same quantum state, so as a
white dwarf's matter is compressed to extremely high density, its electrons are forced into
ever-higher momentum states simply to satisfy this exclusion, generating a substantial pressure
that has nothing to do with temperature and persists even in a hypothetical zero-temperature
star — this is a purely quantum mechanical effect, not a thermal one, and it is what genuinely
holds a white dwarf up.

For a low-mass white dwarf, the electrons remain non-relativistic, and the resulting degeneracy
pressure rises steeply enough with density that a stable equilibrium radius always exists,
balancing gravity for any given mass, with more massive white dwarfs simply being smaller and
denser. As mass increases, however, the electrons are compressed to higher and higher momenta
and eventually become relativistic, and relativistic degeneracy pressure scales with density
differently — too weakly to keep growing fast enough to continue outpacing gravity's pull as
mass increases further. Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar showed in the 1930s that this produces a
sharp maximum mass, now called the Chandrasekhar limit and equal to roughly 1.4 solar masses
(the precise value depending somewhat on the star's chemical composition): below this mass, a
stable equilibrium radius can always be found; above it, no equilibrium radius exists at all,
not merely a very small or very dense one.

This is a genuine, qualitative transition in the underlying physics, not a rough approximation,
which is exactly why the limit has such dramatic observational consequences. A white dwarf that
accretes matter from a companion star in a binary system, pushing its mass toward the
Chandrasekhar limit, cannot simply continue shrinking stably as it approaches that threshold —
instead, the approach to the limit typically triggers runaway thermonuclear fusion throughout
the degenerate core, disrupting the entire star in a Type Ia supernova, an explosion whose
characteristic peak brightness (set by the nearly universal mass at which it's triggered) makes
these events one of astronomy's most important standard candles for measuring cosmological
distances.
