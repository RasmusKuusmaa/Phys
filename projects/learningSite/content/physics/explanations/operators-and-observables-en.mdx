In classical mechanics, a quantity like energy or momentum is simply a
number attached to a particle's state. Quantum mechanics represents each
measurable quantity — called an observable — not as a number but as an
*operator*, a mathematical action that transforms one wavefunction into
another. Position is represented by an operator, momentum by a different
operator (built from spatial derivatives), energy by yet another. The
wavefunction itself is where all the physical information lives; the
operator is the tool that extracts a particular piece of it.

The link between an operator and what a measurement actually produces runs
through eigenvalues. A wavefunction that is an *eigenstate* of a given
operator is a special one that the operator leaves unchanged in form, merely
scaled by a number — that number is the eigenvalue, and it is exactly the
value a measurement of that observable returns for that state. This is not a
minor mathematical detail: it is the complete answer to "what values can a
measurement give?" A system in an eigenstate of energy has a perfectly sharp,
well-defined energy — no spread, no uncertainty in that quantity — even
though its wavefunction may be spread out in position. Measurement outcomes
are always drawn from an operator's eigenvalues, never anything in between.

Different observables generally have different operators with different sets
of eigenstates, and a wavefunction that is a clean eigenstate of one
observable is typically *not* an eigenstate of another — a state with sharp,
definite momentum, for instance, is necessarily spread out over all of space
in position, with no sharp position value at all. This mismatch between which
states are "sharp" for which observables is the operator-based version of the
uncertainty principle: it is not a statement about measurement disturbance,
but about the mathematical structure of which quantities can and cannot be
simultaneously sharp for the same wavefunction.
