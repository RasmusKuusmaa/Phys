A blackbody is an idealised object that absorbs all radiation striking it and
emits radiation determined purely by its own temperature — "black" describes
its perfect absorption at every wavelength, not its literal colour, since a
hot blackbody visibly glows. Its emission spectrum has a very specific
shape: rising from long wavelengths, peaking somewhere, and falling off again
at short wavelengths, with the whole curve shifting to shorter wavelengths
as temperature increases — which is why a heated metal glows first dull red,
then orange, then white as it gets hotter.

Late-nineteenth-century classical physics, treating the radiation as
continuous electromagnetic waves able to carry any amount of energy, could
predict the long-wavelength side of this curve correctly but failed
catastrophically at short wavelengths: the theory predicted the emitted
energy should keep rising without limit as wavelength shrank, implying every
warm object should radiate infinite power in the ultraviolet and beyond — the
ultraviolet catastrophe. This was not an observed result; it was a prediction
manifestly contradicted by every actual measurement, which never showed
anything like infinite emission.

Planck's resolution, in 1900, was to assume that the oscillators in the
blackbody's walls could only emit or absorb energy in discrete packets, each
of size hf, proportional to frequency, rather than in any continuous amount.
At high frequencies (short wavelengths), each packet becomes so large that
very few oscillators have enough energy to emit one at all, which suppresses
short-wavelength emission and reproduces the observed spectrum exactly.
Planck treated this quantisation as a mathematical device to fix the formula,
without initially believing light itself was made of particles — that further
step was Einstein's, five years later, explaining the photoelectric effect.
Planck's assumption nonetheless marks the moment quantum mechanics began: the
first time physics required energy to come in discrete quanta rather than
flowing continuously.
