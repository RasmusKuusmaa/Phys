After a core-collapse supernova, what remains of the star's core is compressed to nuclear
density, roughly the density inside an atomic nucleus itself, packed into a sphere only
about ten kilometres across despite containing more mass than the Sun. At these densities,
ordinary matter as we know it (atoms with electrons orbiting nuclei) cannot exist — instead,
the star is supported against further gravitational collapse by neutron degeneracy
pressure, the same quantum mechanical effect (the Pauli exclusion principle forbidding
neutrons, as fermions, from occupying the same state) that supports a white dwarf, but here
acting on neutrons rather than electrons, at vastly higher density.

How that degeneracy pressure depends on density — the equation of state — is what actually
determines a neutron star's structure: its radius for a given mass, and critically, the
maximum mass it can support before gravity overwhelms even neutron degeneracy pressure and
the star collapses further into a black hole. Unlike an ideal, non-interacting neutron gas
(whose equation of state could be calculated exactly), a real neutron star's interior
involves genuine strong-force interactions between neutrons at densities beyond what can be
directly probed in a laboratory, so the true equation of state is not yet precisely known,
and different theoretical models predict somewhat different maximum masses and radii.

This is an active area of ongoing research, not a settled textbook fact: observations of
real neutron stars' masses and radii (including from gravitational-wave observations of
neutron star mergers, and from X-ray observations of pulsars) are used to constrain which
equation of state is actually correct, directly testing our understanding of how matter
behaves at densities that can never be reproduced in any terrestrial laboratory. A neutron
star is, in this sense, a natural laboratory for nuclear physics at a density regime
otherwise completely inaccessible.
