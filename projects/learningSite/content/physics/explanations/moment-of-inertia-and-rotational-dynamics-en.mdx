Rotational dynamics is Newton's second law with every term swapped for its
turning counterpart: force becomes torque, acceleration becomes angular
acceleration, and mass becomes moment of inertia, giving τ = Iα. The first
two swaps are straightforward. The third is where the interesting physics
hides.

Mass measures how hard an object is to accelerate in a straight line, and it
does not care how that mass is arranged. Moment of inertia cares about
almost nothing else. Each bit of mass contributes in proportion to the
*square* of its distance from the axis, so mass far from the axis counts
enormously more than mass near it. A hoop and a solid disc of identical mass
and radius are not equally hard to spin: the hoop, with all its mass out at
the rim, has twice the moment of inertia.

This also means an object has no single moment of inertia — only a moment of
inertia *about a stated axis*. The same rod is four times harder to spin
about one end than about its centre, purely because the mass has moved
further out on average. It is why a tightrope walker's long pole helps, why
flywheels are heavy at the rim rather than the hub, and why a solid cylinder
beats a hoop to the bottom of a slope even when they weigh the same: less of
the released energy has to go into spin.
