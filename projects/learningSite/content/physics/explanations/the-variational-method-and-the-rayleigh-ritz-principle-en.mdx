Take any normalized trial wavefunction at all, not necessarily an exact eigenstate of anything, and compute the expectation value of the system's Hamiltonian in that state. The Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle guarantees a striking, one-sided result: this expectation value can never come out below the system's true ground-state energy, no matter how the trial wavefunction is chosen. It can equal the ground-state energy exactly (if the trial function happens to be the true ground state), or exceed it by a little or a lot, but it can never undershoot it. The proof follows almost immediately from expanding the trial wavefunction in the system's true (unknown) energy eigenbasis: every term in that expansion contributes an energy at or above the ground-state energy, so the weighted average -- the expectation value -- must too.

This one-sided guarantee turns approximating a ground state into a practical optimization procedure. Choose a trial wavefunction whose functional form is motivated by physical intuition about what the true ground state probably looks like (its symmetry, its rough shape, how it should behave at the boundaries), but leave one or more parameters in that form free to adjust. Compute the energy expectation value as a function of those parameters, then minimize it. Whatever parameter values come out of that minimization give the best approximation available within the chosen functional form, and the resulting minimized energy is guaranteed by the Rayleigh-Ritz principle to sit above the true ground-state energy -- so a lower computed energy is unambiguously a better approximation, never a sign that something has gone wrong.

The variational method's real strength relative to perturbation theory is what it does not require: perturbation theory needs the actual system to be a small deviation away from some exactly solvable one, an assumption that simply fails for many systems of genuine physical interest. The variational method substitutes a completely different requirement instead -- a good physically motivated guess for the trial wavefunction's shape -- and works regardless of how far the true system sits from anything exactly solvable. This is why the method is used so widely, from estimating the ground-state energy of the helium atom (where the two electrons' mutual repulsion makes an exact solution impossible) to modern quantum chemistry's molecular structure calculations. Extending the method to excited states additionally requires constraining the trial wavefunction to be orthogonal to the ground state (and to any lower excited states already found), since without that constraint the minimization simply collapses back onto reapproximating the ground state.
