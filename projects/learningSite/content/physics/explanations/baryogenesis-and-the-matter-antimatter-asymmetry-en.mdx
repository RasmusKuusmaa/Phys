Every fundamental interaction physicists have ever measured that creates a particle also creates its antiparticle in equal number, and standard thermal equilibrium physics in the extremely hot, dense early universe gives no obvious reason to prefer one over the other. Extrapolated naively, this would suggest the early universe contained essentially equal numbers of matter and antimatter particles, which should then have almost entirely annihilated each other into radiation as the universe cooled, since particle and antiparticle annihilate on contact. Yet the observable universe today is made overwhelmingly of matter, with essentially no bulk antimatter anywhere, and detailed measurements (from both the cosmic microwave background and Big Bang nucleosynthesis) pin down the size of the original imbalance precisely: roughly one extra matter particle for every billion matter-antimatter pairs. That tiny surviving excess, left over after the billion-to-one annihilation, is the entire matter content of everything observed today -- every star, planet, and galaxy.

Explaining how that excess arose is the problem of baryogenesis, and in 1967 Andrei Sakharov identified three necessary conditions any successful mechanism must satisfy simultaneously. First, some process must actually violate baryon number conservation, since starting from a baryon-symmetric state, only a baryon-number-violating process can shift the balance at all. Second, both charge-conjugation (C) symmetry and the combined charge-parity (CP) symmetry must be violated; without CP violation specifically, any process creating a matter excess would be exactly matched by a mirror process creating an equal antimatter excess, canceling out to zero net asymmetry. Third, all of this must happen away from thermal equilibrium, because a system in true equilibrium is time-symmetric enough that any asymmetry a baryon-violating, CP-violating process manages to create gets immediately erased by the equilibrium's own reverse reactions -- only an out-of-equilibrium setting lets a generated asymmetry survive and freeze in.

The Standard Model does, in fact, contain CP violation, directly observed in kaon and B-meson decays and incorporated into the CKM matrix describing quark mixing. The trouble is quantitative: when physicists calculate how much matter-antimatter asymmetry the Standard Model's known CP violation, combined with its known (though heavily suppressed) baryon-number-violating processes, could actually generate, the answer comes out many orders of magnitude smaller than the observed one-in-a-billion excess. This shortfall means the Standard Model, taken alone, cannot be the whole story -- some additional source of CP violation or an entirely new baryon-number-violating mechanism beyond the Standard Model must be at work, making the origin of all ordinary matter one of the most significant open problems connecting particle physics and cosmology.
