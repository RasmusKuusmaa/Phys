Inside the bulk of a liquid, each molecule is pulled equally in all
directions by its neighbours, and these attractive forces cancel out. A
molecule sitting at the liquid's surface is different: it has neighbouring
liquid molecules pulling it inward and sideways, but no liquid molecules
above it pulling outward (only much weaker attraction to whatever gas sits
above), so it experiences a net inward pull. The result is that the surface
as a whole behaves as though it were under tension, like a stretched elastic
sheet, resisting any increase in surface area — this is surface tension,
and it is why small liquid drops tend toward a spherical shape, the shape
that minimizes surface area for a given volume, and why some insects can
stand on the surface of water without breaking through it.

Surface tension also governs what happens when a liquid meets a solid
surface, particularly inside a narrow tube — capillarity. Two competing
forces determine the outcome: cohesion, the liquid's attraction to itself,
and adhesion, the liquid's attraction to the tube's wall material. When
adhesion is stronger than cohesion, as for water in a clean glass tube, the
liquid "wets" the wall, curving upward at the edges to form a concave
meniscus, and surface tension pulls the liquid up the narrow tube until the
weight of the raised column balances the upward pull — this is capillary
rise, and it is narrower for wider tubes since a wider tube has
proportionally less wall contact per unit volume of liquid raised. When
cohesion is stronger than adhesion, as for mercury in a glass tube, the
liquid does not wet the wall; it curves downward into a convex meniscus and
is actually depressed below the surrounding liquid level rather than rising.

Capillarity is not a universal effect that happens for every liquid in every
tube; whether a liquid rises, is depressed, or shows no significant capillary
effect at all depends entirely on the relative strength of cohesive and
adhesive forces for that particular liquid-and-wall-material combination.
Capillary action driven by surface tension is responsible for phenomena
ranging from water rising through the narrow vessels of plant stems to the
wicking action of paper towels and the behaviour of ink in a fountain pen.
