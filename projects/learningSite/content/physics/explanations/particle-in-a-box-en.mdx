Take a quantum particle and trap it between two rigid walls. Its wavefunction
must go to zero at each wall — there is no probability of finding it inside
solid wall — and that single requirement does all the work. Only waves that
fit a whole number of half-wavelengths in the gap can satisfy it, exactly
like the modes of a guitar string clamped at both ends. Every other
wavelength is simply not an option.

Because momentum is tied to wavelength, restricting the wavelengths
restricts the momenta, and restricting the momenta restricts the energies.
The allowed energies form a discrete ladder rising as n², with n = 1, 2, 3…
This is where quantisation comes from in the simplest possible setting: not
an extra postulate bolted on, but the consequence of confining a wave.

Two features of that ladder repay attention. It has no zero rung: the n = 1
state has energy above the bottom of the box, so a confined quantum particle
can never be completely at rest. That residual zero-point energy is the
uncertainty principle showing up in a concrete case — pinning the position
down inside the box guarantees a spread of momentum. And the levels rise as
the box narrows, since a shorter box demands a shorter wavelength and hence
a larger momentum. This is why confinement costs energy, why electrons in
small quantum dots glow at bluer colours than those in large ones, and why
matter resists being squeezed long after the classical forces have given up.
