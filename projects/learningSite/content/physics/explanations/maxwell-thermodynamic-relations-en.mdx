Each of the four thermodynamic potentials — internal energy U, enthalpy H, Helmholtz free energy F, and Gibbs free energy G — has a differential written entirely in terms of two natural variables (for G, that's temperature T and pressure p: dG = −S dT + V dp). Because G is a well-behaved function of state, it doesn't matter in which order you take its two partial derivatives — mixing ∂/∂T and ∂/∂p gives the same result either way. Writing that equality out is exactly a Maxwell relation.

The payoff is practical, not just formal. Entropy is awkward to measure directly, but (∂S/∂p) at constant T shows up naturally from G's differential. The matching Maxwell relation says this equals −(∂V/∂T) at constant p — the rate at which a substance expands when heated at fixed pressure, something you can read straight off a thermal-expansion measurement. Four potentials, each with two variables, give four such relations, and each swaps an entropy derivative for a directly measurable mechanical one.

None of this depends on the substance being an ideal gas, or on any particular equation of state. The relations follow from calculus applied to any system in equilibrium, which is exactly why they're called relations rather than laws: nothing new is being asserted about the physics, only that a state function's own consistency constrains how its measurable properties must relate to each other.
