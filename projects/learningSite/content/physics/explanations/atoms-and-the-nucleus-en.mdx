Nearly all of an atom's mass is packed into a nucleus so small that if an
atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be roughly a
pea at the centre — everything else is mostly empty space occupied by the
comparatively feather-light electrons.

That mass imbalance is the opposite of what intuition suggests. Electrons
surround the whole atom and are what most of chemistry cares about, but a
proton or neutron is nearly 2,000 times more massive than an electron, so
the nucleus dominates an atom's mass almost completely despite being a
tiny fraction of its volume.

Every atom of a given element has the same number of protons, which is what
defines the element in the first place, but the number of neutrons can
vary. Atoms of the same element with different neutron counts are called
isotopes — carbon-12 and carbon-14 are both carbon, both with six protons,
differing only in how many neutrons ride along in the nucleus.
