An ordinary star, and even a white dwarf, can be modeled to excellent approximation with the
Newtonian equation of hydrostatic equilibrium: pressure gradients balance the weight of
overlying material, with gravity sourced purely by mass-energy density. A neutron star pushes
this approximation past its breaking point: its gravitational binding energy is a significant
fraction of its total mass-energy, spacetime near and within it is measurably curved, and
Newtonian gravity's simplifying assumptions no longer hold. The Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff (TOV)
equation is the correct replacement, derived by substituting a spherically symmetric,
static metric into the Einstein field equations and working out the resulting equilibrium
condition for a fluid with a given equation of state (pressure as a function of density).

The key physical difference from the Newtonian case is that, in general relativity, pressure
itself contributes to the source of gravity, not merely mass-energy density alone — the TOV
equation's right-hand side includes explicit pressure-dependent terms (both the local pressure
and an integrated mass term that itself depends on the pressure profile throughout the star)
absent from the Newtonian hydrostatic equation entirely. In modest-density, weak-field
conditions this correction is negligible and the TOV equation reduces smoothly to the familiar
Newtonian form, but at neutron-star densities the correction is substantial and qualitatively
changes the star's behaviour.

This extra pressure-sources-gravity effect is exactly what produces a genuine maximum possible
neutron star mass. In the Newtonian picture, a sufficiently "stiff" equation of state (pressure
rising very steeply with density) could in principle support arbitrarily large mass, the same
intuition that lets a sufficiently strong material support more weight in ordinary engineering.
In the TOV picture this intuition breaks down: beyond a certain point, the additional pressure
needed to support more mass itself sources additional gravity, working against the very support
it's meant to provide, so for any given equation of state there exists a maximum mass beyond
which no static equilibrium configuration exists at all — attempting to add more mass drives
further collapse rather than reaching a new, denser equilibrium. The precise numerical value of
this maximum mass depends on the nuclear equation of state at the extreme densities found in a
neutron star's core, which remains imperfectly known, so different equation-of-state models
predict somewhat different maximum masses; observational measurements of actual neutron star
masses and radii, particularly from gravitational-wave observations of neutron star mergers,
are an active and ongoing source of constraints on which equation of state is actually correct.
