The cosmic microwave background reveals that the early universe was almost, but not quite,
perfectly smooth: density varied from place to place by only about one part in 100,000.
Cosmological perturbation theory treats these tiny variations as small perturbations on top
of an otherwise homogeneous, expanding universe, and studies how they evolve under gravity
using the same field equations that describe the universe's overall expansion, just linearized
around that smooth background. In the linear regime, each Fourier mode of the density field
grows independently, with overdense regions pulling in surrounding matter and becoming more
overdense, while underdense regions lose matter and become emptier still.

Ordinary (baryonic) matter could not start this process early, because before recombination
it was ionized and tightly coupled to radiation through Thomson scattering, and radiation
pressure resisted gravitational collapse, effectively smoothing out baryonic perturbations
faster than gravity could grow them. Dark matter, whatever its microscopic nature, does not
interact electromagnetically and felt none of that radiation pressure, so its overdensities
were free to grow under gravity from far earlier on. By the time of recombination, dark matter
had already built up substantial density contrasts, and once ordinary matter was released from
radiation pressure, it fell rapidly into the gravitational potential wells dark matter had
already carved out — without dark matter's head start, ordinary matter alone would not have
had time to grow today's structures within the universe's actual age.

Linear perturbation theory accurately describes this early growth phase, but it breaks down
once a region's density contrast grows of order unity or larger, at which point the evolution
becomes genuinely nonlinear and must be followed with numerical N-body and hydrodynamical
simulations rather than analytic equations. The combination is what actually reproduces the
observed cosmic web: perturbation theory correctly identifies which regions are overdense
enough to eventually collapse and roughly when, while simulations follow the full nonlinear
collapse, merging, and virialization that build the filaments, sheets, clusters, and voids
mapped by galaxy surveys today, a structure whose statistical properties match this picture in
remarkable, quantitative detail.
