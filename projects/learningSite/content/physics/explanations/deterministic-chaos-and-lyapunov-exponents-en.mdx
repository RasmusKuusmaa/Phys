Run a chaotic system twice from exactly the same starting state and you get
exactly the same trajectory: nothing in the equations is random. Run it from two
states that differ by a millionth of a millimetre and the two agree for a while,
then drift apart until they have nothing to do with each other. That is the whole
of chaos — deterministic rules, plus a separation between neighbours that grows
exponentially rather than steadily.

The Lyapunov exponent is the growth rate in that exponential: a small initial
separation is multiplied by e raised to the exponent times the elapsed time, so
the reciprocal of the exponent is roughly how long a small error takes to triple.
The sign is what matters most. Negative means neighbouring states converge and
the motion is predictable; zero means they drift apart only in proportion to
time; positive means the system forgets its initial condition at a definite rate.
Two details keep the definition honest. The exponent is an average over the
attractor, because the stretching rate differs from point to point on it, and the
exponential law describes only the early growth — once the separation is as large
as the attractor itself, the two trajectories are just two unrelated points on
the same object and the gap cannot widen further.

That bound is exactly what separates chaos from ordinary instability. A ball
balanced on a hilltop also has a positive exponent, but it rolls away and never
returns, while a chaotic system keeps folding back on itself, stretching along
one direction and squeezing along another — which is what builds the layered
structure of a strange attractor. The practical consequence is the forecast
horizon. Errors compound multiplicatively while precision is bought additively:
measuring the initial state a hundred times better extends the useful forecast by
the natural logarithm of a hundred divided by the exponent, a fixed number of
days rather than a hundredfold gain. That is why a weather model whose exponent
is about half a day inverse gains a week or so of skill from a vastly better
observing network, and why no instrument will ever deliver a useful three-month
forecast.
