James Clerk Maxwell proposed a thought experiment in 1867 to probe the statistical nature of the
second law of thermodynamics: imagine a tiny, intelligent "demon" controlling a small door between
two chambers of gas at equal temperature. The demon watches individual molecules approach the
door and opens it only to let fast-moving molecules pass into one chamber and slow-moving
molecules into the other, apparently sorting the gas into a hot chamber and a cold chamber over
time without doing any mechanical work on the gas itself. Since this would decrease the system's
entropy without any compensating entropy increase elsewhere, it appeared to violate the second law
of thermodynamics, which forbids exactly this kind of net entropy decrease in an isolated system.

The resolution, developed substantially through the work of Leo Szilard and later Rolf Landauer
and Charles Bennett across the 20th century, hinges on properly accounting for the demon's own
physical requirements. The demon must acquire information about each approaching molecule (its
speed) to decide whether to open the door, and while this measurement step can in principle be
performed reversibly with no fundamental thermodynamic cost, the demon cannot store an
ever-growing record of every molecule it has observed indefinitely if it is to keep operating in a
repeating cycle. At some point it must erase its accumulated memory to make room for new
observations, and Landauer's principle, formulated by Rolf Landauer in 1961, shows that erasing
one bit of stored information is a fundamentally irreversible process that requires dissipating a
minimum amount of energy as heat, kT ln 2 (Boltzmann's constant times temperature times the
natural logarithm of two) per bit erased, and correspondingly generates a minimum increase in
entropy.

This minimum entropy cost of erasure exactly compensates the entropy decrease the demon's sorting
achieves, restoring the second law's validity once the entire cycle — sorting, measuring, and
eventually erasing — is properly accounted for as a single closed system. Landauer's principle is
not merely a resolution to a thought experiment or a loose analogy between information and
physical entropy; it is a precise, quantitative physical law giving an exact minimum energy cost
for erasing information, a prediction that has since been directly tested and confirmed in real
laboratory experiments using actual physical bit-storage and erasure systems, establishing a
genuine, experimentally verified bridge between information theory and thermodynamics.
