An excited atom can lose its extra energy in two very different ways. Left
alone, it eventually undergoes spontaneous emission, releasing a photon at a
random time in a random direction with a random phase — this is how an
ordinary light bulb or a fluorescent tube produces light, and the resulting
light is incoherent, a jumble of unrelated photons. But Einstein showed that
a second process is also possible: if a photon of exactly the right energy
passes near an already-excited atom, it can trigger stimulated emission,
causing the atom to drop to the lower state immediately and emit a second
photon that is identical to the first in energy, phase, direction, and
polarization. The word "laser" is itself an acronym for exactly this
process: Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.

A single stimulated-emission event doubles one photon into two identical
ones, and each of those two can go on to stimulate further emissions from
other excited atoms, so that a cascade can in principle amplify light
enormously while every photon produced stays locked in phase and direction
with the ones that triggered it — this is the physical origin of a laser
beam's coherence, its narrow spectral purity, and its tight directionality,
all traits absent from ordinary incoherent light sources.

Achieving net amplification requires overcoming a competing process:
ordinary atoms in the ground state readily absorb photons of the right
energy rather than emitting them, and in a normal thermal population, atoms
in the ground state vastly outnumber excited atoms, so absorption dominates
and light passing through simply gets absorbed rather than amplified. A
laser medium must therefore be pumped — supplied with energy, often optically
or electrically — to create a population inversion, an artificial
non-equilibrium state in which more atoms sit in the excited state than in
the lower state. Only once this inversion is achieved does stimulated
emission outpace absorption, allowing the cascade of identical photons to
build up faster than it is absorbed, producing sustained laser output.
