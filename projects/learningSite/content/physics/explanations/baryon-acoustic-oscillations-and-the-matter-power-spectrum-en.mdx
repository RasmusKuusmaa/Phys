Before recombination, photons and baryons (ordinary matter) were tightly coupled into a single
fluid through Thomson scattering, and radiation pressure within that fluid resisted
gravitational collapse strongly enough to support genuine sound waves — pressure waves rippling
outward from every initial density perturbation, exactly the same physical mechanism that
carries ordinary sound through air, just on a cosmic scale and carried by a photon-baryon
plasma rather than air molecules. Each of the tiny primordial density perturbations later
responsible for the cosmic microwave background's temperature fluctuations launched such a
spherical sound wave, expanding outward from that perturbation's location.

Recombination abruptly ended this: once photons decoupled from baryons, the plasma's supporting
pressure vanished for the baryonic component, the sound waves stopped propagating, and their
pattern froze into the baryon distribution at whatever radius each wave had reached at that
moment — a fixed physical scale, the sound horizon at recombination, imprinted throughout the
distribution of ordinary matter. This does not create a hard boundary or a wall galaxies cannot
cross; it produces a subtle statistical preference for galaxy pairs to be separated by
approximately that characteristic distance, detectable only by carefully analyzing the
clustering statistics (technically, the two-point correlation function or its Fourier transform,
the matter power spectrum) of enormous galaxy surveys containing millions of galaxies, not
something visible in any individual galaxy's position.

Because the sound horizon's physical size is set by well-understood, essentially
parameter-free pre-recombination plasma physics — and is independently, precisely calibrated
using the cosmic microwave background's own detailed structure — this baryon acoustic
oscillation feature functions as a "standard ruler": an object (or in this case, a statistical
feature) of known physical size whose apparent angular size, measured at different redshifts
(and hence different cosmic epochs), directly constrains the universe's expansion history over
time. Combined with the matter power spectrum's overall shape, which encodes the relative
amounts of dark matter, ordinary matter, and radiation through how perturbations of different
sizes grew differently in the early universe, baryon acoustic oscillation measurements from
large galaxy surveys provide one of cosmology's most robust, least model-dependent tools for
constraining the universe's expansion rate and composition, complementary to and largely
independent of the cosmic distance ladder's chain of calibrated standard candles.
