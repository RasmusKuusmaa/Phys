Very different physical systems — a ferromagnet losing its magnetization, a liquid-gas
system at its critical point, certain binary alloys ordering — show a striking coincidence
near their respective continuous phase transitions: the critical exponents describing how
various quantities vanish or diverge as the transition is approached are often numerically
identical, or fall into just a handful of matching "universality classes," despite the
underlying physics being completely different at the microscopic level. Mean-field theory,
being a crude averaging approximation, offers no real explanation for why this should be
exactly true rather than just roughly similar.

The renormalization group explains it by asking what happens to a system's effective
description under a systematic change of scale: group together small blocks of the original
system (say, clusters of neighbouring spins) into single effective "super-spins," rewrite
the system's interactions in terms of these coarser variables, and repeat. This defines a
flow through the space of possible theories as the length scale is progressively coarsened,
and right at a continuous phase transition, this flow reaches a fixed point — a theory that
looks the same at every subsequent scale, having lost all memory of the microscopic details
it started with.

Universality follows directly: any two systems whose renormalization-group flows lead to
the same fixed point must show the same critical behaviour near their transition, regardless
of how different their microscopic physics actually is, because by the time you've zoomed
out far enough, the flow has washed out those microscopic differences entirely — only a few
large-scale features (the system's spatial dimension, and the symmetry of its order
parameter) survive to determine which fixed point, and hence which universality class and
which critical exponents, a given system flows to. This reframes "why do a magnet and a
fluid share critical exponents" from a mystery into an inevitable consequence of both
flowing to the same fixed point.
