For an ideal (non-interacting) gas of bosons, the Bose-Einstein distribution allows an
unlimited number of particles to occupy the same single-particle ground state, unlike
fermions. At high temperature, the particles spread out over a huge number of available
states and the ground state's occupation is negligible, just like a classical gas; but as
temperature drops, fewer excited states are thermally accessible, and below a specific
critical temperature, the excited states alone can no longer hold the specified total
particle number, however low the chemical potential is pushed.

The only way to conserve the total particle number below that critical temperature is for
the ground state to absorb the shortfall directly, holding a macroscopic number of
particles — a finite fraction of the whole gas, not just "a lot" in some vague sense,
growing to nearly 100% as temperature approaches absolute zero. This is Bose-Einstein
condensation: a genuine phase transition (a discontinuity in how the ground-state occupation
depends on temperature) driven entirely by quantum statistics, requiring no interaction
between the particles whatsoever — it happens for an ideal, non-interacting Bose gas exactly
as described.

This is a purely quantum effect with no classical counterpart: classically, particles never
preferentially pile into one particular state just because of statistics, since classical
particles are distinguishable and each configuration is treated independently. Bose-Einstein
condensation was predicted in the 1920s but only directly observed experimentally in 1995,
in dilute alkali-metal gases cooled to nanokelvin temperatures using laser and evaporative
cooling — the extreme cooling needed is exactly what makes the effect, while universal in
principle for any boson gas, so hard to see directly for anything except the most carefully
isolated, dilute systems.
