A central force is any force whose direction always points exactly toward or
away from one fixed point — gravity from a star, the electric force between
two point charges, a mass on an ideal spring anchored at the origin. The
defining geometric fact about such a force is what makes it special: torque is
force times the perpendicular lever arm, and a force pointing directly along
the line to the pivot has zero perpendicular component about that pivot,
whatever the force's strength or how it depends on distance. Zero torque about
the centre means angular momentum about that centre is exactly conserved,
throughout the entire motion — not approximately, and not only for gravity's
particular 1/r² form, but for any central force whatsoever.

That conservation law is what gives orbital motion its most useful structural
simplification. Angular momentum L = mvr sin θ being fixed links speed and
distance together at every point of the path, which is precisely Kepler's
second law in more general clothing: closer to the centre, the object must
move faster to keep L constant, and farther away it moves slower, sweeping
equal areas in equal times regardless of the orbit's exact shape. Rather than
tracking a full two-dimensional trajectory, the conserved angular momentum lets
the problem be reduced to a single equation in one variable, the radial
distance from the centre — the angular part of the motion is already
determined once L is known.

This is why every central force — not just gravity — produces orbits confined
to a plane (since angular momentum has a fixed direction as well as
magnitude) that sweep out area at a constant rate. What differs between
different central forces is only the *shape* the orbit ultimately takes:
gravity's 1/r² law produces the closed ellipses of Kepler's first law, while
other central forces produce different, sometimes non-closed paths — the
conservation law is universal to the whole family, but the resulting geometry
depends on which particular force is doing the pulling.
