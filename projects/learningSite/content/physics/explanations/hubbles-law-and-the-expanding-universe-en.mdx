Examine the light from distant galaxies and, with only rare nearby
exceptions, its characteristic spectral lines are shifted toward longer
(redder) wavelengths compared with the same lines measured in a laboratory
on Earth — a redshift, closely related to the Doppler shift familiar from
sound and from light emitted by objects receding through space. In the
1920s, Edwin Hubble measured both the redshift and the distance of a number
of galaxies and found a striking relationship: redshift grows directly in
proportion to distance, a relationship now called Hubble's law. A galaxy
twice as far away shows roughly twice the redshift, a galaxy ten times as
far away shows roughly ten times the redshift, and so on, essentially
without exception for sufficiently distant galaxies.

The straightforward reading of this pattern — that every distant galaxy is
physically flying away from us through static, unchanging space, faster the
farther away it already is — raises an immediate puzzle: why would we
happen to be sitting at the one special centre that everything is fleeing
from? The resolution is that this reading is not the correct physical
picture. Instead, general relativity describes the redshift-distance
relationship as arising from the expansion of space itself: the physical
distance between any two sufficiently separated points in the universe
grows over time, not because either point is moving through space in the
ordinary sense, but because the space between them is stretching. A photon
of light travelling across this stretching space has its wavelength
stretched right along with it, redshifted by an amount that grows with how
much space it has crossed — and since more distant galaxies have more
expanding space between them and us, their light is stretched, and
therefore redshifted, correspondingly more, reproducing Hubble's
distance-proportional relationship directly.

This "expanding space" picture resolves the apparent puzzle completely:
because the expansion happens uniformly everywhere, not outward from any
particular point, every observer anywhere in the universe — not just an
observer on Earth — sees every sufficiently distant galaxy receding from
them in exactly the same distance-proportional way, with no special centre
or privileged vantage point required. Hubble's law, and the broader
expansion it reveals, is the central observational pillar supporting the
Big Bang model of cosmology: running the observed expansion backward in
time implies the universe was once far smaller and denser than it is today,
a conclusion later corroborated independently by the discovery of the
cosmic microwave background radiation, the cooled relic of the early
universe's own intense heat.
