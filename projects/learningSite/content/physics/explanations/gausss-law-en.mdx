Gauss's law makes a statement that sounds almost too simple to be useful: the
total electric flux passing out through any closed surface — a sphere, a cube,
any shape at all — depends on nothing but the total charge enclosed inside it.
Charge outside the surface contributes flux going in on one side and exactly
the same flux going out the other, so it cancels completely and never counts.
Where the charge sits *inside* the surface does not matter either; only the
total does.

That total independence from shape is precisely what makes the law powerful
rather than merely elegant. Direct calculation of the electric field from
Coulomb's law means adding up the contribution of every piece of charge
individually, which is often intractable. Gauss's law sidesteps this entirely
when a charge distribution has enough symmetry to choose a surface — called a
Gaussian surface — on which the field is constant by symmetry and can be
pulled outside the flux integral. A point charge suggests a sphere centred on
it; an infinite charged line suggests a cylinder around it; an infinite
charged sheet suggests a flat pillbox through it. In each of these cases what
would be a difficult integral collapses to simple algebra.

None of this restricts the law to symmetric situations — Gauss's law holds
for every charge distribution and every closed surface, always. It is only
*useful for finding the field directly* when symmetry provides a convenient
surface; for anything else it remains true but is put to work differently, as
one of Maxwell's four equations governing the field everywhere at once.
