Nuclear medicine applies nuclear physics in two essentially opposite directions: using
radioactivity to see inside the body without disturbing it (diagnostic imaging) and using
radiation deliberately to destroy targeted tissue (radiotherapy). Positron emission tomography
(PET) uses a radioactive tracer, a biologically active molecule labeled with a positron-emitting
isotope, introduced into the body and taken up preferentially by tissue with the relevant
biological activity (such as high glucose metabolism, common in tumours). When the tracer decays,
the emitted positron travels a very short distance before annihilating with a nearby electron,
producing two 511 keV gamma rays travelling in almost exactly opposite directions. A ring of
detectors surrounding the patient looks for pairs of gamma rays arriving in close time
coincidence at roughly opposite detector positions, and the line connecting each coincident pair
constrains the annihilation point, letting many such coincidences be reconstructed into a
detailed three-dimensional image of tracer concentration throughout the body.

Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) uses a different class of tracer, one that
directly emits a single gamma ray per decay rather than a positron. Because a single gamma ray
carries no directional information analogous to PET's back-to-back pair, SPECT instead uses a
physical collimator — a plate with narrow channels that only pass gamma rays travelling in a
specific direction — placed in front of the detector to determine which direction each detected
gamma ray came from, a generally less efficient and less precise localization method than PET's
electronic coincidence detection, though SPECT tracers are often cheaper and more widely
available.

Radiotherapy instead deliberately delivers a damaging dose of ionizing radiation to destroy
targeted tissue, most commonly a tumour, exploiting the fact that rapidly dividing cancer cells
are generally more vulnerable to radiation damage than most healthy tissue. Rather than simply
irradiating the whole region uniformly, modern radiotherapy techniques deliberately shape the
spatial dose distribution — using multiple beams delivered from different angles that overlap
only at the target, or techniques that modulate beam intensity across the treatment field — to
concentrate the damaging dose specifically at the tumour while keeping the dose received by any
single path of healthy tissue comparatively low. Both diagnostic imaging and radiotherapy depend
critically on dosimetry, the quantitative science of calculating exactly how much radiation
energy is actually absorbed by specific tissues, essential for keeping diagnostic doses as low
as reasonably achievable while still producing a usable image, and for calculating a
radiotherapy treatment plan precisely enough to destroy the target while staying below the
damage threshold for nearby critical organs.
