In the late 1990s, two independent teams measuring distant Type Ia supernovae — reliable
standard candles because their peak brightness is remarkably consistent — found that these
supernovae appeared systematically fainter, and therefore farther away for their measured
redshift, than a universe decelerating under the gravitational pull of matter alone would
predict. This was a direct empirical discovery, not a theoretical prediction confirmed
afterward: the observation itself demonstrated that the universe's expansion is accelerating,
which the second Friedmann equation shows requires a component with sufficiently negative
pressure, now called dark energy, contributing a genuinely repulsive gravitational effect.

Dark energy's behaviour is characterized by its equation-of-state parameter w, the ratio of its
pressure to its energy density. A cosmological constant — a constant energy density that doesn't
dilute as the universe expands, mathematically equivalent to the term Einstein originally added
to his field equations — corresponds to exactly w=-1, unchanging over cosmic time, and is the
simplest model consistent with current observational data. But "dark energy" is the broader
phenomenon, not a synonym for "cosmological constant": current measurements are consistent with
w=-1 but do not rule out alternative models where w differs slightly from -1 or evolves with
cosmic time, an active area of ongoing observational cosmology aimed at distinguishing a true
cosmological constant from a more dynamical form of dark energy.

If dark energy is indeed a cosmological constant, a natural place to look for its physical
origin is the vacuum energy predicted by quantum field theory — even empty space, in quantum
field theory, has a nonzero zero-point energy from the vacuum fluctuations of every quantum
field. Naively summing up this vacuum energy using an energy cutoff at the scale where quantum
gravity effects should become important produces a predicted cosmological constant roughly 120
orders of magnitude larger than the value actually observed, an almost unimaginable mismatch
often called the worst quantitative prediction in the history of physics. This cosmological
constant problem remains a genuinely open foundational puzzle at the boundary between quantum
field theory and general relativity: no accepted mechanism explains why the vacuum energy's
gravitational effect should be so dramatically suppressed relative to its naive theoretical
value, or why the small nonzero value that is observed takes the particular value it does.
