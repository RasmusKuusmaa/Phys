Every star sheds some of its own material into space continuously, a phenomenon called a stellar wind, but the driving mechanism differs sharply depending on the star. In a Sun-like star, the wind originates in the corona, the star's extremely hot, tenuous outer atmosphere; that gas's own thermal pressure is enough to push a slow, steady outflow of particles away from the star, closely analogous (though far less concentrated) to steam pushing its way out of a kettle. This thermally-driven wind carries away mass at a very modest rate -- over the Sun's entire multi-billion-year lifetime, it loses only a tiny fraction of one percent of its total mass this way, small enough that Sun-like stellar evolution models can treat the star's mass as effectively fixed.

Hot, massive, highly luminous stars experience an entirely different driving mechanism. Their intense radiation field is absorbed and scattered by specific spectral lines (transitions of particular ions) in the outer atmosphere, and each such absorption or scattering event transfers a small amount of the photon's momentum to the absorbing ion, pushing it outward; repeated over enormous numbers of photons and ions, this radiation pressure accelerates a substantial, sustained outflow -- a radiation-driven wind, powered ultimately by the star's own luminosity rather than by simple thermal pressure. Because the most massive, luminous stars sit closest to their own Eddington limit, where radiation pressure is already nearly strong enough to overcome gravity outright, their radiation-driven winds can be extremely powerful.

This difference in wind strength has real evolutionary consequences. A massive star's radiation-driven wind, sustained over its comparatively short lifetime, can strip away a substantial fraction of its initial mass well before the star ever reaches the end of its nuclear-burning life, directly reducing its core mass and altering its internal structure and subsequent evolutionary path. Mass loss is therefore not a minor correction for such stars but a genuinely essential ingredient of any realistic evolutionary model, and together with a star's initial mass, it is one of the key factors determining what kind of supernova the star eventually produces and what sort of compact remnant -- white dwarf, neutron star, or black hole -- is left behind.
