For most of the twentieth century the kilogram was a cylinder of platinum-iridium
in a vault outside Paris, and the kilogram was whatever that cylinder weighed. It
was a workable definition with an uncomfortable property: the official copies
drifted relative to it by tens of micrograms over a century, and there was no way
to say which of them had changed. Since May 2019 no SI unit rests on an object.
Each is defined by declaring the numerical value of a constant to be exact — the
caesium hyperfine frequency for the second, the speed of light for the metre, the
Planck constant for the kilogram, the elementary charge for the ampere,
Boltzmann's constant for the kelvin, the Avogadro constant for the mole.

Two things follow. The definitions are now permanent in a way an artefact never
was, and they are democratic: any laboratory with a Kibble balance or an
optical clock can realise the unit from the definition, rather than sending a
mass to France for comparison. And crucially, nothing measurable changed at the
moment of the switch. Continuity was designed in, so the new kilogram matches the
old one within the uncertainty to which the old one was known, and no earlier
measurement needed revising.

Realising a unit is specialist work; using one is not, and the bridge between
them is traceability. A measurement is traceable when its result can be related
to a realisation of the definition through an unbroken chain of comparisons, each
documented and each contributing its own uncertainty. In practice: a national
metrology institute realises the unit, calibrates a reference standard, which
calibrates a working standard, which calibrates the meter on your bench, and the
uncertainty grows a little at every step. This is why a calibration certificate
reports a deviation and an uncertainty rather than a verdict, why calibrations
expire, and why an instrument with a great many digits and no chain behind it
tells you only what it is repeating, not what is true.
