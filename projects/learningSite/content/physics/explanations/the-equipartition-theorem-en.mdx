A single point mass moving in three dimensions has three independent ways to
store kinetic energy — motion along x, along y, along z — each contributing
½mv² to the total, one term per direction. The equipartition theorem
generalises this observation into a precise rule: at thermal equilibrium,
every independent quadratic degree of freedom — every separate way a system
can store energy that enters as the square of some variable — carries the
same average thermal energy, ½kT, regardless of what that degree of freedom
physically is.

For a single structureless (monatomic) gas molecule, only the three
translational directions count, giving ³⁄₂kT of kinetic energy per molecule on
average — exactly the result kinetic theory derives directly. A diatomic
molecule like N₂ has that same translational motion, but it can also *rotate*
about two independent axes perpendicular to the bond (rotation about the bond
axis itself carries essentially no moment of inertia and does not count), and
each of those adds another ½kT. At ordinary temperatures a diatomic gas
therefore carries ⁵⁄₂kT per molecule — more internal energy at the same
temperature than a monatomic gas, purely because it has more independent ways
to store thermal motion.

This is not a minor bookkeeping detail; it directly predicts each gas's molar
heat capacity, since more degrees of freedom mean more energy must be
supplied to raise the temperature by a given amount. At sufficiently high
temperatures, diatomic and polyatomic molecules also begin *vibrating*, adding
yet more degrees of freedom (each vibrational mode actually contributes a
full kT, since it stores energy in both a kinetic and a potential quadratic
term at once) and raising the heat capacity further — which is exactly why
measured heat capacities climb with temperature in a way the simplest kinetic
theory, counting only translation, cannot explain on its own.
