A heat engine takes in heat at a high temperature, converts some of it into
work, and dumps the rest at a low temperature. The obvious engineering
question is how small that dumped fraction can be made. Carnot's answer,
reached before anyone had written down the second law, is that it has a hard
floor set by nothing but the two temperatures: efficiency at best equals
1 − T_cold/T_hot, with the temperatures in kelvin.

The crucial point is what kind of limit this is. It is not a statement about
friction, turbulence or imperfect insulation, all of which are real and all
of which push a practical engine further below the ceiling. Even a perfectly
frictionless, perfectly insulated engine cannot exceed it. The reason is
entropy: taking heat Q from the hot reservoir lowers its entropy by Q/T_hot,
and the only way to balance the books is to deposit entropy somewhere
cooler. The exhaust heat is not waste in the sense of a design flaw — it is
the payment the second law requires.

Two consequences follow. Efficiency depends on the *ratio* of the
temperatures, so lowering the cold side helps exactly as much as raising the
hot side; power stations care about their cooling water for this reason.
And a real engine matching the Carnot limit would have to run infinitely
slowly, through a sequence of reversible steps, producing no power at all.
Practical design is therefore always a trade between efficiency and output,
never a hunt for the missing hundred per cent.
