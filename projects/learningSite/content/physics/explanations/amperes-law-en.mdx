Where Gauss's law relates a field's outward flux through a closed surface to
the charge enclosed, Ampère's law relates the magnetic field's circulation
around a closed *loop* to the current passing through it. Circulation here
means summing the field's component along the loop's direction all the way
around — and exactly as with Gauss's law, that sum depends only on what
passes through the loop, never on the loop's exact size or shape. A tight
circular loop and a large irregular one, both encircling the same current
once, give identical circulation.

This shape-independence is what makes the law computationally useful in
symmetric situations, the same way Gauss's law is: for an infinite straight
wire, choosing a circular loop centred on the wire makes the field constant by
symmetry along the whole loop, and a hard integral becomes simple algebra.

The law as Ampère first wrote it had a hole, found by considering a charging
capacitor. Current flows in the wire leading to each plate, but nothing
crosses the gap between the plates — yet a compass placed near that gap
still deflects, showing a real circulating magnetic field with no current
threading the loop that field circles. Maxwell resolved this by recognising
that the *changing* electric field between the plates, as the capacitor
charges, acts as a source of magnetic field in exactly the way an ordinary
current does — the displacement current. Adding that term completes the law
and is what makes it symmetric with Faraday's law, where a changing magnetic
field similarly produces a circulating electric field. That symmetry between
the corrected Ampère's law and Faraday's law is what allows changing electric
and magnetic fields to sustain one another with no charges or currents
present at all — an electromagnetic wave.
