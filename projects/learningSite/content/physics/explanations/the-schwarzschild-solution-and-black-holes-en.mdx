Karl Schwarzschild found the first exact solution to the Einstein field equations within a
year of their publication, describing the vacuum spacetime outside any spherically
symmetric, non-rotating mass — a star, a planet, or, in the limit, a point mass. For an
ordinary star or planet, the solution matches smoothly onto the interior solution and
reproduces Newtonian gravity to high accuracy at large distances, with small, precisely
measured relativistic corrections (like the perihelion precession of Mercury's orbit) close
to the mass.

The Schwarzschild solution contains a specific radius, the Schwarzschild radius,
proportional to the mass, at which the solution's mathematical expression appears to become
singular. For an ordinary star or planet, this radius lies deep inside the object where the
vacuum solution no longer applies anyway, so it has no physical consequence. But if a mass
is compact enough that its actual physical radius is smaller than its Schwarzschild radius,
the mathematics describes a genuine boundary in spacetime, the event horizon: inside it,
spacetime is curved so extremely that every possible future path, even for light, leads only
further inward, never back out.

The apparent singularity in the Schwarzschild solution's coordinates right at the
Schwarzschild radius turns out to be a coordinate artifact, not a genuine breakdown of the
physics there — switching to a different, better-behaved coordinate system (such as
Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates) shows spacetime is perfectly smooth at the event horizon
itself; a freely falling observer crossing it would notice nothing special happening
locally. The genuine physical singularity, where the curvature itself actually does become
infinite and general relativity's predictions break down, sits at the very centre, r = 0,
not at the horizon — and what actually happens physically there remains an open problem,
since a full theory of quantum gravity is thought to be needed to describe it correctly.
