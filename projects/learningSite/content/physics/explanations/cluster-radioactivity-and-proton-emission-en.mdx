Gamow's explanation of alpha decay treats the emitted alpha particle as tunneling quantum-mechanically through the Coulomb barrier separating it from the parent nucleus, escaping despite not having enough energy to classically climb over that barrier at all. This same tunneling picture turns out to generalize well beyond alpha decay specifically. Certain very heavy nuclei occasionally emit bound fragments considerably larger than a helium nucleus -- carbon-14 and neon-24 are among the most commonly observed examples -- in a process called cluster radioactivity, governed by exactly the same tunneling physics, just applied to a heavier fragment tunneling through a correspondingly reshaped barrier. Cluster radioactivity never replaces a nucleus's ordinary alpha decay; it occurs alongside it, as a genuine but extremely rare side channel, typically many orders of magnitude less probable than the same parent's ordinary alpha emission.

Proton emission is a related but distinct extension of the same tunneling idea, found in nuclei on the opposite, proton-rich side of the chart of nuclides, right at the edge of the proton drip line. There, the last bound proton is only barely attached to the nucleus, and rather than a pre-formed cluster tunneling out, a single individual proton tunnels directly through a barrier shaped by two combined effects: the ordinary Coulomb repulsion pushing the proton away, and a centrifugal (angular momentum) barrier arising from whatever orbital angular momentum that proton carries in its shell-model state. Because this centrifugal barrier depends sensitively on the proton's angular momentum, measuring a proton emitter's decay rate gives direct, sensitive experimental access to the angular momentum and shell-model configuration of the emitted proton's original orbital.

Together, cluster radioactivity and proton emission demonstrate that Gamow's tunneling explanation for alpha decay was not a special, isolated result but an instance of a much more general principle: a nucleus in an energetically favorable position to shed some sub-configuration of its nucleons will do so by quantum tunneling through whatever barrier separates it from that lower-energy state, whether the emitted piece is a single proton, a helium nucleus, or a fragment as large as carbon or neon, with the details of the barrier -- and therefore the observed decay rate -- set by exactly what is being emitted and from which parent nucleus.
