A planet orbiting another star is, in almost every case, far too faint and far too close in
apparent angular separation to its vastly brighter host star for any telescope to resolve
directly — direct imaging succeeds only in a small number of especially favorable cases, such
as young, still-warm giant planets orbiting at wide separation from relatively nearby stars.
The overwhelming majority of the thousands of confirmed exoplanets have instead been found
indirectly, by detecting the planet's gravitational or geometric influence on properties of the
host star that actually can be observed.

The radial velocity method detects a star's own small reflex motion: as a planet orbits, both
planet and star actually orbit their common centre of mass, so the star traces out a small orbit
of its own, periodically moving slightly toward and away from Earth. This tiny back-and-forth
motion produces a correspondingly tiny, periodic Doppler shift in the star's spectral lines,
measurable with sufficiently precise spectroscopy. The size of this Doppler shift depends on the
planet's mass, the star's mass, and the orbital geometry, so radial velocity measurements yield
(with some orbital-inclination ambiguity) the planet's mass, but say nothing directly about its
physical size.

The transit method instead detects a tiny, periodic dimming of the star's observed brightness
as a planet passes directly in front of it (from Earth's vantage point) once per orbit, blocking
a small fraction of the star's light proportional to the planet's cross-sectional area relative
to the star's. Transit photometry therefore directly yields the planet's radius (and orbital
period), but says nothing directly about its mass. Because these two leading methods measure
complementary quantities, applying both to the same planet — as is routinely done for
transit-discovered planets bright enough for follow-up spectroscopy — yields both mass and
radius, letting astronomers calculate the planet's average density and thereby infer its likely
bulk composition: a high density consistent with a rocky, Earth-like planet, or a low density
consistent with a gas-dominated giant, extending exoplanet characterization well beyond simply
confirming a planet's existence.
