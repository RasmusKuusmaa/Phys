A macrostate is everything you can observe about a system from the outside
without tracking every particle — a gas's total pressure, volume and
temperature, or for a set of coins, simply "how many show heads". A
microstate is the complete, exact specification of every individual
particle — every molecule's position and velocity, or every single coin's
face — and enormously many different microstates can produce the identical
macrostate. Flip four coins and "exactly two heads" is one macrostate; HHTT,
HTHT, HTTH, THHT, THTH and TTHH are six distinct microstates that all satisfy
it. The number of microstates sharing a given macrostate is its
*multiplicity*.

Multiplicity is not evenly spread across macrostates, and the imbalance grows
extreme fast as a system gets larger. "All four heads" has multiplicity 1 —
only one sequence gives it. "Exactly two heads" has multiplicity 6, already
six times more likely by simple counting. Scale this up from four coins to
the roughly 10²³ molecules in a mole of gas, and the macrostate where
molecules are spread evenly throughout a container has a multiplicity so
astronomically larger than the macrostate where they happen to cluster in one
corner that the clustered state is never observed in practice — not because
it is forbidden, but because it is so improbable that no system to ever exist
has waited long enough to land on it.

This is what Boltzmann's S = k ln W actually counts: W is the multiplicity of
a macrostate, and entropy is essentially its logarithm. A system drifting
toward higher entropy is doing nothing more mysterious than drifting toward
whichever macrostate has more microstates able to realise it — the second
law of thermodynamics, read this way, is a statement about counting, not
about any special "force" pushing systems toward disorder.
