A plasma is an ionized gas: a collection of particles in which a substantial fraction exist as
free, mobile charged particles (electrons and positive ions) rather than bound together into
neutral atoms or molecules. This is a qualitative distinction from an ordinary neutral gas, not
merely a matter of higher temperature — the presence of freely mobile charge carriers allows a
plasma to conduct electricity, respond collectively to electric and magnetic fields, and support
entirely new families of collective oscillations and waves that a neutral gas cannot sustain at
all, regardless of how hot it becomes. Plasma is often called the fourth state of matter, and,
contrary to the impression that it is an exotic laboratory curiosity, it is in fact the
overwhelmingly dominant state of ordinary matter in the observable universe: stars, the
interstellar and intergalactic medium, and planetary magnetospheres are all composed of plasma,
making solid, liquid, and ordinary neutral gas comparatively rare states of matter on a cosmic
scale.

A defining collective behaviour of a plasma is Debye shielding. If an isolated charge (or a
localized excess of charge) is introduced into a plasma, the plasma's own mobile charges do not
remain passively distributed — oppositely charged particles are attracted toward the intruding
charge while like-charged particles are repelled away from it, rearranging the local charge
density so as to progressively cancel out the intruding charge's electric field with increasing
distance. This screening is not merely a gradual weakening of the field; beyond a characteristic
distance called the Debye length (which depends on the plasma's temperature and density), the
field is essentially completely cancelled, falling off far more steeply with distance than the
ordinary inverse-square vacuum field would. Any region of the plasma much larger than a Debye
length therefore appears essentially electrically neutral from the outside, even though it is
actually composed of separately mobile positive and negative charges — a property called
quasi-neutrality.

The Debye length sets the fundamental spatial scale below which individual particle interactions
matter and above which the plasma behaves as a genuinely collective medium, and it underlies
essentially all of plasma physics's distinctive phenomena: it determines the wavelength scales at
which plasma oscillations and various plasma waves can propagate, sets the thickness of the sheath
region that forms wherever a plasma contacts a solid boundary, and is a central parameter in
describing everything from the solar wind and Earth's ionosphere to the magnetically confined
plasmas used in fusion reactor research.
