Ordinary pulsars are rotation-powered: their characteristic emission draws on rotational kinetic energy, which is why their spin gradually slows over time as that energy is radiated away. A magnetar is a different kind of neutron star entirely. Formed, like any neutron star, in a core-collapse supernova, a magnetar's progenitor core happened to be spinning unusually fast and convecting vigorously as it collapsed, conditions that sustain an efficient dynamo process capable of generating a magnetic field thousands of times stronger than an ordinary pulsar's -- typically on the order of a quadrillion times stronger than any field ever produced in an Earth laboratory. At that strength, the energy stored in the magnetic field itself vastly exceeds the star's rotational energy, making the field, not spin-down, the dominant power source for everything the star does.

That stored magnetic energy does not sit still. As the field slowly evolves and rearranges inside and around the star, it can build up enormous stress in the neutron star's rigid outer crust, occasionally exceeding the crust's structural strength and cracking it in an event called a starquake, closely analogous in spirit to a terrestrial earthquake but driven by magnetic stress rather than tectonic stress. This releases a sudden, intense burst of X-rays and soft (relatively low-energy) gamma rays -- exactly the observational behavior that defines the soft gamma repeater class, so named because these sources repeat such bursts over months and years rather than emitting once.

The most extreme magnetar events, called giant flares, are amongst the most energetic transient phenomena observed anywhere in the galaxy: in a fraction of a second, a giant flare can release more energy than the Sun radiates in tens of thousands of years, briefly outshining every other gamma-ray source in the sky combined, and the most powerful recorded giant flares, originating thousands of light years away, have been intense enough to measurably ionize part of Earth's own upper atmosphere -- a directly detectable planetary-scale effect from a single stellar event across the galaxy. Despite this dramatic behavior, magnetars are genuinely rare: only around thirty are confirmed among the roughly one billion neutron stars thought to populate the galaxy, marking them as a distinct, comparatively uncommon evolutionary outcome of core collapse rather than simply an alternate view of an ordinary pulsar.
