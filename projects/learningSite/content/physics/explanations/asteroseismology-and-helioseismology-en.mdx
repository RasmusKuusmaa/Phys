Convective turbulence churning beneath a star's surface continuously excites sound waves throughout its interior. These are not traveling waves that simply escape; confined by the star's own structure, they reflect and refract repeatedly off internal boundaries and gradients in sound speed, and only certain wavelengths fit back onto themselves constructively after a round trip, setting up genuine standing-wave oscillation modes, each with its own precise resonant frequency. Crucially, each mode's specific frequency depends on the exact internal sound-speed and density profile of the layers that particular wave's path samples, which means the observed spectrum of oscillation frequencies is a direct, physical fingerprint of the star's actual internal structure -- not a model extrapolated from surface data alone, but a genuine measurement reaching into the interior.

Each individual mode's effect at the surface -- a tiny periodic brightness flicker or a tiny periodic Doppler shift in surface velocity -- is far too small to matter on its own. What makes the technique work is that a star oscillates in enormous numbers of distinct modes simultaneously, and years of continuous, ultra-precise photometric or spectroscopic monitoring let astronomers statistically decompose the resulting complex, noisy signal into its full spectrum of discrete frequencies, extracting far more information collectively than any single mode could provide alone. Applied to the Sun, where the star is close and bright enough for its surface oscillation pattern to be spatially resolved and imaged directly, this technique is called helioseismology, and it has mapped the Sun's internal rotation rate as a function of both depth and latitude, precisely located the boundary between its radiative and convective zones, and tested the standard solar model to remarkable precision.

Applied to other stars, which cannot be spatially resolved and so are observed only as a single point of combined, unresolved light, the identical underlying physics is called asteroseismology instead. Space telescopes purpose-built to monitor thousands of stars' brightness continuously and with extreme precision have used this technique to measure other stars' masses, radii, and ages directly from their oscillation spectra -- quantities that would otherwise require much less direct, more model-dependent methods -- turning what began as a way to study the Sun's interior into a general tool for characterizing stars, and the planets found orbiting them, across the galaxy.
