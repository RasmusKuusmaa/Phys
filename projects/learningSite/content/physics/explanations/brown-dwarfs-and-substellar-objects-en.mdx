When a fragment of a collapsing molecular cloud ends up with too little mass, it never reaches the core temperature and density needed to ignite and sustain ordinary hydrogen fusion, the reaction that defines a true star and powers it steadily for most of its life. Below roughly seventy-five times the mass of Jupiter, a collapsing fragment instead becomes a brown dwarf: gravitational contraction still heats its core, and the lightest brown dwarfs do briefly fuse deuterium, a rare hydrogen isotope that ignites at a much lower temperature than ordinary hydrogen, but this limited fuel supply is exhausted quickly, within the first tens of millions of years. Afterward, no internal fusion source remains, and the object is supported against further collapse purely by electron degeneracy pressure -- the same quantum-mechanical effect (electrons resisting compression into the same quantum states) that supports white dwarfs.

This has a direct observational consequence: without an ongoing internal energy source, a brown dwarf can only radiate away the heat left over from its formation and gravitational contraction, and once that reservoir starts draining, nothing replenishes it. A brown dwarf therefore cools and dims steadily and continuously over billions of years, unlike a true fusion-powered star, which burns its core hydrogen at a self-regulated, roughly steady rate and so shines at a roughly constant luminosity for most of its main-sequence lifetime. A given brown dwarf's observed temperature and brightness at any moment consequently depend heavily on its age as well as its mass, making brown dwarfs considerably harder to characterize from a single observation than ordinary stars.

Brown dwarfs occupy a genuine, physically distinct middle ground between true stars and giant planets, not an arbitrary label on one smooth continuum. The lightest true stars are specifically massive enough to sustain hydrogen fusion; brown dwarfs are massive enough to briefly fuse deuterium but never ordinary hydrogen; and giant planets like Jupiter, typically built up through core accretion within a protoplanetary disk rather than direct cloud collapse, never fuse even deuterium at all. Each boundary marks a genuinely different physical threshold being crossed, which is why brown dwarfs are classified as a distinct type of substellar object rather than simply an undersized star or an oversized planet.
