Nothing about an aerosol matters as much as the diameter of its particles.
Settling under gravity goes as diameter squared, so a 50 micrometre grain drops
out of still air in seconds, a 10 micrometre one takes minutes, and below about a
micrometre gravity has effectively stopped mattering — those particles are
carried by air currents, wander by Brownian motion, and leave the atmosphere by
sticking to surfaces, clumping together or being rained out. That is why coarse
dust settles onto a windowsill while smoke haze crosses a border.

Size decides the health effect too, and for the same mechanical reason. Particles
larger than about ten micrometres are caught in the nose and throat; below that
they reach the airways, and below 2.5 micrometres they penetrate to the alveoli
where gas exchange happens. Air-quality regulation is written around exactly
those cut-offs — PM10 and PM2.5 — and both are *mass* concentrations, micrograms
per cubic metre, not counts. Since mass goes as diameter cubed, the number is
dominated by the largest particles in each range, and an event that produces
enormous numbers of ultrafine particles can barely move the PM2.5 figure.

Measuring an aerosol therefore means choosing which physical effect will stand
in for size. The reference method is gravimetric: pull a known volume of air
through a filter with a size-selective inlet and weigh it, which is unambiguous,
traceable and gives one number every 24 hours. Optical counters instead flash
each particle through a laser beam and size it from the scattered pulse, giving
a live size distribution — but scattering depends on refractive index and shape,
so the reading is the diameter of an equivalent calibration sphere and soot reads
differently from sea salt. Other instruments measure attenuation of a beam or of
beta radiation through collected material, or charge particles and measure their
electrical mobility. Each answers a slightly different question, which is why a
cheap sensor and a reference station can disagree without either being faulty,
and why co-location and calibration are part of any serious monitoring
programme.
