The electric field E and magnetic field B are defined in terms of a scalar potential V and a
vector potential A, but that definition involves only certain derivative combinations of V
and A — specifically, B = curl(A) and E = -grad(V) - dA/dt. Because the curl of a gradient
is always zero, adding the gradient of any scalar function to A, while simultaneously
shifting V by the negative time derivative of that same function, leaves both E and B
completely unchanged. This freedom is a gauge transformation, and it means V and A are never
uniquely determined by the physical fields alone.

Since E and B are the only quantities that are directly measurable (they're what a test
charge actually feels, via the Lorentz force law), any two sets of potentials related by a
gauge transformation describe exactly the same physics — asking "which one is the real
potential" has no physical answer, the same way asking which of two equally valid
coordinate systems is the "real" one has none. This is gauge invariance: physical
predictions can never depend on which gauge is chosen, only the mathematical convenience of
the calculation can.

Choosing a specific gauge is purely a calculational convenience. The Coulomb gauge (div A =
0) is convenient for problems without radiation, since it makes the scalar potential obey
the same equation as ordinary electrostatics; the Lorenz gauge (a specific combination of
div A and the time derivative of V) is convenient for radiation and relativistic problems,
since it makes both potentials individually satisfy the same simple wave equation, sourced
by charge and current respectively, and makes the four-potential's covariant nature
manifest. Different gauges make different calculations easier, but every gauge, correctly
used, must predict the exact same E and B in the end.
