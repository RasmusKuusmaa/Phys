Raw experimental data plotted exactly as a physical law predicts often comes
out as a curve rather than a straight line — inverse-square laws, quadratic
relationships, and exponential decays are all common in physics and none of
them produce straight lines when plotted directly. Curves are harder to work
with than straight lines for a very practical reason: the human eye is
excellent at judging whether points lie on a straight line and at spotting
outliers or systematic curvature in the scatter, but far worse at judging
these things against a curved reference. Fitting parameters are also easier
to extract from a line — a slope and an intercept — than from the several
parameters that typically define a curve.

Linearisation exploits this by re-plotting a transformed combination of the
measured variables, chosen specifically so that the underlying physical law
predicts a straight line in the new coordinates. If theory predicts
y = kx², plotting y against x² (rather than against x) produces a straight
line through the origin with slope k — the transformation does not change
the underlying physics at all, it only changes which combination of
variables is placed on each axis. If theory predicts exponential decay,
y = A·e^(−λx), plotting the natural logarithm of y against x produces a
straight line with slope −λ and intercept ln(A), since taking the logarithm
of both sides gives ln(y) = ln(A) − λx, an equation linear in x. Power-law
relationships y = Ax^n are similarly linearised by plotting log(y) against
log(x), which gives a line of slope n and intercept log(A).

The choice of which transformation to apply is not arbitrary; it must follow
directly from the specific functional form the underlying physics predicts.
Applying an unjustified transformation can force almost any data into
looking roughly linear, which defeats the entire purpose — linearisation is
a tool for testing and extracting parameters from a specific predicted
relationship, not for making arbitrary data look tidy. Once genuinely
linearised, a straight-line fit (see least-squares fitting) gives both the
best-fit values of the physical parameters and a clear visual and
statistical check of whether the data actually supports the assumed
relationship in the first place.
