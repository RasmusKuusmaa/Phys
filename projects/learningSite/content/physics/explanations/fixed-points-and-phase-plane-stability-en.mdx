Instead of plotting position against time, plot position against velocity. Each
state of the system is then a single point, and the equations of motion become a
flow: through every point runs one arrow saying where the state goes next. A
whole solution is a curve threaded along those arrows, and the picture of all of
them together is the phase portrait. Fixed points are where the arrow has zero
length — put the system exactly there and it never moves. A pendulum has two of
them within one full turn: hanging straight down, and balanced straight up.

Both are fixed points, but nobody would call them equally stable, and the
difference is visible in the flow around each one. To get at it quantitatively,
write the state as the fixed point plus a small offset and keep only the terms
linear in that offset. What is left is a 2×2 matrix, and its eigenvalues say what
happens to the offset: a negative real part means it decays, so trajectories come
in; a positive real part means it grows, so they leave; an imaginary part means
the offset rotates while it does either. That gives the standard catalogue —
stable and unstable nodes, spirals, and the saddle, which has one eigenvalue of
each sign and so attracts along one direction while repelling along another. The
upright pendulum is a saddle, which is exactly why balancing it is possible in
principle and hopeless in practice: only a perturbation aimed exactly along the
incoming direction ever falls back.

There is one case the linear analysis genuinely cannot settle. When both
eigenvalues are purely imaginary, the linearised picture shows closed orbits — a
centre — but the terms just discarded are the same size as the effect being
predicted, so whether the true trajectories spiral slowly in, slowly out, or
close exactly is decided by the nonlinear terms. An undamped pendulum really does
have closed orbits; add the slightest damping and the centre becomes an inward
spiral. That borderline case is not a defect of the method so much as the place
where nonlinear dynamics starts: it is where limit cycles, bifurcations and
eventually chaos all live.
