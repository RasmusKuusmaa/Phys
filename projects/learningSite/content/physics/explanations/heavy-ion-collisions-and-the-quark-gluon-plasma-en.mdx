Under ordinary conditions, quantum chromodynamics confines quarks and gluons permanently inside
individual hadrons (protons, neutrons, and other composite particles); no isolated quark has ever
been observed. Heavy-ion collision experiments — smashing heavy nuclei like gold or lead
together at energies approaching the speed of light — briefly overcome this confinement across
an extended region of space, by heating and compressing the overlapping volume of colliding
nuclear matter to temperatures and energy densities not seen anywhere in the universe since
roughly a microsecond after the Big Bang. Under these extreme conditions, the usual confinement
breaks down collectively across the whole interaction region, rather than one quark at a time,
producing a quark-gluon plasma in which quarks and gluons from many nucleons move relatively
freely across a volume far larger than any single hadron.

This plasma exists for an extraordinarily brief time, expanding and cooling within an
almost unimaginably short interval before hadronizing back into ordinary particles well before
reaching any detector, so no detector ever directly observes the plasma itself. Instead, its
existence and properties are established indirectly, through characteristic experimental
signatures in the final particles that are best explained by a brief intermediate quark-gluon
plasma phase: the suppression of high-momentum particle jets (since a high-energy quark or gluon
traveling through the dense plasma loses much more energy than it would traveling through
ordinary hadronic matter), and specific patterns of collective flow in how the momenta of the
many produced particles correlate with each other and with the collision's geometry.

A genuinely surprising experimental result, established at facilities including the Relativistic
Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC) and later the Large Hadron Collider (LHC), is that the quark-gluon
plasma does not behave as a weakly interacting gas of nearly free quarks and gluons, despite
what its name might suggest. Instead, it behaves as a nearly perfect fluid, with an
extraordinarily low viscosity-to-entropy ratio close to a theoretical minimum bound, flowing
collectively in a way closer to an ideal liquid than a dilute gas. This finding significantly
reshaped theoretical understanding of how strongly interacting matter behaves at extreme
temperature, and heavy-ion collision experiments remain an essential, ongoing experimental probe
of quantum chromodynamics in the regime where its coupling is too strong for simple perturbative
calculation.
