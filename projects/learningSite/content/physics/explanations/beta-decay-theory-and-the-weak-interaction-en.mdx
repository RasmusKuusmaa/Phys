In beta-minus decay, a neutron inside a nucleus converts into a proton, emitting an electron
and an antineutrino; in beta-plus decay, a proton converts into a neutron, emitting a
positron and a neutrino. Both processes are mediated by the weak interaction, one of the four
fundamental forces, acting here at the level of individual nucleons (and, more fundamentally,
at the level of the quarks inside them, converting a down quark to an up quark or vice versa).
Unlike the nucleus's other decay modes, no particle involved in beta decay was "already
there" beforehand — the electron (or positron) and the neutrino (or antineutrino) are both
created at the instant of decay, directly analogous to how a photon is created when an atom
transitions to a lower energy state rather than pre-existing inside the atom.

Historically, beta decay presented a serious puzzle: if only two bodies emerged from the
decay (the recoiling nucleus and the electron), conservation of energy and momentum would
require the electron to carry a single, fixed kinetic energy for a given parent-daughter
nucleus pair, since a two-body decay has only one possible energy split. But measured
electron energies from beta decay instead form a continuous spectrum ranging up to that
maximum value, seemingly violating energy conservation. In 1930, Wolfgang Pauli proposed a
way out: a third, extremely weakly interacting and nearly massless particle — later named the
neutrino by Fermi — is emitted alongside the electron, sharing the available energy and
momentum between the two of them in a way that varies from decay to decay, which naturally
produces a continuous electron spectrum while still conserving energy overall. This was
initially a bold, largely unverifiable hypothesis; the neutrino wasn't directly detected until
1956, via the Cowan-Reines experiment.

Enrico Fermi built a full quantitative theory of beta decay in 1934, treating it with what is
now recognized as an early application of Fermi's golden rule: the decay rate is proportional
to the square of a weak-interaction coupling constant (analogous to how the fine-structure
constant governs electromagnetic interaction strength) and to the density of available final
states, correctly predicting the shape of the observed electron energy spectrum. This theory
was later understood as a low-energy effective description of the full electroweak theory, in
which beta decay proceeds through the exchange of a virtual W boson, one of the weak force's
carrier particles — Fermi's original four-point contact interaction is recovered as the limit
of W-boson exchange when the W boson's mass is far larger than the energies involved, which is
always the case in ordinary nuclear beta decay.
