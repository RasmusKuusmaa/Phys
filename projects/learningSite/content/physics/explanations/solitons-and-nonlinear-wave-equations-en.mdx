An ordinary wave pulse propagating through a linear, dispersive medium gradually spreads out and
flattens over time and distance, because the pulse is really a superposition of many different
frequency components, and in a dispersive medium different frequencies travel at slightly
different speeds — the pulse's initially sharp shape smears out as these components drift apart.
In certain nonlinear media, however, a special class of self-reinforcing wave, the soliton, can
propagate over remarkably long distances with its shape essentially unchanged, a genuinely
nonlinear phenomenon with no counterpart in ordinary linear wave theory.

A soliton's stability arises from a precise, dynamical balance between two competing effects.
Nonlinearity in the medium — meaning the wave's own amplitude affects how it propagates, rather
than simply superposing linearly — tends, on its own, to progressively steepen a pulse's leading
edge, eventually causing it to break (the same basic mechanism, in a different physical context,
that causes ocean waves to break as they approach shore). Dispersion, on its own, tends to spread
and flatten a pulse, exactly as it does for an ordinary linear wave. A soliton exists precisely in
the regime where these two competing tendencies exactly cancel each other, producing a pulse shape
that propagates indefinitely without either steepening into a shock or dispersing into a spread-out
wave train. Solitons arise as exact solutions to specific nonlinear wave equations (such as the
Korteweg-de Vries equation, originally derived to describe shallow-water waves, and the nonlinear
Schrodinger equation, relevant to optical and other contexts) that combine a nonlinear term with a
dispersive term in exactly the way needed for this balance to be possible.

A particularly striking property, and the origin of the name "soliton" (using the particle-like
"-on" suffix, as in "electron" or "photon"), is how solitons interact with each other: when two
solitons collide or pass through one another, they emerge afterward with their original shape and
speed essentially completely intact, behaving in this specific respect more like idealized,
non-interacting particles than like ordinary overlapping wave pulses, which would generally show
some lasting distortion from the interaction. Solitons were first directly observed as a genuine
physical phenomenon by John Scott Russell in 1834, watching a solitary water wave propagate for
miles along a canal without changing shape, and physically realized solitons are now well
established and technologically significant across multiple fields, including optical solitons
used in certain long-distance fiber-optic communication systems, where the precise balance between
the fiber's nonlinearity and its dispersion allows a light pulse carrying a data signal to
propagate over great distances without spreading out and blurring together with neighbouring
pulses.
