An undamped pendulum swings forever, and how far it swings depends entirely on
how hard you started it: its phase portrait is a family of nested closed loops,
one through every point. A self-sustained oscillator behaves quite differently.
Start a violin string with a heavy bow stroke or a light one, start an electronic
oscillator with a large disturbance or a tiny one, and after a while all of them
run at the same amplitude. In the phase plane that means one isolated closed
loop, with everything around it spiralling in: a limit cycle.

Isolation is the whole distinction. Around a limit cycle there are no other
closed orbits, so the loop is a state the system chooses rather than one it
merely remembers. That requires nonlinearity and a steady supply of energy — over
one turn the system takes energy in while the amplitude is small and dissipates
it while the amplitude is large, and the balance point is the cycle. The bow
feeds the string at a constant speed, the pump current feeds the laser at a
constant rate, and neither of them oscillates: the rhythm is manufactured
internally. This is also the natural continuation of a Hopf bifurcation, where a
fixed point loses stability and hands it to exactly such a loop.

Reading closed loops off a phase portrait gets hard as soon as the space has more
than two dimensions, which is where the Poincaré section earns its place. Choose
a surface the flow crosses transversally, ignore the trajectory entirely, and
mark only the points where it pierces that surface going one way. A periodic
motion pierces the same place every period, so it reduces to a single dot; a
motion with two incommensurable frequencies fills a closed curve; and a chaotic
motion leaves a cloud that on closer inspection has fine, repeating structure
rather than being smeared at random. Turning a continuous flow into a discrete
map this way also connects the subject back to iterated maps like the logistic
map, which is where period doubling and the route to chaos are easiest to see.
