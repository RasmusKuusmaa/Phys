Underneath the many different-looking wave phenomena — a plucked string, a
sound pulse, ripples on water, an electromagnetic pulse in vacuum — sits one
common mathematical structure: the wave equation, relating how a disturbance
curves in space to how it curves in time, with a single constant, the wave
speed, connecting the two. Any function that satisfies this equation describes
a disturbance that propagates without changing shape, travelling at that fixed
speed. The equation does not care what physical quantity is doing the
disturbing — displacement of a string, pressure in air, electric field
strength in vacuum — which is exactly why the same mathematics describes such
different physical situations.

What the wave equation is *not* is a description that fits after the fact to
any arbitrary disturbance. Solving it derives the wave speed from properties
of the medium alone — for a stretched string, from its tension and its mass
per unit length; for sound in a gas, from the gas's pressure and density; for
light, from the vacuum permittivity and permeability that appear in Maxwell's
equations. The speed is a property of the medium (or, for light, of empty
space itself), fixed before any particular wave travels through it — not
something a taller, louder, or higher-frequency wave can change. A big pulse
and a small ripple on the same string travel at exactly the same speed,
because that speed comes from the string, not from the pulse.

Recognising the same equation across mechanics, acoustics and electromagnetism
is what let physicists carry intuition and solution techniques wholesale from
one field to another, and it is the mathematical fact underneath Maxwell's own
discovery that light is an electromagnetic wave: solving his four field
equations together produced exactly this wave equation, with a speed
computable from two purely electrical and magnetic constants that turned out,
numerically, to equal the already-measured speed of light.
