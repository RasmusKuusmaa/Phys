Despite the name, cosmic rays are not electromagnetic radiation but overwhelmingly charged
particles — mostly protons and heavier atomic nuclei stripped of their electrons, with a smaller
admixture of electrons and other particles — arriving at Earth from astrophysical sources
throughout the galaxy and, for the highest-energy events, potentially beyond it. Their electric
charge has an important consequence: unlike photons, which travel in straight lines from their
source, charged cosmic ray particles are deflected by the galaxy's pervasive magnetic fields as
they travel, scrambling their arrival direction and making it generally impossible to trace a
detected cosmic ray's path back to identify its specific source, except statistically or for the
very highest-energy events where deflection is comparatively small.

Diffusive shock acceleration at supernova remnants is the leading explanation for the bulk of
galactic cosmic rays: charged particles trapped near a supernova remnant's expanding shock front
repeatedly cross back and forth across the shock, gaining a small energy boost with each
crossing through interactions with the turbulent magnetic field on either side, a process that
can efficiently accelerate particles up to a characteristic maximum energy set by the
remnant's size, magnetic field strength, and how long the shock persists. This mechanism
plausibly explains the observed cosmic ray energy spectrum up to that characteristic energy, but
falls well short of explaining the rare, most extreme events observed at ultra-high energies far
beyond what any known galactic accelerator could plausibly produce — the specific astrophysical
sources and acceleration mechanisms capable of reaching these ultra-high energies remain a
genuine open problem in astroparticle physics, with candidate sources including active galactic
nuclei and other extreme extragalactic environments.

The highest-energy cosmic rays face an additional physical constraint on how far they can have
traveled: a sufficiently energetic proton interacts with photons of the cosmic microwave
background itself as it propagates through intergalactic space, losing energy through pion
production once its energy exceeds a specific threshold (the Greisen-Zatsepin-Kuzmin, or GZK,
effect). This interaction imposes a theoretical horizon of roughly a hundred megaparsecs — the
"GZK cutoff" — beyond which an ultra-high-energy cosmic ray detected at Earth cannot realistically
have originated, since it would have lost most of its energy to this interaction en route,
meaning the very highest-energy cosmic rays observed must come from astrophysical sources that
are, in cosmic terms, relatively close by.
