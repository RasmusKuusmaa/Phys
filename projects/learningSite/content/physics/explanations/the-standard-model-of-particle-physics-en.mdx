Through most of the twentieth century, particle physics discovered an
ever-growing list of "elementary" particles — hundreds of them by the
1960s — until it became clear that most were not elementary at all but
composites built from a much smaller set of truly fundamental constituents.
The Standard Model is the theory that organizes those genuine fundamentals
and the forces that act between them.

Matter particles fall into two families, both fermions (obeying the Pauli
exclusion principle). Quarks come in six types ("flavours": up, down,
charm, strange, top, bottom) and combine in groups of two or three, bound by
the strong force, to form composite particles called hadrons — a proton is
two up quarks and one down quark, a neutron is two down quarks and one up
quark. Quarks are never observed in isolation; the strong force between them
grows rather than shrinks with separation, so pulling a quark out of a
hadron costs so much energy that it instead creates new quark-antiquark
pairs from the vacuum, a phenomenon called confinement. Leptons are the
second matter family and include the electron along with its heavier
cousins the muon and tau, plus three corresponding neutrinos; unlike quarks,
leptons such as the electron are observed directly and individually and are
not subject to the strong force at all.

Forces between these matter particles are carried by a second class of
particles, bosons, one type for each interaction the Standard Model
describes: the photon carries the electromagnetic force, the W and Z bosons
carry the weak force (responsible for processes like beta decay), and the
gluon carries the strong force that binds quarks together inside hadrons and
indirectly binds protons and neutrons together inside nuclei. A separate
particle, the Higgs boson, is associated with the mechanism that gives many
Standard Model particles their mass. Gravity is notably absent from this
picture: no confirmed force-carrying particle for gravity has been found
within the Standard Model's framework, and unifying gravity with the other
three forces described by the Standard Model remains one of the major open
problems in physics.
