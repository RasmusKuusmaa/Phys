Hydrogen — one proton, one electron — is the one atom whose quantum mechanics
can be solved exactly, and it is the template for everything after it.
Solving it means finding the wavefunctions of an electron in the proton's
Coulomb attraction, and just as with a particle in a box, only certain
solutions fit. The allowed energies come out as −13.6 eV/n², reproducing the
spectral lines of hydrogen that had been measured for decades with no
explanation.

Notice the shape of that ladder. Because of the 1/n², the levels are not
evenly spaced: they crowd closer and closer together as n rises, converging
on zero, which is the energy of an electron just barely unbound. The first
jump, n = 1 to 2, costs 10.2 eV; the jump from n = 5 to 6 costs about 0.17 eV.
This is why hydrogen's spectral series look the way they do, bunching up
towards a series limit.

The solutions are labelled by three quantum numbers — n for the energy and
overall size, l for the shape of the angular distribution, m for its
orientation — and these are what generalise to every other element. What
they describe are orbitals, and an orbital is not a path. There is no
trajectory, no little planet circling; there is a standing wave, and |ψ|²
gives the probability of finding the electron in each region. The familiar
lobed p-orbital pictures are surfaces enclosing most of that probability,
not routes. Add spin and the exclusion principle and these same orbitals,
filled in order, produce the whole structure of the periodic table.
