Not every microstate a system could occupy is equally likely at a given
temperature — a microstate with very high energy is exponentially less
probable than a low-energy one, governed by the Boltzmann factor e^(−E/kT).
The partition function, Z, is simply the sum of this factor over every
accessible microstate: Z = Σ e^(−Eᵢ/kT). It is a single number (for a fixed
temperature) that packages, in one place, how the system's energy levels are
populated at that temperature — hence "partition": it describes how
probability is partitioned among the available states.

Z on its own is not directly a physical quantity you would measure, and it
does not hand you the energy or the entropy without further work. Its real
power is as a generating function: taking the appropriate derivative of ln Z
with respect to temperature yields the average energy; further
manipulation yields entropy, free energy, heat capacity, essentially every
thermodynamic quantity of interest, all derived from this one function rather
than computed separately from first principles each time. Once Z(T) is known
for a system, the rest of its thermodynamics follows by calculus rather than
by re-deriving each quantity from scratch.

This is what makes the partition function the central object of statistical
mechanics rather than a mere bookkeeping tool: it is the bridge between the
microscopic description (the energy levels a system's particles can occupy)
and the macroscopic, measurable thermodynamic quantities the older,
purely classical theory of heat could only describe empirically. Different
physical systems — an ideal gas, a solid's vibrating atoms, a magnet's spins
— have different partition functions, but the same machinery for extracting
thermodynamics from Z applies uniformly to all of them.
