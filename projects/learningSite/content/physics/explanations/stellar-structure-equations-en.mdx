Four coupled differential equations, integrated from the star's centre out to its surface,
determine a star's internal structure. Hydrostatic equilibrium balances the crushing weight
of every overlying layer against the outward push of pressure at each radius, exactly the
condition that keeps the star from either collapsing under its own gravity or blowing itself
apart. Mass continuity tracks how the enclosed mass grows as you move outward through shells
of the star's density profile.

Energy generation tracks how the star's luminosity (the rate of energy flow through a given
radius) builds up as you move outward through shells that are actively fusing hydrogen or
heavier elements, adding energy locally wherever nuclear reactions are occurring; outside the
fusing core, luminosity stays constant with radius, since no more energy is being added.
Energy transport describes how that energy actually moves outward — either by radiation
(photons diffusing outward through the stellar material) or, where radiation alone can't
carry the required flux, by convection (bulk motion of hot material rising and cool material
sinking), whichever mechanism is actually more efficient at each radius.

Together with an equation of state (relating pressure, density and temperature for the
stellar material) and a specification of the star's total mass and chemical composition,
these four equations, in principle, completely determine the star's structure — its central
temperature and pressure, its radius, its surface temperature and luminosity — without any
additional free parameters. This is why a star's mass and composition alone are usually
enough to predict essentially all of its other properties, the physical basis for the
empirical mass-luminosity relation and for the main sequence's position on the
Hertzsprung-Russell diagram.
