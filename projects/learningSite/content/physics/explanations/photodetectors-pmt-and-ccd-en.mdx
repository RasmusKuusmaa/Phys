A single photon carries so little energy that the one electron it can knock loose via the photoelectric effect is far too small a signal to measure directly — you need some way to turn one electron into a burst of billions. A photomultiplier tube does exactly that with a chain of electrodes called dynodes, held at successively higher voltages. The photocathode releases one electron per absorbed photon; that electron accelerates toward the first dynode and, on impact, knocks loose several secondary electrons of its own. Each of those accelerates to the next dynode and repeats the process. With ten dynode stages and a modest gain of four secondary electrons per stage, the single original electron becomes roughly a million by the time it reaches the anode — an exponential cascade, not an additive one, because the multiplication compounds at every stage rather than just adding up.

A CCD image sensor uses the same underlying photoelectric effect but spreads it out instead of amplifying it. Each pixel is a tiny region of semiconductor where an absorbed photon frees a charge carrier, and that charge simply accumulates in place for the whole exposure rather than being multiplied. After the exposure, the accumulated charge from each pixel is shifted, row by row, into a readout register that converts it to a digital value — this is the "charge-coupled" part of the name, referring to the way charge is passed from one pixel to the next during readout rather than read directly in place.

Neither device tells you what colour hit it. A photomultiplier's single output current says nothing about the photon's wavelength — only how many photons arrived, weighted by how efficiently the photocathode responds at that wavelength. A CCD pixel's accumulated charge is exactly the same: proportional to how much light hit it, with no colour information in the charge itself. Colour cameras get around this by putting a different colour filter over each pixel (or by taking multiple exposures through different filters), then combining the results — the sensor itself is colour-blind either way.
