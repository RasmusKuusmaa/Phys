A molecule in a gas travels in a straight line until it hits another one. How far
that is, on average, depends on how much room there is: the mean free path grows
as the gas thins out, in inverse proportion to pressure. In room air it is about
70 nanometres — a molecule collides billions of times a second — while in a good
ultra-high vacuum it can exceed the size of the building. The molecules
themselves are no slower; at a fixed temperature they still move at hundreds of
metres per second. There are simply far fewer of them to run into.

What makes the number useful is comparing it with the apparatus rather than
reading it on its own. While the mean free path is much smaller than the chamber,
molecules mostly talk to each other rather than to the walls, the gas behaves as
a continuous fluid, and a pump works by pushing that fluid along — this is the
viscous regime, where a rotary or scroll pump belongs. Once the mean free path is
much larger, molecules fly from wall to wall and essentially never meet, so
pushing them is meaningless and the pump must instead catch each molecule that
happens to arrive: that is the free-molecular regime and the province of turbo
and cryo pumps. A vacuum system therefore needs both in series, which is why
there is always a backing pump behind the fast one.

Two practical consequences fall out. First, thermal conduction through a gas is
roughly independent of pressure while the mean free path is short, because
removing half the molecules leaves each of the rest carrying heat twice as far —
the losses only collapse once the path exceeds the gap, which is exactly the
condition a vacuum flask is built to reach. Second, in thin-film deposition the
mean free path must be longer than the distance from source to substrate, or the
evaporated atoms scatter on the way and arrive from every direction instead of
in a straight line. Both are the same comparison, made against a different length
each time.
