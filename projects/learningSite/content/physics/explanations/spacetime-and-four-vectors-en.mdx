Once the relativity of simultaneity shows that space and time separations
between events are not independently absolute, it becomes natural to stop
treating space and time as two separate three-plus-one quantities and
instead treat them as a single four-dimensional entity: spacetime, with
coordinates (t, x, y, z). An event — something that happens at a definite
place and a definite time — is a single point in this four-dimensional
space, and how its coordinates are split between "space" and "time" depends
on which inertial observer is doing the splitting, exactly as the relativity
of simultaneity requires.

A four-vector generalizes the familiar three-component vector by adding a
fourth, time-like component, and transforms between inertial frames
according to the Lorentz transformations rather than ordinary rotations. The
position four-vector (ct, x, y, z) is the basic example; the energy-momentum
four-vector (E/c, px, py, pz) is another, combining a particle's energy and
its three-dimensional momentum into a single object. Just as an ordinary
three-dimensional vector has a length that stays the same under rotation
even though its individual x, y, z components change, a four-vector has an
invariant "length" — for the position four-vector, this is the spacetime
interval, and for the energy-momentum four-vector, it directly reproduces the
relativistic relation between energy, momentum, and rest mass. This
invariant is the same number for every inertial observer, even though each
observer computes the individual components of the four-vector differently.

The practical payoff is that quantities built from an invariant four-vector
length are automatically true in every inertial frame — a huge simplification
compared with re-deriving results separately for the space part and the time
part, or separately for the momentum part and the energy part, in every new
frame. This is why the spacetime and four-vector formalism, rather than
treating space, time, momentum, and energy as unrelated classical quantities,
is the natural mathematical language of special relativity, and it extends
directly into general relativity's treatment of spacetime as a
four-dimensional geometric object in its own right.
