The liquid-drop model treats the nucleus, in analogy with a drop of incompressible charged
liquid, as held together by a short-range attractive force between neighbouring nucleons
(analogous to intermolecular forces) opposed by the long-range electrostatic repulsion between
protons. This analogy is remarkably productive: nucleons deep inside the nucleus are
surrounded on all sides by neighbours pulling on them (like molecules deep inside a liquid),
while nucleons at the surface have fewer neighbours and are less tightly bound (like surface
tension in a liquid drop), giving both a volume term and a surface term in the total binding
energy.

The semi-empirical (Weizsäcker) mass formula assembles five such physically motivated terms:
a volume term proportional to the total nucleon number A (since the short-range force means
each nucleon contributes roughly equally, regardless of the nucleus's overall size); a surface
term, negative and proportional to the nuclear surface area, penalizing nucleons at the
surface for having fewer neighbours; a Coulomb term, negative and growing with the number of
proton pairs, capturing the electrostatic repulsion between all protons; an asymmetry term,
negative and growing with the imbalance between proton and neutron numbers, reflecting the
Pauli exclusion principle's preference for filling proton and neutron energy levels evenly;
and a pairing term that gives extra binding to nuclei with even numbers of protons and
neutrons, reflecting a residual attractive interaction between like nucleons in the same
state.

The volume and surface terms both scale with the nuclear size, but the volume term dominates
for larger nuclei since volume grows faster than surface area — this is why binding energy
per nucleon initially rises with nucleon number. But the Coulomb term grows with the number of
proton pairs even faster, since it scales with the square of the number of protons roughly,
and eventually overtakes the volume term's advantage, causing binding energy per nucleon to
peak around iron and nickel (mass number around 56-62) and decline for heavier nuclei. This is
precisely why heavy nuclei like uranium are less tightly bound than medium-mass nuclei, why
splitting a heavy nucleus into two medium-mass fragments releases energy (fission), and why
fusing very light nuclei into medium-mass ones also releases energy (fusion) — both processes
move nuclei toward the binding-energy peak the semi-empirical mass formula explains.
