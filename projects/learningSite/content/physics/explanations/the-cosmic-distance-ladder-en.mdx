No single technique can measure distances across the full range astronomers care about, from
nearby stars to the most distant observable galaxies, so the cosmic distance ladder builds up
distance measurements as a sequence of overlapping methods, each rung calibrated against the
one below it. The base of the ladder is geometric parallax: as Earth orbits the Sun, a nearby
star appears to shift slightly against the background of much more distant stars, and that
shift's angle, combined with simple trigonometry and the known size of Earth's orbit, gives a
direct, essentially assumption-free distance measurement. Parallax only works for relatively
nearby stars, however, since the parallax angle shrinks below what can be measured once a star
is far enough away.

The next rungs rely on standard candles: objects whose intrinsic (absolute) luminosity — how
bright they actually are, not how bright they appear — is known or can be inferred from some
other observable property. Cepheid variable stars, whose pulsation period is tightly related to
their intrinsic luminosity, are calibrated using parallax measurements of nearby Cepheids, and
once calibrated, a Cepheid's observed apparent brightness combined with its known intrinsic
luminosity (via the inverse-square law) gives its distance directly, extending measurements to
galaxies too distant for parallax to reach at all. Type Ia supernovae, whose peak brightness is
remarkably consistent because they detonate near the Chandrasekhar mass limit, extend the ladder
further still, calibrated in turn against galaxies whose distance is independently known from
Cepheids, reaching distances far beyond what any individual galaxy's Cepheid population could
resolve.

Because each rung's calibration depends entirely on the rung beneath it, a systematic error
anywhere in the ladder does not stay isolated to that one rung — it propagates upward,
silently distorting every distance measurement built using rungs above it. This is precisely why
resolving current tensions in cosmology, most notably the disagreement between different methods
for measuring the Hubble constant (the local distance-ladder value versus the value inferred from
the cosmic microwave background), requires painstaking, independent cross-checks of every
individual rung's calibration rather than assuming the discrepancy must originate from whichever
single rung happens to be under the most scrutiny at any given time.
