Push a swing at random moments and little happens; push it once per cycle,
at the right point in the cycle, and it climbs higher every time. That is
resonance, and the important part is that the *size* of the push is almost
irrelevant. What matters is timing. When the driving frequency matches the
oscillator's natural frequency, every push arrives in phase with the motion
and adds energy, and a tiny force can build an enormous amplitude — limited
only by how fast damping bleeds energy away.

Off resonance, the oscillator does not simply respond less; it responds
*late*. Drive it slowly and it follows the force almost exactly. Drive it
fast and it lags by half a cycle, moving one way while the force pushes the
other. Right at resonance the lag is a quarter cycle — which is precisely
what makes the force push hardest when the oscillator is moving fastest, and
so pump in the most energy per cycle.

Coupling two oscillators does something related. Linked by a spring or a
shared support, they stop having independent frequencies and acquire shared
patterns of motion — normal modes — in which the whole system oscillates at
one frequency. Two identical pendulums on a common bar have a mode where
they swing together and one where they swing oppositely, at slightly
different frequencies. Start one alone and you have excited both modes at
once; they drift in and out of step, and the visible result is energy
sloshing from one pendulum to the other and back.
