Water does not pile up inside a pipe and it does not vanish. If ten litres
enter one end each second, ten litres must leave the other end each second,
and ten litres must cross every cross-section in between. That is the
continuity equation, and written out it says A·v is the same everywhere
along the pipe: cross-sectional area times flow speed.

The immediate consequence runs against intuition. Squeeze the pipe and the
fluid speeds up, because the same volume per second now has to get through a
smaller opening. Halve the area and the speed doubles. Everyone has used
this — putting a thumb over the end of a garden hose does not reduce the
water delivered, it accelerates the same flow into a jet. The narrowing
constrains the *area*, not the volume passing per second.

Keeping flow rate and speed distinct is the other half of the idea. Flow
rate is a volume per second, measured in cubic metres per second; speed is a
distance per second. A wide slow river and a narrow fast stream can carry
identical flow rates. Continuity says nothing about how fast the fluid must
move — only that whatever the speed and area do, their product holds
constant, provided the fluid is incompressible and nothing leaks out along
the way.
