Charge conjugation (C) swaps every particle for its antiparticle; parity (P) mirror-reflects
space. Parity alone was shown to be violated by the weak interaction in 1956-57, in the famous
experiment by Chien-Shiung Wu observing beta decay from polarized cobalt-60 nuclei, which found
that emitted electrons preferentially emerged in a direction correlated with the nuclear spin in
a way a mirror-image process would not reproduce. This left open the possibility that the
combined operation CP — applying both C and P together — might still be an exact symmetry, even
though P alone was not. In 1964, experiments with neutral kaons found measurable CP violation as
well: a specific kaon decay mode occurred at a rate too small to be zero but inconsistent with
exact CP symmetry, demonstrating that even this combined symmetry is not respected exactly by the
weak interaction.

Within the Standard Model, the mechanism behind CP violation lives in the Cabibbo-Kobayashi-
Maskawa (CKM) matrix, which describes how the weak interaction mixes the different quark flavours
(the fact that a quark's weak-interaction partner is not necessarily the same as its mass
eigenstate partner). With three generations of quarks, this matrix necessarily contains one
irreducible complex phase that cannot be removed by any choice of convention, and it is precisely
this complex phase that is the Standard Model's built-in source of CP violation — a
mathematically necessary consequence of having (at least) three quark generations rather than
just two, for which no such irreducible phase would exist.

CP violation matters well beyond kaon and B-meson physics because it is one of three necessary
conditions (the Sakharov conditions, alongside baryon number violation and departure from thermal
equilibrium) that any successful theory of baryogenesis — explaining why the observable universe
contains overwhelmingly more matter than antimatter, despite the Big Bang presumably producing
both in equal amounts — must include. However, the amount of CP violation actually measured in
quark physics, while real and precisely confirmed by decades of kaon and B-meson experiments, is
quantitatively far too small, by many orders of magnitude, to account for the observed cosmic
matter-antimatter asymmetry on its own. This shortfall is a genuine, unresolved problem, actively
motivating searches for additional sources of CP violation beyond the Standard Model, including in
the neutrino sector (where an analogous complex phase in the neutrino mixing matrix is being
actively measured) and in physics beyond the Standard Model more broadly.
