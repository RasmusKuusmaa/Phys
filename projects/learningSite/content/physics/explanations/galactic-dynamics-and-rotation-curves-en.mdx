For a star orbiting at radius r in circular orbit around a galaxy, Newtonian gravity
relates its orbital speed directly to the total mass enclosed within that radius: if
nearly all the mass were concentrated toward the centre (as the visible starlight
suggests), orbital speed should fall off with increasing radius, the same Keplerian
decline that governs planets in the solar system. Measuring actual orbital speeds — from
the Doppler shift of light or radio emission from stars and gas at different radii — gives
a galaxy's rotation curve directly, a plot of orbital speed against distance from the
centre.

Beginning with Vera Rubin's systematic observations in the 1970s, essentially every spiral
galaxy measured shows a rotation curve that stays roughly flat well beyond the edge of the
visible, luminous disk, rather than declining the way Newtonian gravity predicts if the
visible matter were all there is. A flat rotation curve at large radius requires the
enclosed mass to keep growing roughly linearly with radius out to very large distances, far
beyond where the visible stars and gas actually end — mass that emits no detectable light at
any wavelength, distributed in a roughly spherical halo extending well past the visible
disk.

This is the single most direct and widely reproduced piece of observational evidence for
dark matter: it doesn't rely on any assumption about what dark matter actually is, only that
Newtonian gravity (an extremely well-tested theory in every other regime) correctly
predicts orbital speed from enclosed mass, and that the enclosed mass inferred from rotation
curves is consistently, dramatically larger than the mass visible as stars and gas.
Alternative theories that modify gravity itself instead of positing unseen mass (such as
MOND) can also reproduce flat rotation curves for individual galaxies, but dark matter
remains the standard explanation because it also correctly accounts for numerous other
independent lines of evidence — galaxy cluster dynamics, gravitational lensing, and the
detailed structure of the cosmic microwave background — that a pure modified-gravity theory
struggles to explain simultaneously.
