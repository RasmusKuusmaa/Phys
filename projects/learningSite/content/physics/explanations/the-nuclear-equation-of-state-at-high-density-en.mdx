The nuclear equation of state describes how nuclear matter's pressure depends on its density
and composition (the relative proportion of neutrons and protons). Near ordinary nuclear
saturation density — the density found inside stable atomic nuclei — the equation of state is
well constrained by decades of laboratory measurements of nuclear masses, radii, and
scattering experiments involving stable nuclei, giving physicists solid empirical grounding for
how nuclear matter behaves under these comparatively familiar conditions.

Deep inside a neutron star's core, however, densities reach several times ordinary nuclear
density, conditions that cannot be recreated in any static laboratory setting and that probe
aspects of the strong nuclear force's short-range behaviour that remain genuinely uncertain.
Different theoretical models for the equation of state at these extreme densities — built from
different assumptions about how nucleons interact at short range — predict measurably different
outcomes: some allow larger maximum neutron star masses and radii than others, and some predict
the appearance of exotic matter phases beyond ordinary neutrons and protons, including hyperons
(baryons containing one or more strange quarks, which can become energetically favorable to
produce at sufficiently high density) or even a transition to deconfined quark matter, where
individual nucleons cease to be well-defined objects and quarks move comparatively freely. Which,
if any, of these exotic phases actually appears inside a real neutron star's core remains a
genuinely open theoretical question, not a settled matter of ordinary nucleonic matter simply
continuing unchanged to arbitrarily high density.

This uncertainty is not purely academic, however, since it can be constrained by observation.
Different candidate equations of state predict different relationships between a neutron star's
mass and its radius, and different maximum possible masses (via the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff
equation), so precise astrophysical measurements of actual neutron star masses and radii —
including, increasingly, joint mass-radius measurements from X-ray timing and gravitational-wave
observations of neutron star mergers, which also constrain the equation of state through how
the star deforms tidally just before merger — directly rule out equation-of-state models
predicting properties inconsistent with what's actually observed. Combined with laboratory
heavy-ion collision experiments, which briefly recreate dense, hot nuclear matter (though at
higher temperature and different composition than a neutron star's core) on Earth, astrophysical
observation has become an essential, increasingly precise complementary tool alongside nuclear
theory for constraining how nuclear matter genuinely behaves at the highest densities found
anywhere in the universe.
