The uncertainty principle is often told as a story about clumsiness: to see
an electron you must bounce a photon off it, and the photon kicks it, so you
spoil the momentum while learning the position. The story is memorable and
it is wrong — or at least it describes a real effect that is not what the
principle is about. Heisenberg's limit survives no matter how gentle the
probe, because it is not about probing at all.

It is about waves. A wave with one sharp wavelength has to extend over all
space; the moment you confine it to a small region, you can only build that
localised shape by adding together many different wavelengths. This is
ordinary wave mathematics, true of sound and water waves as much as of
electrons. What quantum mechanics adds is de Broglie's link between
wavelength and momentum. Position spread and momentum spread are then two
faces of the same wave, and Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2 is a theorem about it.

So the electron does not secretly possess an exact position and an exact
momentum that we are prevented from reading. It has a wavefunction, and a
wavefunction narrow in position is *made of* a wide range of momenta. Note
also what the principle does not say: any single quantity can be measured as
precisely as you like. The trade-off applies only to particular pairs —
position with momentum, energy with time — and it is why an electron
confined to an atom cannot simply sit still at the nucleus, since squeezing
its position would cost more kinetic energy than the binding provides.
