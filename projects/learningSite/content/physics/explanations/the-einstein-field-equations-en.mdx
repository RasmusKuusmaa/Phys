The Einstein field equations set a specific measure of spacetime curvature built from the
metric tensor (the Einstein tensor, which packages the Ricci curvature tensor and the metric
itself) equal to the stress-energy tensor, which describes the density and flow of mass,
energy, momentum and pressure at every point. Unlike Newton's law of gravity, which is one
equation for a scalar potential, the field equations are actually ten coupled, nonlinear
partial differential equations (for the ten independent components of the symmetric metric
tensor in four dimensions), making them vastly harder to solve in general.

The equation's two sides capture Wheeler's famous summary exactly: the left side (the
Einstein tensor) is built purely from the geometry of spacetime — how it's curved; the right
side (the stress-energy tensor) is built purely from the matter and energy present — how
much of it, and how it's moving. The field equations say these two are locked together:
matter and energy directly determine spacetime's curvature, and (via the geodesic equation)
that curvature in turn determines how matter and energy move through it, a fully two-way,
self-consistent coupling rather than one thing simply causing the other in one direction
only.

Because the equations are nonlinear, they don't obey superposition the way Newtonian
gravity's sourced potential does — you can't just add together the gravitational fields of
two masses independently and expect the correct combined result, since the curvature itself
gravitates and contributes back to the total curvature. This nonlinearity is what makes the
equations so difficult to solve exactly (only a small number of exact solutions are known,
each for a highly symmetric matter distribution — a single non-rotating mass, an idealized
uniform cosmological fluid) and is also directly responsible for genuinely nonlinear
phenomena with no Newtonian counterpart at all, such as gravitational waves carrying energy
away from a source.
