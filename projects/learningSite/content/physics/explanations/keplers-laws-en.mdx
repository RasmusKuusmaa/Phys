Kepler found his three laws by fitting decades of naked-eye observations of
planetary positions, decades before Newton had any theory of gravity to
explain them. The first law says orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one
focus, not circles — a genuine break from millennia of assumed circular
motion. The second says a line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal
areas in equal times, which forces a planet to move fastest when nearest the
Sun (at perihelion) and slowest when farthest (at aphelion) — an ellipse
traversed at constant speed would violate this law. The third relates
different planets to each other: the square of the orbital period is
proportional to the cube of the orbit's size, T² ∝ r³, so a planet twice as
far out does not orbit twice as slowly but nearly three times as slowly.

These were empirical regularities, discovered with no underlying mechanism —
Kepler had found the pattern without knowing why nature followed it. Newton's
achievement, decades later, was showing that all three laws fall out
automatically from a single assumption: gravity pulls with a force
proportional to 1/r². Elliptical orbits, the area law, and the period-radius
relationship are not three separate facts about the solar system; they are
three consequences of one force law, derivable together from Newton's
mechanics rather than requiring three separate empirical rules.

This is one of the clearest examples in the history of physics of empirical
law preceding — and then being explained by — physical theory, and it is
exactly the same trajectory Newtonian gravitation itself would later follow
when general relativity explained *why* mass produces that particular
inverse-square attraction in the first place.
