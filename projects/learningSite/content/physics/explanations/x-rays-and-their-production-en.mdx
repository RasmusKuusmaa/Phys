X-rays are commonly produced in an X-ray tube: electrons are accelerated
through a large voltage and slammed into a solid metal target. The resulting
spectrum of emitted radiation has two distinct components, produced by two
different physical mechanisms, and telling them apart is the key to
understanding X-ray production.

The first component is a smooth, continuous spectrum called bremsstrahlung,
German for "braking radiation." As an incoming electron passes close to a
target atom's nucleus, the nucleus's electric field deflects and decelerates
it, and an accelerating (or decelerating) charge radiates electromagnetic
energy. Because each electron can lose anywhere from a small fraction to
almost all of its kinetic energy in a single such deflection, depending on
how closely it passes the nucleus, the resulting photon energies span a
continuous range up to a sharp maximum set by the electron's full kinetic
energy — a photon carrying away all of an electron's energy in one
deceleration event sets the shortest possible wavelength in the spectrum.

The second component consists of sharp, narrow peaks called characteristic
X-rays, superimposed on the continuous background. These arise from a
different process entirely: a sufficiently energetic incoming electron can
knock an electron out of an inner shell of a target atom, leaving a vacancy.
An electron from a higher shell then drops down to fill that vacancy,
emitting a photon whose energy equals the difference between the two atomic
energy levels involved — exactly analogous to the discrete-line emission
that produces atomic spectra generally, but here the transition is between
the innermost, most tightly bound shells. Because these energy-level
spacings are set entirely by the target element's own atomic structure,
characteristic X-ray line energies are a fingerprint of the target material,
independent of the bombarding electrons' exact energy (so long as it exceeds
the threshold needed to eject the inner-shell electron in the first place) —
this fingerprint is the basis of X-ray fluorescence techniques used to
identify unknown materials.
