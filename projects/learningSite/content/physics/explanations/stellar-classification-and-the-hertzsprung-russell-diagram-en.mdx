A star's colour is a direct, visible consequence of its surface temperature,
via the same blackbody radiation principle that governs any hot, glowing
object: a hotter surface radiates more strongly toward shorter wavelengths,
shifting its overall colour from red (cooler) through yellow and white to
blue (hotter). Astronomers use this relationship to classify stars into
spectral types, ordered from hottest to coolest, and quantify a star's
brightness independent of its distance from Earth using luminosity, the
total power the star radiates. Plotting a large sample of stars with
temperature (or equivalently spectral type) along one axis and luminosity
along the other produces the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram, one of the most
information-dense and widely used diagrams in astrophysics.

The striking feature of this diagram, once enough stars are plotted, is
that stars do not scatter randomly across it. The large majority fall
along a single, fairly narrow diagonal band running from hot, highly
luminous stars in the upper left to cool, dim stars in the lower right,
called the main sequence. This pattern is not a coincidence: a star spends
the great majority of its life fusing hydrogen into helium in its core, and
for a star in this stable, hydrogen-burning phase, both its surface
temperature and its luminosity are set almost entirely by a single
underlying variable, its mass — a more massive star has a hotter, more
luminous core, burns hotter at its surface, and shines with far greater
total luminosity than a lower-mass star, producing the tight, orderly band
seen on the diagram. The Sun, a fairly ordinary, middling-mass star,
sits roughly in the middle of the main sequence.

Not every star lies on the main sequence, and the diagram's other regions
correspond to distinct evolutionary stages away from stable hydrogen
burning. Once a star exhausts the hydrogen fuel in its core, it evolves off
the main sequence: its outer layers expand and cool while its luminosity
can actually increase, moving it into the upper-right region of the
diagram populated by giant and supergiant stars — larger, cooler, but very
luminous due to their enormous surface area. Later still, many stars end
their lives as white dwarfs, small, extremely hot, faint stellar remnants
that occupy the lower-left region of the diagram — hot in surface
temperature but dim because of their tiny radiating area. Because a star's
position on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram reflects both its mass and its
evolutionary stage, the diagram serves simultaneously as a tool for
classifying stars observed today and for reconstructing the life story of
stars in general.
