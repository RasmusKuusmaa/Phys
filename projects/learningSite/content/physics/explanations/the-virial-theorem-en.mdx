For a bound system of particles interacting through forces that follow a power-law relationship
with distance (force proportional to distance raised to some exponent n), the virial theorem
relates the system's time-averaged kinetic energy to its time-averaged potential energy, without
requiring detailed knowledge of any individual particle's trajectory. The theorem follows from a
general argument about the long-time average of the rate of change of a quantity called the
virial (related to the sum of each particle's momentum dotted with its position), which must
vanish on average for any bound system that remains in a statistically steady state indefinitely
— from this single, quite general condition, the specific relationship between average kinetic
and potential energy follows directly once the force law's power-law exponent is specified.

For an inverse-square force — exactly the force law governing both Newtonian gravity and Coulomb
electrostatic attraction — the virial theorem yields a strikingly simple, specific result: the
time-averaged kinetic energy equals exactly minus one-half the time-averaged potential energy.
This specific numerical factor is not universal to all force laws; a different power-law
exponent produces a different relationship (for instance, a simple harmonic oscillator's linear
restoring force yields average kinetic energy equal to average potential energy, not related by a
factor of minus one-half), so the well-known minus-one-half result is specifically tied to the
inverse-square case.

This relationship is put to direct, practical use throughout astrophysics to estimate the total
mass of gravitationally bound systems — star clusters, galaxies, and galaxy clusters — using only
observed velocities, without needing to trace any individual orbit. Since the system's kinetic
energy can be estimated directly from the observed spread (velocity dispersion) of its
constituent stars' or galaxies' velocities, and the virial theorem fixes the relationship between
that kinetic energy and the system's total gravitational potential energy (which itself depends
on the system's total mass and characteristic size), measuring the velocity spread and size
together yields a direct estimate of the system's total mass. This technique, applied to galaxy
clusters by Fritz Zwicky in the 1930s, was one of the earliest pieces of evidence for dark
matter: the total mass inferred from the virial theorem applied to observed galaxy velocities
substantially exceeded the mass visible in stars and gas alone, a discrepancy that remains one of
dark matter's most important lines of observational evidence.
