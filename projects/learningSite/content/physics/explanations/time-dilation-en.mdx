If the speed of light is the same for everyone, clocks cannot be. Picture a
clock that works by bouncing a light pulse between two mirrors, one tick per
round trip. Watch that clock fly past you and the pulse no longer travels
straight up and down — from your position it traces a zigzag, a longer path.
Since it covers that longer path at the same speed c, each tick takes longer.
The moving clock runs slow.

Nothing has gone wrong with the clock. The mechanism is untouched, and the
result does not depend on the clock being made of mirrors — a mechanical
watch, a decaying particle and a human heartbeat all slow by the same
factor, because it is the time between the events that has changed, not
the machinery marking it. Nor is it an artefact of signal delay: the effect
survives after you correct for how long the light took to reach you.

The effect is also symmetric, which is the part most worth sitting with.
Each observer measures the *other's* clock as the slow one, and neither is
mistaken, because "at the same time" means something different in each
frame. Cosmic-ray muons are the standard demonstration: they reach the
ground in numbers that their at-rest lifetime forbids, because in our frame
their internal clocks are stretched — while in their own frame it is the
atmosphere that is thin, not their lifetime that is long.
