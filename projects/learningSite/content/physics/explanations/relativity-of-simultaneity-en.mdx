In everyday experience, "at the same time" seems like an obvious, universal
fact: either two events happen simultaneously or they don't, and everyone
should agree on which. Special relativity shows this intuition is wrong once
the two postulates — the laws of physics are the same in every inertial
frame, and the speed of light in vacuum is the same for every inertial
observer — are taken seriously.

The classic illustration involves two events separated in space, such as
lightning striking both ends of a moving train. An observer standing exactly
midway between the two strike points, at rest relative to the train, sees
the light from both strikes arrive at the same instant and concludes the
strikes were simultaneous. An observer on the platform, watching the same
two strikes but moving relative to the train, is at the midpoint of the
strike locations only in the platform's own rest frame; because light
travels at the same fixed speed for this observer too, and because the
train observer's position is moving during the light's travel time, the
platform observer generally finds that light from one strike arrives before
light from the other, and concludes the strikes were not simultaneous. Both
observers are applying the same physics correctly and using the same
constant speed of light; they simply disagree about the timing of spatially
separated events, and neither is more "correct" than the other.

This is not a perceptual illusion or an artifact of not accounting for light
travel time — both observers have already corrected for light-travel delay
using their own frame's constant light speed, and they still disagree.
Simultaneity for spatially separated events is a frame-dependent notion, not
an absolute one, and this relativity of simultaneity is the conceptual root
from which effects like time dilation and length contraction can be derived
via the Lorentz transformations. The effect exists at every relative speed,
not only near the speed of light — it is simply too small to notice at
everyday speeds, growing to become dramatic and directly measurable only as
relative speeds approach the speed of light.
