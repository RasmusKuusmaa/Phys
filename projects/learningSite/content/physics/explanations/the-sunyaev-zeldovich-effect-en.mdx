The cosmic microwave background's photons have been traveling essentially undisturbed since recombination, but along the way, a small fraction of them pass directly through the hot, ionized intracluster medium filling a galaxy cluster. That gas is populated with high-energy free electrons, and when a microwave background photon scatters off one of these electrons, the electron (moving much faster than the photon's effective thermal speed) typically transfers some of its own kinetic energy to the photon, boosting it to slightly higher energy -- a process called inverse Compton scattering, the reverse of the more familiar Compton scattering where a photon loses energy to an electron.

Applied across an entire cluster's worth of hot electrons, this process has a very specific, calculable effect on the observed microwave background spectrum in that particular direction of sky: photons are systematically removed from the spectrum's lower-energy (longer-wavelength) side and added to its higher-energy (shorter-wavelength) side, producing a small but distinctive spectral distortion known as the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect, named for Rashid Sunyaev and Yakov Zel'dovich, who predicted it theoretically. Crucially, the size of this distortion depends only on the total amount of hot electron gas the photons passed through along the line of sight -- essentially the gas's density integrated along the path -- and not on how far away that gas is.

This distance-independence is what makes the effect such a valuable observational tool. An ordinary observation of a cluster's brightness, whether in visible light from its galaxies or in X-rays from its hot gas, necessarily dims with distance according to the standard inverse-square-law-like falloff, making the most distant clusters progressively harder to detect. The Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect's distortion strength does not share this limitation, since it depends on the gas actually present along the line of sight rather than on how much of the emitted light happens to reach a distant telescope. This lets astronomers survey and detect galaxy clusters, and estimate the mass of their hot gas directly, out to distances and epochs where X-ray emission has become far too faint to find clusters reliably, making the two techniques genuinely complementary tools for mapping the universe's large-scale structure across cosmic time.
