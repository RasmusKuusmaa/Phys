In vacuum, Maxwell's equations fix the speed of an electromagnetic wave at
exactly c. Inside a material, the wave still travels through the same
vacuum between atoms, but the atoms' electrons feel the oscillating field and
oscillate along with it, re-radiating their own electromagnetic waves that
lag slightly behind the driving field. The net effect of the original wave
plus this cloud of delayed re-radiation is a combined wave that appears to
travel slower than c — not because light itself has changed, but because the
material's response builds in a delay at every point along the way.

That slowdown is what the refractive index n measures: n = c/v, the factor by
which a material's electrons slow the wave's apparent progress. A higher
refractive index means a slower speed inside that material, full stop —
despite the word's association in casual usage with "bending", n is defined by
speed, and the bending of light at an interface (what most people picture
first) is itself a consequence of that speed change, not a separate fact.

One quantity survives the crossing into a medium unchanged: frequency. It is
set by whatever oscillates the wave in the first place — the source — and
every atom in the material that responds to the wave must oscillate at that
same driven frequency, so nothing downstream can alter it. Since v = fλ and v
drops while f holds fixed, wavelength must shorten to compensate — light
turning from blue to violet is a change of source, never something a medium
can do to a wave passing through it. This is the standard trap in numerical
problems: reach for "the wavelength changed, so the frequency must have too"
and the answer comes out wrong, because it is precisely the frequency that
cannot change.
