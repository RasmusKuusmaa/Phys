Newtonian mechanics asks you to identify every force, including the ones
that exist only to hold the system together — the normal force from a track,
the tension in a rigid rod, the reaction at a hinge. These constraint forces
are usually unknown at the start, do no work, and are of no interest in the
answer, yet you have to solve for them anyway. Lagrangian mechanics is a
reformulation that lets you skip them entirely.

The move is to describe the system with as many coordinates as it has genuine
freedoms, and no more: one angle for a pendulum, one distance along the track
for a bead. Then form the Lagrangian, L = T − V, kinetic energy *minus*
potential energy. The minus sign matters and is the most common slip — L is
not the total energy. Feeding L through the Euler–Lagrange equation produces
one equation of motion per coordinate, with the constraint forces already
gone.

This is not a rival theory. For any problem both methods can handle, they
give identical motion, because the Lagrangian formulation is derivable from
Newton's laws and vice versa. What changes is the labour and the reach: the
same recipe works in whatever coordinates suit the problem, extends cleanly
to fields and to quantum mechanics, and makes conservation laws fall out of
symmetries — if L does not depend on a coordinate, the quantity paired with
it is conserved, with no separate argument required.
