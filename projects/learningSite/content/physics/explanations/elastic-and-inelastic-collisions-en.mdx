Momentum conservation follows from Newton's third law alone — whatever force
one colliding object exerts on the other, the other exerts back equally and
oppositely, and that symmetry holds regardless of what the objects are made
of or what happens to their kinetic energy. Momentum is therefore conserved in
*every* collision, full stop, elastic or not.

Kinetic energy has no such guarantee. Collisions can convert kinetic energy
into heat, sound, or the work of permanently deforming the colliding objects,
and that conversion only ever runs one way — energy leaves the "kinetic"
column and does not come back. A collision that conserves kinetic energy
exactly is called elastic, and it is the special case, not the default;
billiard balls and gas molecules colliding come close, but two lumps of clay
colliding and sticking together do not, because deforming and merging the clay
consumes real energy.

A perfectly inelastic collision is the extreme of that spectrum: the objects
stick together and move off with a shared final velocity, and it loses the
*maximum* kinetic energy consistent with momentum still being conserved — not
all of it, and momentum conservation is exactly what limits how much can be
lost. Sticking together is what happens when the objects share the least
kinetic energy compatible with a common final velocity; two clay lumps of
equal mass colliding head-on at equal speed can even end up momentarily at
rest, all their kinetic energy gone, while momentum — which was zero to begin
with — is still conserved at zero. Between the elastic and perfectly inelastic
extremes sits every real collision, losing some intermediate amount of kinetic
energy while never losing any momentum at all.
