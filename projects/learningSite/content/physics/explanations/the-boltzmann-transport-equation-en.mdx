Rather than tracking every individual particle's exact trajectory, the Boltzmann transport
equation tracks a single function f(r, v, t): the density of particles at position r, with
velocity v, at time t. Its time evolution has two distinct pieces — a streaming term,
describing how f changes just from particles moving freely and responding to any external
force, and a collision term, describing how collisions between particles redistribute
velocities, pushing the distribution toward local equilibrium.

In true thermal equilibrium with no external gradients, the collision term exactly balances
the streaming term and f settles into the time-independent Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution —
but the equation's real power shows up away from equilibrium, when a temperature gradient,
an electric field, or a concentration gradient is imposed. Solving (often approximately, via
the relaxation-time approximation, which assumes collisions relax any deviation from local
equilibrium at a single characteristic rate) for how f responds to that imposed gradient
gives macroscopic transport coefficients directly: electrical conductivity from the response
to an electric field, thermal conductivity from the response to a temperature gradient,
viscosity from the response to a velocity gradient.

This is what makes the Boltzmann equation the standard bridge between microscopic collision
physics and macroscopic transport phenomena: it doesn't just say that a gas has some
resistance or some viscosity, it calculates the value from the underlying collision
cross-sections and mean free path, connecting a measurable macroscopic number to
microscopic mechanics. The same equation, applied to electrons in a metal or semiconductor
scattering off lattice vibrations and impurities rather than colliding with each other, is
the standard starting point for calculating electrical and thermal conductivity in
solid-state physics.
