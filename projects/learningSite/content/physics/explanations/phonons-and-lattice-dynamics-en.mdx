A crystal's atoms are not fixed in place; they vibrate about their equilibrium lattice
positions, and because each atom is coupled to its neighbours, these vibrations aren't
independent — the same normal-mode analysis that decouples any system of coupled
oscillators applies here, giving a spectrum of collective vibrational modes, each with its
own frequency, distinguished by wavevector k rather than by which atom is moving.

Quantizing each of these normal modes, exactly the way the quantum harmonic oscillator is
quantized, gives phonons: each mode's energy comes in discrete steps of h-bar times its
frequency, and a mode excited to its n-th level is described as containing n phonons of that
mode. A phonon isn't a real particle sitting somewhere in the crystal — it's a quantum of
collective lattice vibration — but it carries a well-defined energy and crystal momentum
and, for essentially every practical calculation (heat capacity, thermal conductivity,
electron-phonon scattering), behaves exactly like a genuine particle would.

This particle-like behaviour is what makes phonons so useful: a solid's low-temperature
specific heat, its thermal conductivity, and even superconductivity in conventional
superconductors (where phonon exchange is the attractive interaction between electrons that
pairs them up in BCS theory) are all most naturally understood as phonons behaving like a
gas of quasiparticles — being created, destroyed, and scattering off electrons, impurities
and each other, the same conceptual toolkit used for photons and other genuine particles,
applied to collective vibrations of a many-atom system.
