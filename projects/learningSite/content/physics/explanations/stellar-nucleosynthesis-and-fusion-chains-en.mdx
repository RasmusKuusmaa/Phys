In stars like the Sun, hydrogen fuses into helium mainly through the proton-proton chain: a
sequence of reactions starting with two protons fusing directly (an intrinsically slow step,
since it requires one proton to convert to a neutron via the weak interaction mid-collision,
which is why a star like the Sun burns hydrogen at all only over billions of years rather
than in a runaway instant). In more massive stars, whose hotter cores favour a different
reaction pathway, the CNO cycle achieves the same net result — four protons converted to one
helium nucleus — using carbon, nitrogen and oxygen nuclei already present as catalysts,
cycling through a sequence of proton captures and radioactive decays that regenerate the
original carbon nucleus at the end.

Once a star's core exhausts its hydrogen, fusion there stops and the core contracts and
heats up under gravity's unopposed pull, until it reaches the roughly 100-million-kelvin
temperature needed for the triple-alpha process: three helium-4 nuclei fusing to form one
carbon-12 nucleus, via a short-lived, unstable beryllium-8 intermediate that must be struck
by a third helium nucleus before it decays back apart — a reaction whose rate depends
critically on a precisely-tuned nuclear resonance in carbon-12 that Fred Hoyle correctly
predicted must exist, purely from the observed cosmic abundance of carbon, before it was
experimentally confirmed.

In stars massive enough to keep contracting and heating further after helium is exhausted,
successive fusion stages build up progressively heavier elements — carbon into neon and
oxygen, then further stages up toward silicon and finally iron — with each successive fuel
burning faster and at a higher temperature than the last. Iron marks a genuine endpoint for
this process: it sits at the peak of nuclear binding energy per nucleon, so fusing it
further would consume energy rather than release it, which is exactly why elements heavier
than iron are produced by different processes entirely (neutron capture during a star's
later life or, more dramatically, in the extreme conditions of a supernova or neutron star
merger), not by ordinary stellar fusion.
