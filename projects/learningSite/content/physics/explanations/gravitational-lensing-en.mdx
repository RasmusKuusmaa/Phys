General relativity holds that mass and energy curve spacetime, and that light, like anything
else, follows the straightest possible path through that curved geometry — a geodesic. When a
concentration of mass sits along the line of sight between a distant source and an observer, it
curves spacetime enough to bend the paths of light rays passing nearby, acting as a lens in
close analogy to a glass lens bending light through refraction, except here it is spacetime
itself, not the light's speed, doing the bending. The result depends sensitively on the exact
geometry: near-perfect alignment between source, lens, and observer produces a ring of light
around the lensing mass (an Einstein ring), while the more common, imperfect alignments produce
distorted arcs, multiple offset images of the same background source, or subtler distortions.

Strong lensing, dramatic enough to see directly in a single image, occurs around very massive,
compact concentrations of mass such as galaxy clusters, producing the giant arcs and multiple
images seen in cluster images, and it has become a standard tool for mapping a cluster's total
mass distribution in detail, since the degree and pattern of distortion depends precisely on
how much mass lies where. Weak lensing is far subtler: any individual background galaxy's
shape is only slightly sheared, an effect too small to detect in a single image, but by
statistically averaging the shapes of enormous numbers of background galaxies, weak lensing
surveys can reconstruct the mass distribution of foreground structures, including ordinary
individual galaxies, without needing any dramatic, single-image alignment at all.

Because lensing responds to the full distribution of mass and energy along the line of sight —
regardless of whether that mass emits, absorbs, or reflects any light — it is one of the most
direct tools available for mapping where the universe's mass actually is, independent of any
assumption about how luminous it is. This has made gravitational lensing one of the strongest
pieces of evidence for dark matter: lensing maps around galaxy clusters, most famously the
Bullet Cluster, reveal total mass distributions substantially larger than, and in some cases
spatially offset from, the distribution of visible matter, a mismatch far more naturally
explained by a large, mostly invisible mass component than by any known modification to how
visible matter alone would gravitate.
