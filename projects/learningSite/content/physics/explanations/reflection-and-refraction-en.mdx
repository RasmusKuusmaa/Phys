When light hits a surface, some of it bounces off — reflection — and,
if the surface leads into a transparent material, some of it passes through
while bending — refraction. Most surfaces you look at every day do a bit of
both.

Reflection follows a simple rule: the angle at which light arrives, measured
from an imaginary line perpendicular to the surface called the normal,
equals the angle at which it leaves. Both angles are measured from that
normal line, not from the surface itself — a very common slip when first
learning the law.

Refraction happens because light travels at different speeds in different
materials, and that speed change bends the ray's path at the boundary. Light
slows down and bends toward the normal when entering a denser material, and
speeds up and bends away from the normal when leaving one — the bending
always happens, in one direction or the other, whenever light crosses into a
different transparent material.
