Whether a fluid flow is smooth and orderly or chaotic and swirling depends
on a competition between two kinds of forces within the flow. Inertial
forces tend to keep fluid parcels moving along their existing paths, driving
disturbances to grow and cascade into eddies and chaos; viscous forces
resist relative motion between adjacent layers of fluid, damping out
disturbances and smoothing the flow back toward orderly layers. The Reynolds
number, Re, is the dimensionless ratio of a flow's characteristic inertial
forces to its characteristic viscous forces, combining the flow speed,
a characteristic length scale (such as a pipe's diameter), the fluid's
density, and its viscosity into one number.

Because the Reynolds number is dimensionless — the units of speed, length,
density, and viscosity cancel out entirely in its definition — the same
numerical value has the same physical meaning regardless of what units were
used to compute it, and regardless of whether the flow in question is honey
moving through a small tube or air moving over an aircraft wing. This is
what makes the Reynolds number such a powerful predictive tool: a low
Reynolds number (viscous forces dominant) predicts laminar flow, fluid
moving in smooth, parallel layers with no mixing between them; a high
Reynolds number (inertial forces dominant) predicts turbulent flow,
characterized by chaotic eddies, rapid mixing, and unpredictable local
fluctuations in velocity and pressure. Between these regimes lies a
transitional range where the flow can be intermittently laminar and
turbulent, sensitive to small disturbances.

Critically, whether a given real flow ends up laminar or turbulent is not
determined by speed alone — a fast flow of a very viscous fluid through a
narrow channel can still be laminar, while a slow flow of a low-viscosity
fluid through a wide channel can already be turbulent, because it is the
combination captured in the Reynolds number, not speed in isolation, that
sets the outcome. This is why the same qualitative transition from laminar
to turbulent flow is observed at wildly different absolute speeds depending
on the fluid and geometry involved, from blood flow in arteries to water
flow in pipes to airflow over a wing, and why engineers use the Reynolds
number rather than speed alone when designing systems where the difference
between laminar and turbulent flow matters for drag, mixing, or heat
transfer.
