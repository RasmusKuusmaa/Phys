For two positively charged nuclei to fuse, they must approach closely enough for the short-range
strong nuclear force to take over from the long-range electrostatic Coulomb repulsion pushing
them apart. Classically, this requires kinetic energy comparable to the Coulomb barrier's
height at that close range, typically on the order of a mega-electronvolt or more — but typical
stellar core temperatures (tens of millions of kelvin for hydrogen burning) correspond to
average thermal kinetic energies of only a few kilo-electronvolts, many orders of magnitude too
low. Classically, fusion inside ordinary stars should essentially never happen. It happens
anyway, and at a rate that powers stars for billions of years, because of quantum mechanical
tunneling: a nucleus does not need enough energy to classically clear the barrier, only a
nonzero (if often small) quantum probability of tunneling through it, and that tunneling
probability, while tiny at these energies, is not zero.

The actual reaction rate at a given temperature results from a competition between two
opposing effects as a function of the nuclei's relative kinetic energy. The Maxwell-Boltzmann
distribution means the number of particle pairs available at a given energy falls off rapidly
as energy increases above the thermal average. But the tunneling probability rises extremely
steeply with energy (following the Gamow factor, derived from the WKB approximation applied to
the Coulomb barrier), since higher-energy particles need to tunnel through a thinner effective
barrier. Multiplying these two rapidly varying, oppositely trending factors together produces a
sharply peaked function of energy — the Gamow peak — sitting well above the thermal average
energy, at the specific energy where the product of "particles available" and "tunneling
probability" is maximized.

The Gamow peak's location and width depend on both the plasma temperature and the specific
reacting nuclei's atomic numbers (through the Coulomb barrier height, proportional to the
product of their charges), so different fusion reactions have quite different Gamow peaks even
at the same temperature — a key reason why hydrogen burning (via the low-barrier proton-proton
chain) ignites at far lower temperatures than helium burning (via the higher-barrier
triple-alpha process), which in turn ignites at far lower temperatures than carbon burning, and
so on through a star's successive nuclear burning stages. The reaction rate that actually
determines how fast a star consumes its fuel, and hence its lifetime and eventual fate, is set
by integrating the fusion cross-section against this Gamow-peaked energy distribution, not by
any naive classical estimate using the average thermal energy alone.
