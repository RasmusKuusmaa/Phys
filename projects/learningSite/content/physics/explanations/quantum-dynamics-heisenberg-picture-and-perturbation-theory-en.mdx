The Schrödinger equation puts time evolution into the state: the wavefunction changes, while an operator like position or momentum stays the same fixed mathematical object at every instant. The Heisenberg picture repackages the exact same physics by doing the opposite — it freezes the state vector at some reference time and instead lets the operators evolve, governed by an equation built from the commutator with the Hamiltonian. Every measurable prediction, every expectation value, comes out identical either way; the split between "what evolves" and "what's fixed" is a choice of bookkeeping, not a fact about nature.

The Heisenberg picture earns its keep in a specific situation: when you want to track how an operator's own dynamics compare to its classical counterpart, since the Heisenberg equation of motion looks structurally like Hamilton's equations from classical mechanics, just with commutators standing in for Poisson brackets.

Time-dependent perturbation theory asks a related but different question, usually in the Schrödinger picture: a system sits in an eigenstate of some Hamiltonian, then a small time-dependent term gets switched on. The unperturbed eigenstates don't change shape — they remain a fixed basis throughout — but the system's state becomes a mixture of them, with amplitudes that shift over time according to the perturbation's matrix elements between states. Fermi's golden rule packages the leading-order result of this calculation into a transition rate, one of the most-used formulas in atomic, nuclear and particle physics for "how fast does this decay or transition happen."
