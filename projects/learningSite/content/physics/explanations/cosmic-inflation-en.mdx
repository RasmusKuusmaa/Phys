Run the standard hot Big Bang model backward without modification, and two puzzles appear immediately. The first is the horizon problem: the cosmic microwave background looks almost perfectly uniform in temperature across the whole sky, yet regions separated by more than about a degree, tracing the standard expansion history back to recombination, were never close enough for light (let alone anything slower) to have passed between them -- there was simply no time for that. Ordinary physical processes equalize temperature only by exchanging heat between regions in causal contact, so this uniformity, under standard expansion alone, has no explanation; it looks like an initial condition that was simply asserted, not derived. The second is the flatness problem: the universe's overall spatial curvature is observed to be extremely close to zero (flat), but flatness is a knife-edge, unstable condition under standard Big Bang expansion -- any tiny deviation from it early on should have been amplified enormously by today, so observing near-perfect flatness now requires an absurdly fine-tuned initial curvature unless something actively drove the universe toward flatness.

Cosmic inflation resolves both problems with a single proposed mechanism: an extremely brief early episode, ending a tiny fraction of a second after the Big Bang, during which the universe underwent exponential (not merely fast) expansion, driven by the near-constant potential energy of a scalar field slowly rolling down a very flat potential. Because the expansion was exponential, an enormous factor of stretching happened in a vanishingly short time. This resolves the horizon problem because the entire region that would become today's observable universe was, before inflation, a single, small, causally-connected patch already at one uniform temperature -- inflation then stretched that one thermalized patch to enormous size, carrying its uniform temperature along with it. It resolves the flatness problem because exponential expansion dilutes spatial curvature exponentially fast, driving whatever curvature existed beforehand toward zero, the same way inflating a balloon to an enormous size makes any patch of its surface look locally flatter.

Inflation does more than patch over these two problems: because it is driven by a quantum field, that field necessarily has quantum fluctuations, and inflation's exponential expansion stretches those microscopic quantum fluctuations up to macroscopic, eventually astronomical scales, freezing them in as small density variations across the universe. These are exactly the seed density perturbations that cosmological perturbation theory tracks forward through cosmic history -- the same fluctuations imprinted as temperature variations in the cosmic microwave background, and the same fluctuations that later grew under gravity into every galaxy, galaxy cluster, and cosmic void observed today. Inflation itself ends in a process called reheating, converting the scalar field's energy into the hot, dense soup of particles that the standard hot Big Bang model then takes over from.
