Whenever a free, unbound nucleus emits a gamma-ray photon, momentum conservation demands that the nucleus itself recoil, exactly as a gun recoils when firing a bullet. That recoil carries away a small but measurable amount of energy, shifting the emitted photon's energy down from the nucleus's exact nuclear transition energy. This shift, though tiny, is normally larger than the photon's own natural linewidth, so the emitted photon's energy no longer matches what an identical nucleus at rest would need to absorb it resonantly -- ordinary free-nucleus gamma emission cannot be resonantly reabsorbed.

Rudolf Mössbauer discovered, initially by accident while studying gamma-ray absorption at low temperature, that this picture changes dramatically when the emitting nucleus is rigidly bound inside a crystal lattice rather than free. In a substantial fraction of such emissions, the recoil momentum is absorbed not by the single nucleus alone but by the crystal lattice as a whole, acting as one enormously more massive rigid body. Since recoil energy for a given momentum kick scales inversely with the recoiling mass, and the crystal's effective mass in these events is vastly larger than a single nucleus's, the resulting energy shift becomes utterly negligible. The emitted photon's energy is then essentially identical to the nucleus's exact transition energy, restoring genuinely resonant, recoil-free gamma emission and absorption -- the Mössbauer effect.

Because recoil-free emission is so precisely sharp in energy, Mössbauer spectroscopy becomes an extraordinarily sensitive probe of any additional, otherwise minuscule energy shift affecting the emitting or absorbing nucleus: shifts from the local chemical bonding environment, from an applied magnetic field splitting nuclear energy levels, or from purely kinematic effects. This sensitivity was put to a striking test in the 1959 Pound-Rebka experiment, which used the Mössbauer effect to directly measure the tiny gravitational redshift a gamma-ray photon accumulates falling through just twenty-two metres of a laboratory tower -- a landmark, laboratory-scale confirmation of a genuine general-relativistic prediction, achieved decades before space-based tests of gravitational redshift became possible, alongside the technique's now-routine use in chemistry and materials science (Mössbauer spectroscopy) to probe local atomic environments.
