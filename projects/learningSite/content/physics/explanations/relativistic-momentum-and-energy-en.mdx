Newton's momentum p = mv has a problem: keep pushing an object and it should
reach any speed you like, including speeds past c. Relativity fixes this by
attaching a factor that grows without bound as v approaches c. At everyday
speeds that factor is indistinguishable from 1, which is why the classical
formula works so well; at 0.99c it is about 7, and it climbs steeply from
there. Push harder and the momentum and energy keep rising — but the speed
only creeps closer to c, never through it.

That is the honest answer to "why can't anything go faster than light?" It
is not that a barrier appears. It is that the energy needed to close the
remaining gap in speed grows towards infinity, so the gap never closes. The
particle is not being held back; the relationship between energy and speed
is simply not the one Newtonian mechanics assumed.

The same reworking of energy produces E = mc². Mass turns out to be a form
of energy that an object carries just by existing — its rest energy, present
whether it is in a reactor, in a stone, or in you. Nuclear reactions are
famous for it only because they convert an unusually large fraction of it
into other forms; a chemical fire does the same thing, in amounts far too
small to notice. The equation is not a special rule for nuclear physics, it
is a statement about what mass *is*.
