Classically, a ball rolled at a hill it hasn't the energy to climb rolls
back. Quantum mechanically, the particle's wavefunction does not stop dead
at the barrier face. Inside the barrier it stops oscillating and instead
decays exponentially — but exponential decay never quite reaches zero, so if
the barrier is thin enough, a small but nonzero amplitude emerges on the far
side. Square it and you have a real probability of finding the particle
through.

The popular explanation, that the particle "borrows" energy to get over the
top, is worth discarding. Nothing is borrowed and the particle never has
more energy than it started with; it does not go over the barrier at all,
and it is never found part-way inside it. What crosses is the wavefunction,
and the energy on the far side is exactly the energy on the near side.

The exponential is the practically important part. Probability falls off
steeply with both the width of the barrier and the square root of its height,
so tunnelling is overwhelming at atomic scales and utterly negligible at
everyday ones — which is why you cannot walk through a wall but an alpha
particle escapes a nucleus. That same steepness is what makes the scanning
tunnelling microscope work: a current that changes by orders of magnitude
over a fraction of an atomic diameter turns tunnelling into a height sensor
sharp enough to image single atoms. Tunnelling also runs the Sun, letting
protons fuse at temperatures far below what classical repulsion would demand.
