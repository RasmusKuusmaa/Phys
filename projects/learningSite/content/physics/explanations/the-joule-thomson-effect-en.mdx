When a gas is forced through a valve, porous plug, or other restriction from a region of high
pressure to a region of low pressure — a process called throttling — with no heat exchanged with
the surroundings and no external work extracted from the process, its temperature can change. For
an ideal gas, this temperature change is always exactly zero, a direct consequence of an ideal
gas's internal energy depending only on temperature and not at all on pressure or volume, since
an ideal gas has no intermolecular forces between its molecules. A real gas, however, generally
does experience a temperature change during throttling, because real gas molecules do exert
intermolecular forces on one another, and as the gas expands during throttling, work is done
either against these attractive forces (pulling molecules apart, which costs energy drawn from
the gas's thermal motion, cooling the gas) or with the assistance of net repulsive interactions at
very high density (which can instead heat the gas).

Whether a specific real gas cools or heats upon throttling depends on its temperature relative to
a characteristic value called the inversion temperature. Below the inversion temperature,
intermolecular attraction dominates the relevant energy balance, and the gas cools upon
throttling — this is the familiar, more commonly encountered regime for many common gases (such as
nitrogen, oxygen, and carbon dioxide) near everyday conditions. Above the inversion temperature,
the same gas actually heats up upon throttling instead. This has a genuinely practical
consequence: hydrogen and helium have unusually low inversion temperatures, well below ordinary
room temperature, so throttling them starting from room temperature actually heats them rather
than cooling them; these gases must first be pre-cooled by other means (typically using a
different refrigerant already below their inversion temperature) before Joule-Thomson throttling
can be applied to cool them further.

The Joule-Thomson cooling effect, applied repeatedly in a cascade of compression, cooling
(typically using heat exchangers and the already-cooled outgoing gas stream), and further
throttling stages, is the working principle behind most industrial gas liquefaction processes.
Large-scale production of liquid nitrogen and liquid oxygen — used throughout medicine, industry,
and scientific research — and the liquefaction of natural gas for large-scale transport (liquefied
natural gas, or LNG) both rely fundamentally on this effect, making it one of thermodynamics' most
economically significant practical applications rather than a narrow laboratory curiosity.
