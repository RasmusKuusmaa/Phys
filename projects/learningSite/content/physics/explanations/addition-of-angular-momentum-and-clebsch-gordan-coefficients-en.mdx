Two independent angular momenta j1 and j2 — say, two electrons' spins, or one particle's
orbital and spin angular momentum — can each be described by their own quantum numbers, but
a state like "j1 = 1/2 and j2 = 1/2, both pointing in some direction" is not automatically a
state of definite total angular momentum J = j1 + j2. The rules of quantum angular momentum
addition say the allowed total quantum number j ranges over every value from |j1 - j2| up to
j1 + j2 in integer steps, not just the naive vector sum.

For two spin-1/2 particles, this means the total spin can be either j = 1 (a triplet of
three states, symmetric under exchanging the two particles) or j = 0 (a single singlet
state, antisymmetric under exchange) — four total combined states in either description,
split differently depending on whether you track each particle's individual spin
projection or the pair's total spin and its projection. The Clebsch-Gordan coefficients are
exactly the numbers that convert between these two equally valid, complete descriptions:
they tell you how to write each total-angular-momentum state as a specific combination of
the individual-particle states, and vice versa.

This matters far beyond a bookkeeping exercise: which total angular momentum states are
even accessible constrains which transitions and interactions are allowed at all (a
selection rule), determines the fine structure of atomic spectra when orbital and spin
angular momentum combine, and underlies how nucleons combine their spins inside a nucleus.
Any calculation involving several sources of angular momentum in the same quantum system —
multi-electron atoms, coupled nuclear spins, particle physics multiplets — eventually needs
the same machinery to go from "each piece's own angular momentum" to "the whole system's
total angular momentum."
