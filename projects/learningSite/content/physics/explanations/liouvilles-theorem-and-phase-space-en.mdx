Phase space is the space of every possible position-and-momentum snapshot a system could be in. A single system is one point in that space; an ensemble of many identical systems started under slightly different conditions is a whole cloud of points, and as time passes each point flows along its own trajectory according to Hamilton's equations.

Liouville's theorem says that flow behaves like an incompressible fluid: the density of points around any given one never changes as you follow it forward in time. A small blob of initial conditions can stretch, shear and fold into an arbitrarily long, thin, tangled filament — real Hamiltonian systems do exactly this whenever the dynamics are chaotic — but the volume of phase space it occupies is exactly conserved. Nothing about the shape is protected, only the volume.

This is what makes probability distributions in phase space well-behaved: since points are neither created, destroyed, nor packed together, a probability density defined on phase space simply gets carried along by the flow rather than needing a separate equation to track how "crowded" any region becomes. It's also the resolution of an apparent puzzle with the second law — the exact, fine-grained phase-space volume of an evolving system never grows, yet a coarse-grained entropy (which can't resolve arbitrarily fine filaments) does grow, because the same fixed volume gets spread thinner and thinner across a coarse grid.
