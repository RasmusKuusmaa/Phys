Expand 1/|r - r'| — the distance between a field point far away and a source point inside a
charge distribution — as a power series in r'/r, and the electrostatic potential of any
localized charge distribution becomes a sum of terms, each falling off as a successive power
of 1/r: 1/r for the monopole term, 1/r^2 for the dipole term, 1/r^3 for the quadrupole term,
and so on. Each term's coefficient (the corresponding multipole moment) is a fixed integral
over the charge distribution, independent of where the field point is.

The monopole moment is just the total charge, and it alone determines the potential's leading
far-field behaviour whenever it's nonzero — from far enough away, any charged object looks
like a point charge, its shape entirely invisible in the potential's dominant term. Only when
the total charge is exactly zero does the dipole moment take over as the leading behaviour,
and only when both the total charge and the dipole moment vanish does the quadrupole term
become the dominant, farthest-reaching contribution.

This is why a neutral system with no net dipole moment (a symmetric molecule, for instance)
can still have a nonzero quadrupole moment and a correspondingly weaker, faster-falling
long-range field — nothing about being uncharged and non-polar makes an object's field
vanish outright, it just pushes the leading term to a higher, faster-decaying multipole
order. The same machinery, applied to current distributions rather than charge
distributions, gives the magnetic multipole expansion, whose leading term is the magnetic
dipole rather than a monopole, since no magnetic monopoles have ever been observed.
