Beyond the smooth, average binding energy described by the liquid-drop model's volume, surface, Coulomb, and asymmetry terms, nuclear binding energies show a clear, systematic extra effect: nuclei with even numbers of both protons and neutrons (even-even nuclei) are measurably more tightly bound than their odd-numbered neighbors would suggest from the smooth trend alone. This is the direct fingerprint of a residual nuclear force acting between identical nucleons occupying the same shell-model orbital, an attraction that specifically favors two such nucleons coupling their angular momentum projections to cancel, forming a pair. A nucleus with all its nucleons paired up this way sits in an especially low-energy, extra-stable configuration.

This physical situation -- identical fermions near a Fermi surface experiencing a short-range attraction that drives them to condense into correlated pairs -- is not unique to nuclei. It is essentially the same physics BCS theory was built to describe for electrons in a superconductor, and nuclear structure theory borrowed the BCS mathematical formalism directly, adapting it to nucleons and the nuclear force rather than treating it as a separate, merely analogous theory. Just as paired electrons in a superconductor must be broken apart at an energy cost (the superconducting gap) before they can scatter individually, breaking a nucleon pair costs a characteristic pairing energy, visibly suppressing the density of low-energy excited states just above a paired nuclear ground state.

A fully paired nucleus is therefore said to behave as a nuclear superfluid, and this has observable consequences distinct from, though closely related to, ordinary electronic superconductivity. One of the clearest is in rotating nuclei: a nucleus with fully paired nucleons resists being set into rotation differently than an unpaired, rigid-body configuration would, because pairing correlations partially decouple individual nucleons from the nucleus's overall rotational motion, reducing its effective moment of inertia below the simple rigid-body value. Matching the observed moments of inertia of rotating nuclei (measured via their rotational band energy spacings) against pairing theory's predictions was, historically, one of the strongest confirmations that nuclear pairing is a genuine physical effect rather than a convenient bookkeeping device -- direct evidence that the same pairing mechanism underlying zero-resistance current in a superconducting metal also operates deep inside the atomic nucleus.
