The Ising model places a two-state variable (spin up or spin down) on every site of a
lattice, with an energy that favours neighbouring spins pointing the same way. At high
temperature, thermal fluctuations randomize the spins and the average magnetization is
zero; below a specific critical temperature, the interaction wins out over thermal
randomization and the spins spontaneously align, giving a nonzero net magnetization even
with no external field pushing them to — the simplest microscopic picture of a
ferromagnetic phase transition.

Solving the model exactly means accounting for every spin's actual interaction with its
actual neighbours, a genuinely hard many-body problem in more than one dimension.
Mean-field theory sidesteps this by replacing each spin's neighbours with a single
effective, self-consistent average field — every spin then only has to respond to this one
mean field rather than to its specific neighbours individually, turning the many-body
problem into a single-spin problem whose self-consistency condition can be solved directly,
correctly predicting a phase transition at some nonzero critical temperature.

Mean-field theory gets the qualitative picture right — a phase transition exists,
magnetization appears continuously below the critical temperature — but its quantitative
predictions (the exact critical temperature, and especially the critical exponents
describing how quantities behave right at the transition) are generally wrong in low
dimensions, since averaging away neighbour-to-neighbour fluctuations specifically throws
away the correlated fluctuations that matter most near a critical point. It works
increasingly well as the number of interacting neighbours grows (higher spatial dimension,
or longer-range interactions), which is exactly the regime in which the averaging
approximation becomes accurate rather than merely convenient.
