When a giant molecular cloud fragments hierarchically under the Jeans instability, it does not produce stars of one uniform mass, or masses scattered evenly across the possible range. Instead, the resulting distribution of stellar masses at birth follows a strikingly consistent statistical pattern called the initial mass function (IMF). Edwin Salpeter first characterized its shape in 1955 as a power law: the number of stars formed per unit mass interval falls off steeply as mass increases, meaning low-mass stars vastly outnumber high-mass ones. A typical newly formed cluster contains a huge number of faint, long-lived red dwarfs for every single short-lived, massive O or B star.

The IMF describes a population at the moment of its birth -- a snapshot of how many stars of each mass formed together, not how any individual star subsequently changes. What happens afterward (a star losing mass through winds, transferring mass to a binary companion, or exploding as a supernova) is separate stellar-evolution physics layered on top of this fixed initial distribution. Remarkably, essentially the same IMF shape is observed in star-forming regions with very different compositions, densities, and even across a wide range of cosmic time, suggesting some underlying universality to the fragmentation process, even though a first-principles derivation of exactly why fragmentation consistently lands on this particular shape remains an active area of research rather than settled theory.

The IMF's practical importance goes well beyond describing individual clusters: because it is so consistently reproduced, astronomers use it as a standard conversion tool, translating an observed galaxy's total measured light (which is dominated by a small number of massive, extremely luminous stars) into an estimate of the galaxy's total stellar mass (which is dominated numerically, though not in light, by the vastly more numerous low-mass stars) and its recent star-formation rate. This single statistical tool is what lets a telescope image of starlight be turned into a physically meaningful estimate of how much mass a galaxy has actually built into stars over its history.
