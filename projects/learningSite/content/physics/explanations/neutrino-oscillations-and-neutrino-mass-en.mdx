The Standard Model, as originally formulated, treats neutrinos as exactly massless,
appearing in exactly one of three "flavours" (electron, muon, tau neutrinos) tied to the
charged lepton produced alongside them in a weak interaction. But solar, atmospheric and
reactor neutrino experiments over several decades found something the massless picture
cannot explain: a neutrino created in one flavour has a probability, oscillating
periodically with the distance travelled, of being detected as a different flavour later —
electron neutrinos from the sun, for instance, are measured on Earth with a substantially
reduced electron-flavour rate, with the "missing" fraction showing up as muon and tau
flavours instead.

This oscillation is only possible if two conditions both hold: neutrinos have nonzero mass
(a massless particle, always moving at exactly the speed of light, experiences no proper
time in which to oscillate at all), and the three flavour states aren't identical to the
three mass eigenstates — each flavour state is instead a specific quantum superposition of
different mass states, analogous to how quark flavour and quark mass eigenstates are related
by the CKM matrix. As a neutrino propagates, the different mass components of that
superposition accumulate different quantum phases at different rates, and recombining them
at a later point can give a different flavour composition than the one it started with.

The discovery of neutrino oscillation, confirmed independently by multiple experiments and
recognized by the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physics, was the first direct experimental evidence
that the Standard Model's original massless-neutrino assumption is wrong — a genuine gap the
Standard Model must be extended to accommodate, rather than a prediction it correctly made.
The oscillation experiments measure differences between the squares of the neutrino mass
states rather than the absolute masses themselves, so the actual absolute scale of neutrino
mass, and even the exact ordering of the three mass states, remains an open experimental
question.
