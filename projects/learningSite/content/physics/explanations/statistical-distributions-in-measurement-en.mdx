Repeat a physical measurement several times under seemingly identical
conditions and the results almost never come out exactly the same — small,
uncontrollable random influences (tiny timing variations, minor instrument
fluctuations, small reading inconsistencies) perturb each individual
reading a little differently. Rather than being a nuisance to be eliminated,
this scatter has a predictable statistical structure that measurement
science relies on directly.

When a measured value is affected by many small, independent random error
sources acting together, the resulting scatter of results tends to follow
the normal distribution (also called the Gaussian distribution), the
familiar symmetric bell-shaped curve centred on the true underlying value.
This is a consequence of the central limit theorem, which shows that adding
together many independent random contributions — regardless of the detailed
distribution of each individual contribution — produces a sum that is
approximately normally distributed, provided no single contribution
dominates the total. This is why the normal distribution shows up so
pervasively in measurement, even though the underlying causes of
measurement error can vary enormously from one experiment to another.

The normal distribution's spread is fully characterised by its standard
deviation, a single number describing how tightly the measurements cluster
around their mean: about 68% of individual measurements fall within one
standard deviation of the mean, and about 95% fall within two, for a
genuinely normal distribution. It is worth distinguishing this from the
uncertainty of the mean itself — the standard deviation describes the spread
of individual measurements, but averaging many measurements together
reduces the uncertainty in the mean value by a factor related to the square
root of the number of measurements, since random errors partially cancel
when averaged, while the spread of any single new measurement remains set by
the same standard deviation regardless of how many measurements have already
been taken.

This statistical structure is what justifies reporting a measurement as a
single mean value plus an uncertainty rather than as a raw list of scattered
numbers, and it underlies standard tools for comparing measurements — such
as asking whether two experimentally determined values agree with each
other within their stated uncertainties, or whether an outlier reading is
statistically consistent with the rest of a data set or should be
investigated as a likely mistake.
