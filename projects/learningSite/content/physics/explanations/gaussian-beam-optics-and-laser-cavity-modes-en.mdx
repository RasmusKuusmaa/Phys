Idealized geometric optics treats a well-collimated light beam as a perfectly parallel bundle of
rays, implicitly suggesting that with sufficiently good optics, a beam could be made arbitrarily
narrow and kept perfectly parallel indefinitely. Real light, being a wave, cannot behave this way:
any beam confined to a finite width unavoidably spreads out, or diverges, as it propagates,
a direct consequence of diffraction rather than any imperfection in the optics producing it. The
Gaussian beam is the standard mathematical model describing this realistic behaviour for the kind
of smooth, single-lobed intensity profile a well-behaved laser typically produces: its intensity
is highest along the beam's central axis and falls off smoothly (following a Gaussian function)
moving away from that axis, and the beam's width, measured this way, varies smoothly along the
direction of propagation, reaching a genuine minimum — the beam waist — at exactly one location,
and spreading out on either side of that point.

A Gaussian beam's divergence angle and its beam waist size are not independent quantities that
could each be minimized separately; they are fundamentally linked by diffraction, with their
product bounded below by a value set by the light's wavelength. Making the beam waist smaller
(focusing the beam more tightly) unavoidably increases the beam's divergence angle away from that
waist, and vice versa — a direct manifestation of the same diffraction limit that sets a minimum
achievable focused spot size for any given optical system, regardless of how well-designed that
system is.

Inside a laser cavity — typically two mirrors facing each other with the laser's gain medium in
between — only a discrete set of specific field patterns can exist self-consistently, reproducing
themselves after each round trip between the mirrors; these are the cavity's transverse modes.
The simplest, lowest-order mode (denoted TEM00) is exactly the fundamental Gaussian beam profile
described above, with a single smooth intensity peak on-axis, and lasers operating predominantly
in this mode produce the highest-quality output beam achievable, with the best possible
focusability for a given wavelength and optical system. Higher-order transverse modes, which the
cavity can also support under some conditions, have more complex intensity patterns with multiple
lobes and generally worse focusability, so laser cavity design (mirror curvature, spacing, and
gain-medium properties) is deliberately engineered to favor the fundamental mode whenever a
high-quality, tightly focusable output beam is the goal, as is the case for most precision laser
applications.
