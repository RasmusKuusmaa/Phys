When a fissile nucleus like uranium-235 absorbs a neutron and undergoes fission, it does not
split into two exactly equal-mass fragments, despite the naive symmetric expectation. The
actual distribution of fragment masses, the fission yield curve, has a distinctive double-humped
shape: fragments are produced overwhelmingly in two characteristic, unequal mass groups (roughly
a "light" group and a "heavy" group), with genuinely symmetric fission — splitting into two
equal-mass fragments — being comparatively rare. This asymmetry is a real nuclear structure
effect, related to the influence of nuclear shell structure on the fissioning nucleus's
preferred deformation pathway, not a simple statistical accident.

Every fission fragment, regardless of which mass group it falls into, is produced far too
neutron-rich to be a stable nuclide. This follows directly from the parent nucleus's own
neutron-to-proton ratio: heavy fissile nuclei like uranium-235 have a substantially higher
neutron-to-proton ratio than the stable nuclei found in the fragments' much lower mass region, so
when the nucleus splits, both fragments inherit a neutron-to-proton ratio far above what's stable
for nuclei of their new, smaller size. Each fragment therefore undergoes a chain of successive
beta decays, converting a neutron into a proton (and thereby increasing atomic number by one)
with each step, working its way back down toward the valley of nuclear stability, and often
passing through several distinct radioactive isotopes — the decay chain — before finally reaching
a genuinely stable end nuclide.

These specific fission products and their decay-chain daughters, rather than any remaining
unfissioned fuel, dominate the radioactivity and long-term hazard associated with spent nuclear
fuel and reactor accidents. Iodine-131, though short-lived (an eight-day half-life), is
biologically significant because the thyroid gland concentrates iodine, making it a primary
medical concern following an accidental release. Caesium-137 and strontium-90, both with
multi-decade half-lives and significant biological uptake (caesium behaving chemically similarly
to potassium, strontium to calcium), are major long-term contributors to spent fuel's radioactive
inventory and to environmental contamination following a release. Understanding fission product
yields and their specific decay chains in detail is therefore directly relevant to nuclear reactor
safety, radiological emergency response, and the long-term challenge of nuclear waste management,
not merely an abstract question of nuclear structure.
