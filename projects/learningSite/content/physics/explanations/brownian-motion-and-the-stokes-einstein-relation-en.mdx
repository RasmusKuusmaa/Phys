Robert Brown watched pollen grains in water in 1827 and found them in ceaseless
irregular motion. The grains were not alive and nothing was stirring the water:
they were being hit by water molecules, roughly a million million million times a
second, from every direction at once. The hits nearly cancel, and the small
residue left over at each instant is what shakes the grain. What you see under
the microscope is therefore a random walk with its steps too small and too
frequent to resolve individually.

Einstein's 1905 paper turned that picture into something measurable. The trick
was to notice that the molecular collisions do two jobs at the same time: they
kick the particle around, and they resist it when it moves. Those cannot be
independent, and the connection between them — the first fluctuation-dissipation
relation — fixes the diffusion coefficient as the thermal energy divided by the
drag coefficient. For a sphere in a viscous fluid the drag is Stokes', six pi
times viscosity times radius, which gives the Stokes-Einstein relation: warmer
means faster diffusion, thicker or bigger means slower, and no free parameters
are left over.

Two consequences are worth carrying away. First, the relation contains
Boltzmann's constant, so measuring the jitter of a visible particle gives a value
for a molecular quantity — Perrin did exactly that in 1908, extracted Avogadro's
number, and effectively ended the argument about whether atoms were real. Second,
run it backwards: measure a diffusion coefficient by watching how quickly a
speckle pattern of scattered laser light fluctuates, and the same equation
returns the particle's radius. That is what a dynamic light scattering
instrument does, and it is routine for sizing proteins, polymers and
nanoparticles today.
