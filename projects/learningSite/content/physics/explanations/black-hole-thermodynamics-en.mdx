Classical general relativity alone already produces laws for black holes that look exactly
like the laws of ordinary thermodynamics: a black hole's event horizon area never decreases
in any classical process (analogous to the second law's statement that entropy never
decreases), a stationary black hole's surface gravity is uniform everywhere on its horizon
(analogous to a system in thermal equilibrium having one uniform temperature), and there's
even a "zeroth law" and a "third law" with matching structure. For years this was treated as
a striking but purely mathematical coincidence, since a classical black hole absorbs
everything and (classically) emits nothing, giving it a temperature of exactly zero — an
object at absolute zero has no business obeying thermodynamic laws in any physical sense.

Stephen Hawking showed the analogy is not just formal: taking quantum field theory into
account near a black hole's event horizon, particle-antiparticle pairs that quantum
mechanics allows to spontaneously appear and annihilate everywhere in vacuum can, right at
the horizon, be separated — one member falling in, the other escaping to infinity as real,
observable radiation. This Hawking radiation gives a black hole a genuine, nonzero
temperature, inversely proportional to its mass, and the horizon area genuinely does behave
as a true entropy, satisfying an actual generalized second law (horizon area plus ordinary
entropy outside never decreases) rather than a coincidental lookalike.

Because Hawking radiation carries mass-energy away, a black hole slowly loses mass over
time and, given long enough, would evaporate completely — for a stellar-mass or larger
black hole this process is spectacularly slow (far longer than the current age of the
universe, since the radiation rate is inversely tied to mass and stellar-mass black holes
are extremely cold), so it has no measurable astrophysical consequence for any known black
hole, but it is the leading concrete clue physicists have that quantum mechanics and gravity
must fit together into some deeper unified theory, since resolving exactly what happens to
information that falls into an evaporating black hole (the black hole information paradox)
remains a genuinely open theoretical problem.
