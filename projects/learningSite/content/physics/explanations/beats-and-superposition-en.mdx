Superposition is the statement that when two waves overlap in the same
medium, their displacements simply add at every point, moment by moment, and
each wave continues on afterward exactly as if the other had never been
there. Waves do not collide, merge or bounce off one another the way
particles do; they pass straight through each other, and the pattern seen
while they overlap is nothing more than the running sum of the two.

Beats are what that addition produces when the two frequencies are close but
not identical. At some moments the two waves are in phase and reinforce,
producing a loud peak; a short time later they have drifted out of phase and
nearly cancel, producing a quiet dip. Because the two frequencies are slightly
different, this in-phase, out-of-phase cycle repeats steadily, and the ear
hears it as a slow throbbing in loudness layered on top of the underlying
pitch — a beat. If the two frequencies were exactly equal there would be no
drift and therefore no beating at all, so beats require a *difference*, not a
match.

The beat frequency is simply |f₁ − f₂|, the plain difference between the two
component frequencies — not their average, which is instead roughly the pitch
of the combined tone that the throbbing is layered on. Musicians use this
directly: two strings tuned close but not quite together produce an audible
beat, and tuning consists of adjusting one string until that beat frequency
drops toward zero, at which point the two are in tune.
