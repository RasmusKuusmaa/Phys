Spiral galaxies rotate faster at large distance from their center than the gravitational pull of their visible, luminous matter alone can account for under standard Newtonian gravity -- the same puzzle that motivates dark matter. In 1983, Mordehai Milgrom proposed a different resolution: rather than adding unseen mass, modify the effective law of gravity (or, in an equivalent formulation, the law of inertia) itself, but only in the regime of extremely low acceleration, below a characteristic threshold roughly a hundred billionth of Earth's surface gravity -- a regime essentially never probed in the Solar System or in any laboratory test of gravity, which is precisely why the modification can be tuned to leave everyday, well-tested physics untouched while altering behavior only in galaxies' faint, slowly-rotating outskirts.

MOND's genuine success is real and specific: it was built to reproduce, and does successfully reproduce, the empirically observed Tully-Fisher relation (a tight relationship between a spiral galaxy's total luminosity and its outer rotation speed) and fits the detailed rotation curve shapes of a great many individual galaxies using only their visible mass distribution, with no adjustable dark-matter halo parameters at all. This is a genuine predictive achievement that any serious alternative to dark matter needs to be judged against, and it is why MOND remains a serious subject of ongoing research rather than a dismissed curiosity.

The trouble for MOND appears at larger scales. Applied to galaxy clusters, MOND's modification alone falls significantly short of accounting for the full observed mass discrepancy, forcing MOND-based models to still posit some additional unseen mass in clusters -- undercutting the original motivation of eliminating dark matter altogether. Even more damaging is the Bullet Cluster: a pure modification of gravity tied to the distribution of ordinary matter predicts that the lensing signal (tracing total gravitating mass) should track the ordinary matter's location directly, yet the observed lensing mass sits offset from the hot, X-ray-emitting gas that makes up most of the cluster's ordinary matter. Dark matter explains this cleanly, since collisionless dark matter and the galaxies embedded in it pass through a cluster collision largely undisturbed while the gas collides electromagnetically and lags behind; a purely modified-gravity account has no comparably natural way to displace where gravity's source appears to be from where the ordinary matter actually is. This combination -- real, specific success at galactic scales, but serious unresolved trouble at cluster scales and beyond -- is why the great majority of the astrophysics community favors dark matter as the better-supported overall explanation, while MOND persists as an actively studied minority position specifically because its galactic-scale success still lacks a fully satisfying explanation within the standard dark-matter picture either.
