Not every solid has the same kind of internal order. In a crystalline solid,
atoms are arranged in a pattern that repeats regularly over long distances —
metals, salts, quartz, and diamond are all crystalline. In an amorphous solid
such as ordinary glass, atoms are still packed together tightly, but without
that long-range repeating order; local bonding arrangements are similar from
place to place, but there is no periodic pattern that extends indefinitely
through the material. This distinction — long-range periodic order versus its
absence — is what "crystalline" means physically.

Describing a crystal's structure precisely requires separating two ideas
that are easy to conflate. The lattice is a purely mathematical object: an
infinite, periodic array of points in space, generated by repeating a fixed
set of translation vectors, with every lattice point having an environment
identical to every other. The lattice by itself says nothing about atoms —
it is a scaffold. The actual crystal is built by attaching a basis — one or
more atoms, in a fixed arrangement — to every point of the lattice. A simple
crystal like copper has a basis of a single atom per lattice point; other
crystals, including many common minerals and semiconductor compounds, have a
basis of two, several, or many atoms repeated at each lattice point,
producing much more complex unit cells while the underlying lattice remains
simple.

This lattice-plus-basis picture is not just bookkeeping — it is the
foundation for essentially all of solid-state physics. The periodicity of
the lattice is what allows electron wavefunctions in a crystal to be
classified using Bloch's theorem, which in turn is what produces the allowed
and forbidden energy bands (covered in the next concept) that determine
whether a material is a conductor, semiconductor, or insulator. The same
periodicity is also what makes X-ray and electron diffraction techniques
work, since a periodic array of scattering centers produces the constructive
interference patterns (Bragg diffraction) used to determine a crystal's
structure experimentally in the first place.
