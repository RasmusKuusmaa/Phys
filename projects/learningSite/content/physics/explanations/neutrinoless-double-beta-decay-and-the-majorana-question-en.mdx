Double beta decay is a rare nuclear process in which a nucleus simultaneously converts two
neutrons into two protons, emitting two electrons and, in the standard version, two
antineutrinos. It occurs only in a small number of nuclei where ordinary single beta decay is
energetically forbidden or strongly suppressed but the combined two-step process remains
allowed, and it has been directly observed, fully consistent with the Standard Model and
conserving lepton number (the antineutrinos and electrons carry lepton number in a way that
balances exactly).

Neutrinoless double beta decay is a distinct, purely hypothetical variant of this process:
instead of emitting two antineutrinos, the process would emit only the two electrons, with no
neutrinos escaping at all. This is only possible under one specific condition: the neutrino
must be a Majorana fermion — its own antiparticle — rather than a Dirac fermion, distinct from
its antiparticle, as every other known fundamental fermion (electrons, quarks) is confirmed to
be. Because the neutrino is electrically neutral, it is not automatically required to differ
from its antiparticle the way a charged particle must (a charged particle's antiparticle
necessarily carries opposite charge and so cannot be identical to it), leaving the neutrino as
the one known fermion for which this Majorana possibility remains genuinely open. If the
neutrino is Majorana, an internal neutrino emitted at one vertex of the decay can effectively be
absorbed as an antineutrino at the other vertex, allowing the two virtual neutrino lines to
cancel internally and permitting the process to proceed with no neutrinos in the final state at
all.

This hypothetical decay has never been observed, and its rate, if it occurs at all, is predicted
to be extraordinarily slow, requiring extremely sensitive, large-scale, deeply shielded detector
experiments (using isotopes like germanium-76 or xenon-136) searching for a specific,
characteristic total energy signature from the two emitted electrons. Confirming it would be
significant well beyond simply resolving the neutrino's classification: because this process
would create two electrons from nothing else but nucleons (violating overall lepton number
conservation, since standard double beta decay's antineutrinos exactly balance the created
electrons' lepton number and a neutrinoless version has no such balancing), it would demonstrate
that lepton number is not a strictly conserved quantity in nature. This has significant
implications reaching well beyond neutrino physics itself, including for leptogenesis theories
attempting to explain why the observable universe contains far more matter than antimatter,
making the search for neutrinoless double beta decay one of the most significant open
experimental programs in contemporary particle and nuclear physics.
