Newtonian mechanics for a rigid body only ever needs one net force and one net torque, because a rigid body by definition does not deform -- every internal point maintains a fixed relationship to every other. A deformable solid is different: forces are transmitted continuously throughout its interior, and the force per unit area (traction) carried across an imagined internal surface at any given point depends sensitively on how that surface is oriented. Cut a stretched rod perpendicular to its length and the surface feels a large normal force per unit area pulling it apart; cut the same rod at an angle through the same point and the traction on that differently-oriented surface is different in both magnitude and direction. A single vector cannot capture this orientation-dependence; a full description requires the stress tensor, a rank-two object whose components specify the traction on every possible surface orientation passing through a given point.

The corresponding local deformation is captured by the strain tensor, which describes how nearby material points shift relative to each other -- stretching, compressing, and shearing -- in the neighborhood of a point. For a linear elastic material, the two tensors are related by a direct generalization of Hooke's law: rather than one scalar spring constant relating a single force to a single displacement, the full relationship is a rank-four elastic modulus tensor connecting every component of stress to every component of strain. In the most general anisotropic solid (where the material's elastic response genuinely differs with direction, as in a single crystal or a fiber-reinforced composite), this tensor has as many as twenty-one independent components that must be measured or calculated separately.

Most familiar, everyday materials are effectively isotropic -- their elastic response looks the same in every direction -- and this high degree of symmetry collapses the twenty-one independent elastic constants down to just two: the bulk modulus, describing resistance to uniform compression, and the shear modulus, describing resistance to shape-changing deformation at constant volume. The familiar single "stiffness" of an everyday material is really this isotropic special case; the underlying physics, for any real solid, is fundamentally the full tensor relationship, of which the single-spring-constant picture from an introductory course is only the simplest possible instance.
