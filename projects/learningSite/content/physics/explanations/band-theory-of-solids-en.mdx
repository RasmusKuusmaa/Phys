A single isolated atom has sharp, discrete allowed energy levels for its
electrons, with well-defined gaps between them, exactly as quantum
mechanics predicts for a confined system. Bring a large number of identical
atoms close together into a crystal, and this picture changes dramatically.
Neighbouring atoms' outer electron wavefunctions begin to overlap, and by
the Pauli exclusion principle, no two electrons in the combined system can
share exactly the same quantum state. What was a single sharp energy level
in the isolated atom splits, in the crystal, into an enormous number of
extremely closely spaced sub-levels — one for each atom's contribution to
that shared level — and with a macroscopic number of atoms (on the order of
10²³ per cubic centimetre), these sub-levels merge into what is, for all
practical purposes, a continuous allowed energy band.

Not every energy is allowed, however. Between one allowed band and the next
lies a forbidden band gap, an energy range that a crystal electron simply
cannot have, a direct consequence of the crystal lattice's periodicity acting
on the electron wavefunctions. The resulting picture of alternating allowed
bands and forbidden gaps is band theory, and it explains far more about a
solid's electronic behaviour than atomic energy levels ever could on their
own.

Whether a material conducts electricity, semiconducts, or insulates is
determined entirely by how these bands are filled and how large the gaps
between them are. In a conductor such as a metal, the highest occupied band
(the valence band) is only partially filled, or overlaps with the next band
up, so electrons near the top of the filled levels can move into
neighbouring, only slightly higher-energy states under an applied electric
field with almost no resistance — this is what makes metals conduct so
well. In an insulator, the valence band is completely filled and separated
from the next empty band (the conduction band) by a large band gap — several
electronvolts — that ordinary thermal energy at room temperature cannot
bridge, so essentially no electrons are available to carry current. A
semiconductor has exactly the same qualitative band structure as an
insulator — a filled valence band separated from an empty conduction band by
a gap — but with a much smaller gap, typically around one electronvolt, small
enough that a modest number of electrons are thermally excited across it at
room temperature, giving semiconductors their intermediate, temperature- and
impurity-sensitive conductivity.
