Expand the potential energy of any system to second order around a stable equilibrium and
the result is always a sum of quadratic terms — a set of effective springs coupling every
generalized coordinate to every other. The kinetic energy near equilibrium is likewise a
quadratic form in the generalized velocities, built from a mass (or "inertia") matrix.
Together they turn the equations of motion into a single matrix equation, M q'' = -K q,
where M and K are the constant mass and stiffness matrices evaluated at equilibrium.

Solving that matrix equation by assuming every coordinate oscillates at the same frequency,
q(t) = a cos(omega t), turns it into a generalized eigenvalue problem: K a = omega^2 M a.
Each eigenvalue omega^2 gives one of the system's normal-mode frequencies, and its
eigenvector a gives the corresponding normal mode — the specific pattern of relative
amplitudes and phases with which every coordinate must move for the whole system to
oscillate at that single frequency, all coordinates in step.

The real payoff is that the normal-mode coordinates decouple completely: written in terms of
them, the equations of motion become N independent simple-harmonic oscillators, one per
mode, each with its own frequency and no coupling term left over. Any general small motion
of the system, however complicated it looks in the original coordinates, is just a
superposition of these normal modes — which is why the same machinery describes two coupled
pendulums, the vibration spectrum of a polyatomic molecule, and the phonon modes of a
crystal lattice with no change in the underlying method.
