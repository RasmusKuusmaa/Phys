A double slit produces bright fringes wherever light from the two slits
arrives in phase, at angles satisfying d sin θ = mλ, but those fringes are
broad and only moderately bright, because there are only two waves to add up.
A diffraction grating uses the exact same physics — the same governing
equation, with d now the spacing between adjacent slits — but with thousands
of slits instead of two. The bright fringes still occur at the same angles
the two-slit formula predicts, since the condition for constructive
interference between *any* pair of adjacent slits is unchanged. What changes
dramatically is the sharpness: with thousands of slits all interfering
constructively at once, the bright fringes become intensely bright and
extremely narrow, while the destructive interference between all those slits
crushes everything in between to near-total darkness. More slits does not
just add brightness; it is what sharpens a broad double-slit fringe into a
sharp, precisely located line.

That sharpness is precisely why gratings, not double slits, are the practical
tool for measuring wavelength. Because the bright-fringe angle depends on
wavelength, a grating illuminated with light of several different wavelengths
sends each one to a slightly different, sharply defined angle rather than
producing overlapping smears — separating white light into a clean, well
resolved spectrum, and letting the wavelengths present in an unknown light
source be read off precisely from where its bright lines land. This is the
working principle behind spectroscopy: a grating spectrometer measures
exactly the angles at which a source's light forms bright fringes and
converts those angles back into wavelengths using the grating equation,
turning a light source's colour composition into a directly measurable
quantity.
