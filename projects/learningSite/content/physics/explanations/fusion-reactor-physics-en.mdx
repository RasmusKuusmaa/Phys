Fusing two light nuclei together releases energy, since the resulting nucleus can sit higher
on the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve than its ingredients (the same curve, explained by the
liquid-drop model, that peaks around iron). But before that fusion can occur, the two positively
charged nuclei must first overcome their mutual Coulomb repulsion at very close range, which
requires extremely high kinetic energies — in practice, temperatures of tens to hundreds of
millions of degrees, at which matter no longer exists as a gas but as plasma: a soup of
free-moving ions and electrons. Creating and sustaining conditions this extreme is
substantially harder than sustaining a fission chain reaction, which is precisely why fusion
power, despite fusion fuel's greater abundance and lower waste output in principle, has proven
so much harder to achieve as a practical energy source than fission.

The Lawson criterion formalizes what a fusion reactor needs to achieve net energy gain: the
product of the plasma's particle density, its energy confinement time (how long the plasma
retains its heat before losing it), and its temperature (the "fusion triple product") must
exceed a threshold value for the fusion reactions to release more energy than is needed to
create and sustain the plasma. This is a combined condition, not a temperature threshold
alone — a plasma that is hot enough but too dilute, or hot enough but loses its heat too
quickly (too short a confinement time), still fails to reach net gain, which is why fusion
research programs invest heavily in both heating methods and confinement strategies rather
than temperature alone.

Two fundamentally different engineering strategies address the confinement problem. Magnetic
confinement, most prominently in the tokamak design, uses strong magnetic fields shaped into a
torus to hold a relatively low-density plasma in place continuously for a comparatively long
confinement time (potentially indefinitely, in principle), trading lower density for longer
confinement. Inertial confinement instead compresses a small pellet of fusion fuel to
extremely high density using powerful lasers (or other intense energy delivery) converging
from multiple directions, aiming for fusion conditions to be reached and the reaction to occur
before the compressed fuel has time to fly apart under its own pressure — relying on the
fuel's own inertia, rather than any external confining field, to hold it together for the
brief window fusion requires. Both approaches remain active areas of ongoing research and
engineering development toward a practical, net-energy-positive fusion power plant.
