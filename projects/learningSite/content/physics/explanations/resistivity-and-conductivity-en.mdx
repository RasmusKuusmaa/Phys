Resistance is a property of one specific object — this particular wire, this
particular resistor. Resistivity is a property of the material it is made
from, independent of shape or size, the way density is independent of how much
of a substance you have. Copper has one resistivity wherever it appears; a
copper wire's resistance depends on that resistivity together with its
specific length and cross-section.

The relationship is R = ρL/A: resistance grows in direct proportion to length,
and falls in inverse proportion to cross-sectional area. Both directions have
an intuitive reading once stated. A longer wire gives charge carriers a longer
obstacle course to push through, so resistance rises with length — the opposite
of the common guess that more metal must mean less resistance. A wider wire
gives more parallel lanes for current to flow through at once, so resistance
falls as area grows; halving the area is like closing half the lanes on a
road.

Conductivity is simply the reciprocal, σ = 1/ρ: a material that resists weakly
conducts strongly, and vice versa. Metals have low resistivity and are
excellent conductors; rubber and glass have enormous resistivity and are used
as insulators for exactly that reason. Resistivity also depends on
temperature — for most metals it rises as the metal heats, since the ions
vibrate more and scatter the flowing electrons more often, which is part of
why a light bulb's filament resistance is much higher glowing than cold.
