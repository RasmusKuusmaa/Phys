Recombination let photons decouple from matter and stream freely for the first time, producing the cosmic microwave background, and left the universe's ordinary matter as an almost perfectly neutral gas of hydrogen and helium. For several hundred million years afterward -- the cosmic dark ages -- that gas simply existed, gravitationally assembling into the first structures under dark matter's guidance, but with no luminous stars or galaxies yet formed to light it up. The universe was "dark" in the sense of having no visible light sources, not in the sense of being empty; it was full of gas quietly collapsing toward the first galaxies.

Reionization is what ended the dark ages. As the first stars, galaxies, and possibly early accreting black holes switched on, they flooded their surroundings with ultraviolet radiation energetic enough to strip the electron back off nearby neutral hydrogen atoms, creating an expanding bubble of ionized gas around each source. Unlike recombination, which was a smooth transition of the whole universe driven by the overall cooling of cosmic expansion, reionization proceeded patchily and gradually: individual ionized bubbles grew outward from the earliest, most vigorously star-forming regions, and only over hundreds of millions of years did these bubbles expand enough to overlap, merge, and eventually reionize essentially the entire intergalactic medium. Different regions completed this process at noticeably different times depending on the local abundance of early light sources.

Because reionization was driven by real astrophysical objects, it offers a direct probe of the very first generation of galaxies. Astronomers detect its imprint using the most distant known quasars as background lamps: neutral hydrogen still present along the line of sight absorbs a very specific wavelength of the quasar's light (the Lyman-alpha transition) so strongly that even a modest amount of remaining neutral gas produces a near-total absorption trough in the observed spectrum (the Gunn-Peterson trough), while gas that has already been reionized leaves no such trough; mapping how this absorption signature changes with the quasars' distance lets astronomers trace reionization's patchy progress directly, pinning down roughly when and how quickly the intergalactic medium's last neutral hydrogen was finally cleared away.
