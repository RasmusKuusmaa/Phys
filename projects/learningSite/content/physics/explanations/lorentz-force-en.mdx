A magnetic field only pushes on charge that is already moving, and it pushes
in a very specific direction: perpendicular to both the velocity and the
field itself. The magnitude is F = qvB sin θ, largest when velocity and field
are perpendicular and zero when they are parallel — a charge moving straight
along the field lines feels nothing at all, and a charge sitting still feels
nothing regardless of how strong the field is, since v = 0 makes the force
zero outright.

That perpendicularity has a consequence worth sitting with: a force always at
right angles to the motion can never do work, because work requires a
component of force along the direction of travel and there is none here. A
charged particle circling in a magnetic field moves at constant speed forever,
however long it circles — the magnetic force bends the path without ever
changing how fast the particle is going. This is exactly analogous to
uniform circular motion under gravity or tension: the centripetal force turns
the direction of velocity without touching its magnitude.

Because the force continuously redirects the velocity while never changing its
size, the natural path is a circle, with the magnetic force supplying exactly
the centripetal force needed: qvB = mv²/r. If the particle also has a velocity
component along the field, that component is unaffected — nothing perpendicular
to it acts on it — so the full motion is a circle superimposed on steady
motion along the field, a helix. This is the physics behind mass spectrometers,
cyclotrons, and the auroras: charged particles from the solar wind spiral
along Earth's magnetic field lines toward the poles rather than travelling
straight through space.
