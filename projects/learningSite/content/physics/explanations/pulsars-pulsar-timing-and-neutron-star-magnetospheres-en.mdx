A pulsar is a rapidly rotating neutron star with an extremely strong magnetic field, typically
misaligned with its rotation axis. Charged particles accelerated along the magnetic field lines
near the magnetic poles emit a continuous, tightly beamed stream of radiation (commonly radio
waves, though some pulsars emit strongly at X-ray or gamma-ray energies too), and because the
magnetic and rotation axes are misaligned, this beam sweeps around in a cone as the star rotates,
much like a lighthouse beam sweeping across the horizon. An observer only sees a pulse of
emission when that beam happens to sweep directly across their line of sight once per rotation —
the underlying emission is continuous and unchanging, and the "pulsing" is purely a geometric
effect of beam orientation relative to the observer, not an actual variation in emitted
brightness. Consequently, many neutron stars whose beams never sweep across Earth's line of
sight exist but are simply not observable as pulsars at all, despite being physically identical
kinds of object.

A neutron star's rotation, once established, is extraordinarily stable, since there is very
little to disturb it aside from a slow, gradual spin-down due to the beamed emission carrying
away rotational energy and angular momentum (and occasional abrupt "glitches" in some pulsars,
thought to arise from internal superfluid dynamics). The most stable millisecond pulsars rival
the best atomic clocks in long-term timing stability, which makes precision pulsar timing an
extraordinarily sensitive astrophysical measurement technique: tiny, periodic variations in the
arrival times of successive pulses reveal the presence of orbiting companions (binary pulsars,
including systems with planet-mass companions detected purely through their gravitational tug on
the pulsar's timing), let general relativity's predictions be tested to extraordinary precision
in strong-field binary pulsar systems, and, through pulsar timing arrays that monitor
correlated timing variations across many pulsars simultaneously, allow the detection of an
extremely low-frequency stochastic gravitational-wave background thought to arise from the
superposition of countless supermassive black hole binary mergers throughout the universe's
history.
