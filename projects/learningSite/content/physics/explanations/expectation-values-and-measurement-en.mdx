When a wavefunction is not an eigenstate of the quantity being measured — the
usual situation, since most physically realistic states are not perfectly
sharp in any one observable — quantum mechanics cannot predict what a single
measurement will return with certainty. What it predicts instead is the
*expectation value*: the average result that would emerge from measuring
that same quantity on a large number of identically prepared systems, each
outcome weighted by its quantum-mechanical probability. This is calculated
directly from the wavefunction and the relevant operator, and it is a genuine,
well-defined prediction — just not a prediction about any one measurement.

Confusing the expectation value with the outcome of a single measurement is
the most common misreading of this idea. Any individual measurement returns
one specific eigenvalue of the operator, chosen probabilistically, not the
expectation value itself — which typically is not even one of the possible
eigenvalues at all, in the same way that an average die roll of 3.5 is not a
value any single roll can produce. Measure many identically prepared systems
and the *average* of all those individual, eigenvalue-only outcomes converges
on the expectation value; no single measurement needs to land anywhere near
it.

Measurement in quantum mechanics is also not a passive read-out of a
pre-existing value; performing it changes the system. Before measurement, the
wavefunction may be spread across many possible outcomes; immediately after a
measurement returns a particular eigenvalue, the wavefunction is found to have
collapsed onto the corresponding eigenstate — a genuinely different state from
whatever it was before. This is why identically prepared systems, sharing the
same pre-measurement wavefunction, can still yield different individual
results: the measurement is not revealing a hidden pre-existing answer, it is
part of what determines the outcome.
