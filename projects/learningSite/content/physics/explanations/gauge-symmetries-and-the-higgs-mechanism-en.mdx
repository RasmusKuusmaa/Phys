A global symmetry requires a theory to look the same under some transformation applied
identically everywhere in space at once; a local (gauge) symmetry demands the theory look
the same even when that transformation is applied independently, differently, at every
single point in spacetime. Upgrading a global symmetry to a local one is a genuinely
stronger requirement, and it cannot be satisfied by the original matter fields alone — it
forces the introduction of a new field, a gauge field, whose job is exactly to compensate
for the point-to-point variation and restore the symmetry; the photon, and the W, Z and
gluon fields, are all gauge fields required by promoting a symmetry of the Standard Model to
a local one.

A naive gauge field of this kind is necessarily massless — a mass term for it in the
theory's Lagrangian would itself violate the very gauge symmetry that required the field to
exist in the first place. This is exactly right for the photon (genuinely massless) and the
gluons (also massless), but experimentally the W and Z bosons that carry the weak force are
anything but massless — among the heaviest known elementary particles — creating a direct
conflict between the gauge symmetry the weak interaction is built on and the masses those
force carriers are observed to have.

The Higgs mechanism resolves this without simply abandoning the gauge symmetry: it
introduces a new field, the Higgs field, that (unlike the vacuum of empty space one might
naively picture) has a nonzero value even in its lowest-energy state, filling all of space
uniformly. The W and Z bosons acquire mass through their interaction with this ever-present
background field — the symmetry of the underlying equations is still fully intact, it is
only the particular vacuum state the universe actually sits in that fails to display that
symmetry obviously (a "spontaneously broken" symmetry), and the residual excitation of the
Higgs field itself, discovered at the LHC in 2012, is the Higgs boson.
