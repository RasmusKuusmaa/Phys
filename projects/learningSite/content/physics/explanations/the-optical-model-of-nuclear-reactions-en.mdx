The compound nucleus model describes one important limit of nuclear reaction behavior well: an incoming particle is captured entirely, its energy shared and thoroughly randomized among all the target nucleus's nucleons, forming a long-lived intermediate state that eventually decays with no memory of exactly how it formed. But a substantial fraction of incident particles, particularly at higher energies, never form this equilibrated compound state at all -- they scatter directly and elastically off the nucleus's surface region, retaining most of their original energy and direction, a fast process the compound-nucleus picture cannot describe.

The optical model fills exactly this gap by deliberately borrowing its mathematics from classical optics. Rather than tracking every one of the target nucleus's many individual nucleons, it replaces the entire complicated many-body system with a single, smoothly-varying effective potential well, and treats the incoming particle as moving through that potential the way a single particle moves through an external field -- precisely analogous to how light passing through a partially transparent, refracting medium like cloudy glass can be described by one effective refractive index rather than by tracking every individual molecule the light interacts with. Crucially, the model's potential is complex-valued: the real part describes ordinary elastic refraction and scattering, while the imaginary part plays the role of an absorption coefficient, describing the rate at which incoming particles are genuinely removed from the elastic-scattering channel and diverted into every other open reaction channel -- compound-nucleus formation among them, but also inelastic scattering, particle transfer, and more.

The optical model and the compound nucleus model are therefore not competing explanations of the same physics but a genuine division of labour describing two different, coexisting reaction mechanisms. A handful of adjustable optical potential parameters (depth, radius, surface diffuseness, absorption strength), fit once to reproduce a nucleus's observed elastic-scattering data, let the same model successfully predict total reaction cross-sections and the strength of various reaction channels across a wide range of energies and target nuclei. Together, the two models span nuclear reaction behavior from fast, direct elastic scattering (the optical model's domain) to slow, fully equilibrated compound-nucleus decay (the compound-nucleus model's domain), giving nuclear physics a complete picture rather than requiring either model to explain everything alone.
