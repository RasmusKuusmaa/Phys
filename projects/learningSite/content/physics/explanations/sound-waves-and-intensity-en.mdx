Sound is a mechanical pressure wave: a source vibrates, pushing neighbouring
molecules together and pulling them apart in a travelling pattern of
compressions and rarefactions. That mechanism is exactly why sound cannot
cross a vacuum — with no molecules present, there is nothing to compress and
nothing to pass the disturbance along, which is why explosions in space films
are dramatic license rather than physics. The speed at which sound travels
also depends entirely on the medium's own stiffness and density, not on how
"thin" it feels: sound moves faster through denser, stiffer media, roughly
340 m/s in air but around 1500 m/s in water and over 5000 m/s in steel, the
reverse of the everyday intuition that thin media should be faster.

Intensity measures how much power a wave delivers per unit area, and for a
small source radiating equally in all directions, that power spreads out over
the surface of an ever-expanding sphere as distance grows. Since a sphere's
surface area grows as r², the same total power spread over a bigger and
bigger area means intensity falls as 1/r² — an inverse-square law, the same
mathematical shape met in gravity and in the electric field of a point
charge, for exactly the same underlying reason: something spreading undiminished
through three-dimensional space.

That inverse-square falloff is why doubling your distance from a speaker
does not merely halve the loudness you experience — it quarters the
intensity. Human hearing itself is roughly logarithmic in its response to
intensity, which is why sound levels are reported in decibels rather than raw
intensity units, but the physical intensity reaching your ear obeys the plain
inverse-square law regardless of how your ear or brain then interprets it.
