A Michelson interferometer takes a single beam of light and splits it with a
half-silvered mirror into two perpendicular paths, sends each half to a
separate mirror some distance away, and recombines the two returning beams
back into one. The recombined light interferes, and whether that interference
is constructive or destructive at any point depends entirely on the
difference in the total distance each half travelled — the path-length
difference between the two arms.

That dependence is what makes the instrument extraordinarily sensitive.
Because visible light has a wavelength of only a few hundred nanometres,
shifting one mirror by even a small fraction of that distance measurably
shifts the interference pattern — moving bright and dark fringes across a
detector in a way that can be counted and converted directly into a length
change far too small to measure by any mechanical ruler. This sensitivity is
why interferometers of this general design remain the basis of some of the
most precise length and distance measurements made today, up to and including
the kilometre-scale detectors that first observed gravitational waves by
registering length changes thousands of times smaller than a proton.

The instrument's most famous historical use was Michelson and Morley's 1887
experiment, which compared the travel time of light sent along two
perpendicular arms as Earth moved through the presumed "luminiferous ether."
If light's speed depended on Earth's motion through that medium, rotating the
apparatus should have shifted the interference pattern measurably. It did
not — a null result, but a highly informative one rather than a failure: the
absence of any detectable ether wind was a direct experimental clue that light
propagates at the same speed regardless of an observer's motion, one of the
key pieces of evidence that fed directly into Einstein's postulates of special
relativity less than two decades later.
