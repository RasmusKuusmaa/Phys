The centre of mass is the single point that represents the average position
of a system's mass, each part weighted by how much mass it contributes:
x_cm = (m₁x₁ + m₂x₂ + …)/(m₁ + m₂ + …). For two equal masses it sits exactly
halfway between them, but change the masses and it shifts toward the heavier
one — a small moon and a large planet have their mutual centre of mass much
closer to the planet's centre than to the midpoint between them.

Nothing requires the centre of mass to sit on any actual material. A ring or
a hollow sphere has uniform mass only around its rim or shell, and by
symmetry its centre of mass sits at the geometric centre — empty space,
with no mass there at all. The definition is purely about the weighted
average of position, not about where matter happens to be.

The reason the centre of mass earns a name of its own, rather than being a
mere bookkeeping convenience, is how it moves: exactly as if the system's
entire mass were a single point particle, subject only to the *external*
forces acting on the system as a whole. Internal forces — a spring pushing
two blocks apart, a collision between billiard balls, an exploding
firework — always come in equal and opposite pairs by Newton's third law, so
their effects on the centre of mass cancel exactly, whatever chaos unfolds
between the individual parts. A firework that explodes in mid-air scatters
fragments in every direction, yet their combined centre of mass keeps falling
along the same smooth parabola the unexploded shell would have followed,
right up until a fragment hits the ground and an genuinely external force
finally acts.
