A particle in a box has discrete energy levels, and the smaller the box the
further apart they are. A bulk crystal is an enormous box, so its levels are
packed so densely that they merge into continuous bands. Shrink the crystal
towards a few nanometres, though, and the box becomes small enough for the
spacing to matter again: the levels near the band edges separate out, the gap
between the highest filled and lowest empty state widens, and the material starts
to behave partly like a very large molecule. This is quantum confinement, and the
scale at which it sets in is the size an electron-hole pair would naturally
occupy in that material — the exciton Bohr radius — which is a few nanometres in
common semiconductors and is why it is a nanoscale phenomenon specifically.

The visible consequence is that colour becomes a matter of size. Cadmium selenide
dots of two nanometres emit blue, and the same compound at six emits red, with
no chemical difference between the vials. That tunability is the point of
quantum-dot displays and of the fluorescent labels used in biology, and it is
also a useful diagnostic: if a nanoparticle's colour tracks its size, confinement
is at work, whereas gold nanoparticles get their colour from a plasmon resonance
instead and behave quite differently.

Confining motion in only some directions gives the other members of the family: a
quantum well confines one direction and leaves a two-dimensional sheet of free
motion, a wire leaves one, a dot leaves none. What matters about each is less the
number of states than how they are distributed in energy. The bulk density of
states climbs smoothly from the band edge; the well's rises in steps, the wire's
spikes at each subband, and the dot's collapses into sharp lines. Concentrating
states at the band edge rather than spreading them over a range is exactly what a
laser wants, which is why essentially every semiconductor laser made today is a
quantum-well device, and it is the same reasoning that drives interest in
two-dimensional materials like graphene and the transition-metal dichalcogenides.
