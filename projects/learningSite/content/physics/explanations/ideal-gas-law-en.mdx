The ideal gas law ties four properties of a gas together into one equation:
pressure times volume equals the amount of substance times a constant times
the absolute temperature. Change any one of them, and at least one of the
others has to respond.

Because it's one equation linking four quantities, holding one of them fixed
turns it into a familiar, simpler relationship. Keep temperature constant
and it becomes Boyle's law — pressure and volume trade off against each
other. Keep pressure constant and it becomes Charles's law — volume and
temperature move together. The ideal gas law is really the parent of all
these special cases.

Temperature here must be measured on an absolute scale — kelvin, not
Celsius — because the law is really about the average kinetic energy of the
gas particles, and that energy is naturally zero at absolute zero, not at
the arbitrary zero point of the Celsius scale.
