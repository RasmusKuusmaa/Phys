A physical quantity is always a number paired with a unit — "5" on its own
means nothing in physics, but "5 metres" describes something real. Dropping
the unit doesn't just lose detail, it loses the entire meaning.

The SI system fixes seven base units — the metre, kilogram, second, ampere,
kelvin, mole and candela — and builds every other unit from combinations of
those seven. A newton, for instance, is just a shorthand for kilogram times
metre per second squared; writing out force in base units instead of
newtons would work exactly the same, only more clumsily.

Keeping units attached through a calculation is also a built-in error
checker: if a formula for a speed spits out an answer in kilograms, something
has gone wrong in the algebra, unit mismatches are one of the fastest ways to
catch a mistake before it becomes a wrong final answer.
