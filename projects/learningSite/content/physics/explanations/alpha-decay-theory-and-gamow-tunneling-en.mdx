An alpha particle (a helium-4 nucleus, two protons and two neutrons bound together) inside a
heavy nucleus experiences a potential energy landscape with two competing pieces: the short-range
strong nuclear force holds it inside the nucleus, while the long-range electrostatic Coulomb
repulsion between the alpha particle's positive charge and the remaining nucleus's positive
charge pushes outward, creating a substantial energy barrier the alpha particle would face
immediately upon leaving the nucleus's short-range attractive region. For essentially every
observed alpha-emitting nucleus, this barrier's peak height significantly exceeds the alpha
particle's actual kinetic energy inside the nucleus, meaning that classically the alpha particle
could never escape at all — it simply does not have enough energy to climb over the barrier.

Alpha decay nonetheless happens routinely, and George Gamow's 1928 explanation (developed
independently around the same time by Ronald Gurney and Edward Condon) resolved this puzzle by
applying the newly developed quantum mechanics: rather than needing enough energy to classically
pass over the barrier, the alpha particle has a small but strictly nonzero quantum mechanical
probability of tunneling directly through it on each "attempt" to escape (loosely pictured as the
alpha particle bouncing against the barrier's inner wall repeatedly). This was one of the very
first successful applications of quantum mechanics to a real, previously unexplained nuclear
phenomenon, providing striking early evidence that quantum tunneling was a genuine physical
effect rather than a mathematical curiosity confined to idealized toy problems.

The tunneling probability through a barrier depends, via the same WKB-approximation machinery
used for quantum tunneling generally, exponentially on the barrier's height and width relative to
the particle's energy — and this exponential sensitivity is exactly what explains one of alpha
decay's most striking experimental features: observed half-lives span an almost unimaginable
range, from microseconds to far longer than the age of the universe, roughly thirty orders of
magnitude, even though the corresponding alpha particle energies vary only modestly, typically by
no more than a factor of a few. A relatively small increase in the alpha particle's energy
significantly thins the effective barrier width it must tunnel through, producing an
exponentially larger tunneling probability and hence a dramatically shorter half-life — the
Geiger-Nuttall law, an empirical relationship between alpha decay energy and half-life discovered
before Gamow's theoretical explanation, falls directly out of this exponential tunneling
dependence, providing a striking quantitative confirmation of the theory.
