The density fluctuations tracked by cosmological perturbation theory did not grow into today's galaxies all at once. Instead, structure formation proceeds hierarchically: because smaller-scale density enhancements cross the threshold for gravitational collapse earliest, the smallest dark-matter clumps collapse first, and these small halos then merge repeatedly over billions of years, building up progressively larger dark-matter halos. Ordinary (baryonic) gas follows the same gravitational potential wells the dark matter creates, falling in, radiatively cooling, sinking toward each halo's center, and condensing into stars there -- the actual visible galaxy sitting at the center of a much larger, invisible dark-matter halo. A large galaxy observed today is therefore the accumulated product of a long assembly history of smaller building blocks, not something that formed complete and unchanging from the start.

Edwin Hubble organized the resulting zoo of galaxy shapes into his famous "tuning fork" classification scheme: spiral galaxies, with flattened, rotating disks of gas and stars often organized into striking spiral arms, and elliptical galaxies, smooth, roughly ellipsoidal systems with comparatively little ongoing star formation. The physical distinction runs deeper than shape alone: spiral disks are rotation-supported, meaning ordered orbital motion in one dominant direction holds the disk up against gravity, while elliptical galaxies are pressure-supported, meaning random, largely disordered stellar orbits in many different directions provide the supporting "pressure" instead, much like the random thermal motion of gas molecules supports a star against its own gravity.

Major mergers -- collisions between two galaxies of comparable mass -- are understood as the dominant mechanism that converts one morphological type into the other. Such a merger violently disrupts both galaxies' orderly rotating disks, gravitationally scrambling the stars' orbits into the random, disordered motion characteristic of an elliptical galaxy; simulations and observations of ongoing mergers both support this picture directly. Mergers also typically drive remaining cold gas inward in a burst of star formation and can fuel the central supermassive black hole, often consuming or expelling most of the galaxy's remaining gas reservoir in the process -- which is why elliptical galaxies, having usually undergone this violent history, tend to host old stellar populations with little gas left to form new stars, while spiral galaxies, having largely avoided major mergers, retain the cold gas reservoirs that keep new star formation going in their disks today.
