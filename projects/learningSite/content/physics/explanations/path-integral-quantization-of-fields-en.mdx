Feynman's path integral formulation of ordinary quantum mechanics computes a transition
amplitude by summing the complex phase exp(iS/hbar) over every possible trajectory a particle
could take between two points, where S is the classical action of that trajectory — trajectories
close to the classical path interfere constructively, while wildly different ones tend to cancel,
recovering classical mechanics as an emergent limit. Quantum field theory generalizes this same
idea from a single particle's trajectory (a function of one variable, time) to an entire field
configuration (a function of every point in spacetime): the path integral now sums over every
possible way the field could be configured throughout all of space and time, weighted by
exp(iS[field]/hbar), where S is now the action functional of the entire field history. This is
a genuine conceptual leap, not merely a bigger calculation — it involves integrating over a
space with infinitely many degrees of freedom (the field's value at every spacetime point)
rather than the comparatively modest number a particle's trajectory has.

The central computational object built from this integral is the generating functional, which
packages every correlation function (vacuum expectation values of products of field operators
at different spacetime points) of the theory into a single object, obtained by adding a source
term to the action and differentiating the resulting integral with respect to that source.
Expanding the generating functional perturbatively in the interaction strength produces exactly
the sum of Feynman diagrams a particle physicist computes with: each diagram represents one term
in that expansion, propagators arise from the free (non-interacting) part of the path integral,
and vertices arise directly from the interaction terms in the action, with symmetry factors and
combinatorial coefficients falling out of the expansion's bookkeeping automatically. The path
integral is therefore not an alternative to Feynman diagrams that happens to agree with them —
it is the object the diagrams are systematically derived from, the actual computational
foundation underneath every scattering amplitude and cross-section calculation.

Despite its enormous practical success, the path integral for interacting quantum field
theories has never been given a fully rigorous mathematical foundation — a genuine, well-defined
measure over the infinite-dimensional space of field configurations for an interacting theory
remains, to this day, an open problem in mathematical physics. In practice, physicists work
around this through well-tested formal techniques: perturbative expansion order by order,
analytic continuation to imaginary (Euclidean) time where the integral behaves better, and
lattice discretization, which replaces continuous spacetime with a finite grid, making the
integral genuinely finite-dimensional and numerically computable, at the cost of introducing an
artificial lattice spacing that must be removed at the end of the calculation.
