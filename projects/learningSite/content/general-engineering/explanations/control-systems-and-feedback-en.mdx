Every controller falls into one of two families based on whether it looks at the result of its own action. An open-loop controller commands an action and stops there -- a microwave running for a fixed time, an irrigation system watering on a timer -- with no sensor telling it whether the food actually got hot or the soil actually got wet. This is a perfectly good engineering choice when the plant's behavior is predictable and disturbances are small, since it is cheaper, simpler, and structurally cannot become unstable the way a feedback loop can. A closed-loop controller instead measures its own output, subtracts it from the desired setpoint to get an error signal, and feeds that error back into the actuator -- which is what lets a cruise control hold speed climbing a hill or a thermostat hold temperature with the door opening and closing, situations an open-loop timer could never handle correctly.

The PID control law is the standard recipe for turning an error signal into a corrective action, built from three terms that each fix a different weakness of the others. The proportional term reacts to the error's present size, giving an immediate, obvious response -- but a proportional term alone typically leaves a small permanent offset for any system fighting a constant disturbance, since its own corrective action shrinks to nothing exactly as the error does. The integral term fixes this by accumulating the error over time, so even a tiny sustained error keeps growing the corrective action until the offset is finally driven out. The derivative term looks instead at how fast the error is changing, applying a braking action that anticipates and damps out overshoot before the proportional and integral terms drive the system past the setpoint. Real controllers tune the relative weight of these three terms (the gains) to the specific plant being controlled, not to some universal ideal ratio.

Feedback's central trap is that it is not free of cost: a control loop's own gain has to respect the physical system's delay and inertia, or the loop stops correcting and starts fighting itself. Push a thermostat's gain high enough relative to how slowly a room's air actually changes temperature and the system will overshoot the setpoint, undershoot correcting for the overshoot, and settle into a sustained oscillation -- or, pushed further, diverge outright. This is why "just add more feedback, or a bigger gain" is not a universal fix: every real feedback loop has a stability margin set by the plant it controls, and staying inside that margin is as much a part of control-system design as choosing the control law's mathematical form in the first place.
