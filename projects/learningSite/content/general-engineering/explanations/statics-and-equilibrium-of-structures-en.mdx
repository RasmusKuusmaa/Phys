Every rigid body problem in statics starts from the same two physical facts: forces add like vectors, and turning effects (moments) add like signed scalars about any chosen point. Together, "net force is zero" and "net moment about any point is zero" are necessary and sufficient for a body to remain motionless -- not approximately motionless, but exactly, which is why they translate directly into equations rather than rules of thumb. In two dimensions this pair of vector conditions expands into exactly three independent scalar equations (sum Fx = 0, sum Fy = 0, sum M = 0), and that number three is not arbitrary: it is exactly how many unknown reaction components a support system needs to provide for the structure to be both fully constrained and solvable by statics alone.

The free-body diagram is what turns this abstract count into an actual calculation. Isolating one body -- a whole truss, a single beam, or even one pin joint -- and replacing everything it touches (the ground, another member, a cable) with the force that thing actually exerts on the isolated body converts a tangle of physical contact into a clean list of vectors an equilibrium equation can be written about. Get this step wrong -- include a force the body doesn't actually feel, or omit one it does -- and the three equations that follow are solving the wrong problem, however carefully the arithmetic afterward is done.

Trusses add one more piece of structure to lean on: every member is a two-force member, loaded only at its two pinned ends, which means its internal force must point exactly along the member's own axis -- there is no other direction available for a two-force member to push or pull in. That constraint is what makes the method of joints (writing the two force equations at each pin, where every unknown member force is already known to point along a known direction) and the method of sections (cutting cleanly through select members and writing equilibrium for the resulting free half of the truss) solvable at all, and why the answer to "is this member in tension or compression" is simply the sign that falls out of the arithmetic, not a separate judgment call.
