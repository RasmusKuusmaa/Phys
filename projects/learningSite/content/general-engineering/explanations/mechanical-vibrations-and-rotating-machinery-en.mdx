The simplest useful model of a vibrating machine element is a single mass connected to a single spring: a lumped mass m representing the rotor (or blade, or any vibrating component) and a lumped stiffness k representing the elastic restoring force of the shaft or support that holds it. Disturbed and released, this idealised system oscillates at its natural frequency, f_n = (1/2*pi)*sqrt(k/m) -- a genuinely different frequency from the rotational speed the machine is actually driven at, though the two can coincide. When the driving rotational speed matches this natural frequency, the shaft's critical speed, the system is driven at resonance: even the tiny, unavoidable mass imbalance every real manufactured rotor has (its centre of mass never sits exactly on its rotation axis) now excites vibration amplitudes many times larger than the same imbalance would produce running well away from the critical speed, because resonance is precisely the condition under which a small periodic forcing input builds up a large sustained response.

This matters because a shaft can pass a comfortable static-strength check -- its stress under load, computed the way mechanics of materials computes it, sits well below yield with a healthy factor of safety -- and still fail rapidly in service, because that static calculation says nothing about the cyclic stress amplitude resonance-driven vibration actually produces. Repeated cyclic stress at resonance-amplified amplitude drives fatigue crack initiation and growth far faster than the same nominal load would running away from resonance, which is exactly why rotating-machinery design treats critical speed as a design constraint in its own right, not a detail that a sufficient static factor of safety automatically covers: the operating speed range is deliberately chosen to sit safely clear of the critical speed, or, when a machine must pass through a critical speed to reach its operating range, it is accelerated through that speed quickly rather than run there continuously.

Because some residual mass imbalance is unavoidable in any real manufactured rotor, not a sign of a manufacturing defect to be eliminated entirely, dynamic balancing is standard practice rather than an optional refinement: a rotor is spun on a balancing machine that measures the vibration its residual imbalance produces, and small trim masses are added or removed at calculated positions until that measured vibration drops to an acceptable level. Where an application genuinely cannot avoid operating near a resonance (a wide operating speed range that must include a critical speed, for instance), vibration isolators -- compliant mounts placed between the vibrating machine and the surrounding structure -- reduce how much of the vibrating force actually transmits outward, protecting the supporting structure and neighbouring equipment even though the machine element itself still vibrates.
