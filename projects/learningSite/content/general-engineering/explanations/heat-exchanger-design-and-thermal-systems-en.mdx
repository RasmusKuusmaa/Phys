Sizing a heat exchanger comes down to one relationship: heat duty equals an overall heat transfer coefficient, times the heat-transfer area, times a driving temperature difference. The overall coefficient lumps together every resistance heat has to cross -- convection from the hot fluid to the wall, conduction through the wall itself, convection from the wall to the cold fluid -- into one number, so the whole exchanger's thermal performance can be captured without tracking each resistance separately. The area is whatever physical surface the two fluids share, directly or through fins.

The driving temperature difference is the subtle part. The gap between the hot and cold streams is not constant along the exchanger's length: heat crossing from hot to cold shrinks that gap continuously as the fluids travel through, so neither the inlet gap nor the outlet gap alone describes the exchanger, and a plain average of the two systematically overstates the real driving force because the gap doesn't shrink linearly. The log-mean temperature difference (LMTD) is exactly the temperature difference that correctly accounts for this continuous shrinkage, derived directly from the inlet and outlet gaps but weighted logarithmically rather than arithmetically.

Flow arrangement changes how large that gap stays. In a counterflow exchanger, the two streams run in opposite directions, so the hot stream's cooling end sits next to the cold stream's warming end throughout -- a temperature difference that stays comparatively large along the whole length. In a parallel-flow exchanger, both streams move the same direction, starting with the largest possible gap at the shared inlet end and converging toward each other by the shared outlet, which pinches the driving difference down faster overall. For the same area and coefficient, counterflow's higher LMTD means it always transfers at least as much heat, which is why counterflow is the default choice wherever the physical layout allows it.

Extending the exchanger's surface with fins looks like free extra area, but a fin conducts heat along its own length while losing some of it to the surrounding fluid the whole way, so the fin's temperature drops from base to tip. A long or poorly-conducting fin's tip ends up close to the fluid's own temperature and contributes almost nothing, which is why fin effectiveness -- actual heat transferred divided by what the same area would transfer at the base temperature -- is always below the naive "extra area, extra heat" expectation, never equal to it.
