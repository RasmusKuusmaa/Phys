A robot arm is a clean illustration of what mechatronics actually means: it is not a mechanism with sensors and control bolted on afterward, but a single system where the mechanical linkage (rigid links connected by revolute or prismatic joints, exactly the kind of mechanism kinematics-and-dynamics-of-mechanisms already describes), the actuators driving each joint, the sensors measuring each joint's actual angle, and the control loop commanding each actuator are all designed together, because each piece's requirements depend on the others -- an actuator must be sized for the torque the mechanism and its expected load demand, and a sensor must resolve position finely enough for the control loop to actually achieve the required accuracy.

Forward kinematics is the direct geometric calculation: given every joint's current angle, straightforward trigonometry (built from each link's length and each joint's angle) computes exactly where the end effector sits in space, and this calculation always has exactly one answer, since a specific set of joint angles places the mechanism in exactly one configuration. Inverse kinematics asks the opposite, and practically more useful, question -- given a desired end-effector position, what joint angles achieve it -- and this is not guaranteed to have a single clean answer the way forward kinematics does: a simple two-link planar arm reaching for a given point typically has two valid solutions (an 'elbow up' and an 'elbow down' configuration, both placing the end effector at the same point through different joint angles), a fully-extended arm reaching for a point at the very edge of its workspace may have exactly one, and a point genuinely outside the arm's reach has none at all. A robot's motion planner has to handle all three of these cases, not assume inverse kinematics behaves like forward kinematics.

None of this geometry moves anything on its own, which is where actuators and sensors and the control loop connecting them come in. Actuators convert some other form of input energy into the mechanical force or torque that actually drives a joint -- electric motors are the most common in robotics, but hydraulic cylinders (high force density, common in heavy machinery), pneumatic cylinders (fast and simple but less precisely controllable), and piezoelectric actuators (extremely fine positioning at very small displacements) are all actuators too, chosen for different combinations of force, speed, and precision. Sensors -- encoders measuring joint angle being the most direct example -- report what the mechanism is actually doing, and closing a feedback loop around that measurement (the same open-loop-versus-closed-loop distinction control-systems-and-feedback introduces) is what lets the controller continuously correct for friction, unmodelled load variation, and manufacturing tolerance that would otherwise make the mechanism's actual motion drift away from its commanded motion; without that closed loop, the mechanism executes whatever the actuator happens to do given its input, with no way to notice or correct an error.
