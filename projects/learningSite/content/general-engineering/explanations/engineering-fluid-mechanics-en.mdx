Bernoulli's equation, in its purest physics form, holds only along a streamline in an ideal fluid with no friction at all -- a useful starting point, but not directly usable for a real pipe network, where friction against the pipe wall is exactly the thing an engineer needs to size a pump for. Engineering fluid mechanics keeps Bernoulli's basic energy bookkeeping -- pressure, elevation, and velocity all convertible into a common currency of "head" (mechanical energy per unit weight of fluid, expressed as an equivalent height) -- but adds a head-loss term between any two points in the system to account for exactly that friction, turning an idealized conservation law into a design equation a real pipe run can be checked against.

The Darcy-Weisbach equation is the standard way to quantify that head loss for flow through a straight pipe: head loss is proportional to the pipe's length, inversely proportional to its diameter, and -- the detail most worth internalizing -- proportional to the square of the flow velocity, not the velocity itself. That squared dependence means friction losses grow much faster than flow rate does: doubling the flow through a given pipe doesn't double the head loss, it roughly quadruples it, and since the pump power needed to overcome that loss is itself proportional to flow rate times head, pumping power ends up scaling with roughly the cube of velocity -- which is exactly why real piping systems are sized generously rather than run at the smallest diameter that technically fits the required flow.

Real systems also lose head at every fitting, valve, elbow, and sudden change in pipe diameter along the way -- so-called "minor losses", a name that undersells how large they can add up to be in a pipe run with many fittings -- each modeled with its own loss coefficient multiplying the same velocity-squared term the pipe-friction loss uses. Adding up major losses (pipe friction) and minor losses (fittings) across a whole system gives the total head a pump has to supply, which -- combined with the required flow rate -- is exactly the information a pump or turbine's own performance curve is used to select a matching machine against.
