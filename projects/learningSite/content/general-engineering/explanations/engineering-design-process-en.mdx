An engineering design process that never has to revise anything after the first attempt isn't a sign of unusually good engineering -- it's a sign that the design either wasn't tested rigorously enough to find its real problems, or was massively overengineered against risks that never needed that much margin in the first place. The whole point of the design-build-test-revise loop is that some failure modes only reveal themselves under conditions a calculation alone won't fully capture -- a manufacturing tolerance stacking up unfavorably, a load case nobody modeled, an assembly interaction between two otherwise-fine parts -- and each pass through the loop is expected to surface at least some of those before the design ever reaches production, not evidence that something went wrong the first time around.

Failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) is the tool that makes this proactive rather than reactive: instead of waiting for a prototype to fail and then explaining what happened, an FMEA is performed during the design phase, systematically walking through every plausible way each component of a system could fail and scoring each one on three separate axes -- how severe the consequence would be if it happened, how likely it is to happen at all, and how easily it would be detected before it caused real harm. Combining those scores gives a risk priority that tells the design team exactly which failure modes deserve engineering attention first, rather than spreading limited design effort evenly across every conceivable thing that could go wrong.

That risk analysis feeds directly into how a design's safety margins get set. A factor of safety or other explicit design margin is not simply "as large as possible" -- every unit of extra margin usually costs something: more material and mass in a structural part, more expensive components in an electrical system, slower response in a control system tuned overly conservatively -- so the design process weighs a given failure mode's severity and likelihood, as surfaced by the FMEA, against those costs, and sets a margin appropriate to that specific risk rather than defaulting to the largest number available. A part protecting against a catastrophic, hard-to-detect failure earns a much larger margin than one where failure is a minor inconvenience that's easy to catch and cheap to fix.
