Mechanics of materials gives closed-form stress and strain formulas for a handful of simple, idealised shapes -- a straight beam in pure bending, a bar loaded along its axis, a thin-walled pressure vessel. Almost nothing an engineer actually designs looks like one of those idealised shapes exactly: a bracket has fillets and holes, a pressure vessel has a nozzle welded into its side, a wheel has spokes. Finite element analysis handles this by giving up on finding one analytic formula for the whole irregular shape and instead cutting it into many small, simply-shaped pieces -- elements -- connected at shared points called nodes. Each individual element is simple enough that its own stiffness (how it deforms under a unit load applied at its nodes) is known exactly; assembling every element's small stiffness contribution into one large global stiffness matrix turns the whole irregular structure's behaviour into a large system of linear equations relating nodal forces to nodal displacements, which a computer solves numerically.

The mesh is only an approximation of the real, continuous structure, and that approximation has a direct, checkable consequence: the finer the mesh (more, smaller elements), the closer the numerical solution gets to the true continuum answer, but also the more computation it costs. Mesh convergence is the practice of solving the same model on progressively finer meshes and confirming that the quantity you actually care about -- typically the peak stress somewhere -- stops changing appreciably; only once that has happened is the number on the finest mesh actually trustworthy, rather than merely computable. Some features actively defeat this process: a mathematically sharp re-entrant corner (a perfectly sharp interior right angle, with zero fillet radius) produces a genuine stress singularity, where the theoretical stress diverges to infinity as the mesh refines rather than converging to a finite value -- a real geometric feature of the idealised model, not a real material behaviour, and a reminder that a model is a simplification of the real rounded corner that would actually be manufactured.

FEA slots into the iterative engineering design process as the analysis step that replaces hand calculation once a design's geometry is too irregular for one: propose a geometry, mesh and solve it, compare the predicted peak stress against the material's yield strength through a factor of safety, and revise the geometry if the margin isn't adequate -- the same loop mechanics of materials supports for simple shapes, extended to arbitrary ones. This power comes with a corresponding responsibility: because the software will solve whatever mesh and boundary conditions it's given without complaint, a result is only as trustworthy as the engineer's judgment about whether the mesh has converged, whether the boundary conditions actually represent how the part is really supported and loaded, and whether the resulting numbers are even the right order of magnitude -- exactly the sanity check that requires understanding the underlying mechanics of materials in the first place.
