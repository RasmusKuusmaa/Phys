An engineering material's strength number does not come from trust -- it comes from a standardized test whose procedure is agreed internationally before a single specimen is ever loaded. The tensile test is the workhorse: a dogbone-shaped specimen with a precisely machined gauge section is gripped at both ends and pulled apart at a controlled, specified rate while a load cell and an extensometer record force and elongation continuously. The resulting stress-strain curve carries far more information than a single strength number -- it shows the material's stiffness (the initial slope, Young's modulus), its yield point, and its ultimate tensile strength at the curve's highest point, all from one test. Because the specimen's cross-sectional area is fixed by the standard, the raw force reading converts directly to stress: ultimate tensile strength is simply the maximum force the specimen carried, divided by its original cross-sectional area.

Hardness testing exists because a tensile test destroys its specimen and takes real time to run, which is impractical for checking every part on a production line or verifying an existing structure without cutting a sample out of it. Rockwell, Brinell, and Vickers tests instead press a hardened indenter into the material's surface under a known load and measure how much it resists penetration -- fast, non-destructive, and repeatable, but only indirectly related to tensile strength through empirical conversion tables built up from testing many samples of a given material family side by side on both machines. Those tables are convenient, not fundamental: a conversion calibrated for steel says nothing reliable about aluminum or a polymer.

Impact testing asks an entirely different question than either of the above: not how much load a material carries slowly, but how much energy it can absorb in an instant, sudden fracture. A Charpy or Izod test swings a heavy pendulum into a notched specimen and measures how much kinetic energy the pendulum lost breaking it. This matters because toughness and strength can move in opposite directions -- some very strong materials are also brittle, absorbing almost no energy before fracturing, and many common metals become noticeably more brittle as temperature drops, a shift called the ductile-to-brittle transition that a room-temperature tensile test gives no warning of at all. A steel pressure vessel destined for an arctic pipeline needs its impact toughness verified at the coldest temperature it will actually see in service, not just its tensile strength at room temperature.

None of this testing infrastructure would mean anything without the standards bodies (ASTM, ISO, national standards like EN in Europe) that fix every detail of specimen geometry, loading rate, and test environment in a published, citable document. That standardization is what makes a strength number portable: a value measured in a lab in one country is directly comparable to one measured anywhere else, because both followed the same written procedure -- which is exactly the guarantee an engineer relies on when specifying a material for a structure whose failure would have real consequences.
