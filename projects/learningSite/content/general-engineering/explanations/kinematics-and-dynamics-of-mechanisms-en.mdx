A mechanism's whole job is to constrain motion into something useful, and three families cover most of mechanical design: linkages, cams, and gear trains. A four-bar linkage -- four rigid links joined end to end by four pin joints, with one link held fixed as the frame -- turns a continuous input rotation into an output motion whose exact path is a direct mathematical consequence of the four link lengths. The same four lengths, reconnected differently or nudged slightly, can turn a linkage that fully rotates (a crank) into one that only rocks back and forth through a limited arc (a rocker); whether a given link can complete a full turn is decided entirely by the relative lengths of all four links (formalized as the Grashof condition), never by which link happens to be driven.

A cam takes a completely different approach: instead of deriving the output motion from a geometric linkage, it encodes the desired motion directly into a physical profile. As a rotating cam's edge pushes against a spring-loaded follower, the follower's displacement traces out whatever curve the cam's shape was cut to produce -- including a deliberate dwell, where the follower sits still for part of the cycle while the cam keeps turning underneath it, something a simple linkage cannot easily reproduce. This is why cams show up wherever a precisely-timed motion profile matters (valve trains in an engine, for instance) and linkages show up where a simpler repeating coupled motion is enough.

A gear train solves a different problem again: converting rotational speed and torque into each other while power flows through, essentially unchanged, from input to output (real gear trains lose a little to friction, but the ideal relationship ignores that). A gear pair's ratio -- set purely by the ratio of their tooth counts -- multiplies torque by exactly the factor it divides angular speed by. This is why a gear reduction that trades speed for torque can never be a "free" gain: the torque increase and the speed decrease are two faces of the same conserved-power relationship, not two independent knobs. Choosing which of these three tools (or which combination) fits a given design problem is one of the most basic and most consequential decisions in mechanical design.
