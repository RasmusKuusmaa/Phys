A standalone gas-turbine (Brayton-cycle) power plant rejects a large fraction of the heat it adds as hot exhaust, typically well above 400 degrees Celsius, straight to the atmosphere -- heat that a Rankine cycle, whose own combustion-heat input would otherwise need to come from burning more fuel, can instead receive for free by routing that exhaust through a heat-recovery steam generator instead of a smokestack. This is exactly what a combined-cycle plant does: the topping (Brayton) cycle's rejected heat becomes the bottoming (Rankine) cycle's heat input, and because the bottoming cycle only ever gets the fraction of heat the topping cycle failed to convert to work in the first place, the two cycles' contributions combine as eta_combined = eta_topping + eta_bottoming(1 - eta_topping) rather than by simple addition -- a topping cycle at 35% and a bottoming cycle at 25% combine to roughly 51%, not 60%, because the bottoming cycle's 25% only applies to the 65% of heat the topping cycle left on the table.

Exergy analysis asks a genuinely different question from thermal efficiency. The first law guarantees energy is conserved through every process, reversible or not, so tracking energy alone cannot distinguish a well-designed process from a wasteful one -- both conserve exactly the same total energy. Exergy (also called availability) is instead the maximum useful work a stream of energy could still deliver given the temperature of the surrounding environment, and the second law guarantees this quantity is only ever destroyed, never created, by every irreversibility a real process contains: friction, an unrestrained expansion, heat transfer across a finite temperature difference, mixing streams at different temperatures. A throttling valve is the cleanest illustration -- it conserves the fluid's energy exactly (no work or heat crosses its boundary) while still destroying a real amount of exergy, because dropping pressure without extracting work from that pressure drop is itself an irreversibility.

This distinction has direct engineering consequences a thermal-efficiency number alone hides. Comparing exergy destroyed component-by-component across a real plant -- the combustor, each heat exchanger, each turbine stage -- reveals exactly which component is squandering the most useful-work potential, which is not necessarily the component losing the most raw heat: a combustion chamber typically destroys far more exergy than any other single component in a power plant, simply because burning fuel is a highly irreversible chemical reaction, even though the combustor itself may lose comparatively little energy to its surroundings. Exergy analysis therefore tells a designer where to spend redesign effort for the largest genuine efficiency gain, information thermal efficiency, which only tracks energy quantities, cannot provide on its own.
