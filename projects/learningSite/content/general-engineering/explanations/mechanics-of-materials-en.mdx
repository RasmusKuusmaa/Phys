Statics tells you whether a structure holds still; mechanics of materials tells you whether it survives. The moment a real body deforms even slightly under load, two new quantities become the right way to describe what's happening internally: stress, the internal force distributed over the cross-sectional area it acts through (force per unit area, so the same applied force produces a much higher stress in a thin member than a thick one), and strain, the fractional change in length or shape that stress produces. Within a material's elastic region -- small enough deformations that the material springs back completely once the load is removed -- stress and strain are related by a straight-line proportionality, Hooke's law, and the constant of proportionality (the elastic, or Young's, modulus) is simply how stiff the material is: a large modulus means a given stress produces very little strain.

A beam bent by a transverse load develops a stress distribution across its cross-section that is worth visualizing directly: the outer fiber on the side the beam bends away from stretches (tension), the outer fiber on the side it bends toward compresses, and somewhere in between -- along the beam's neutral axis -- the stress passes through exactly zero, since fibers there are neither stretched nor compressed. The peak stress, at the outer fiber farthest from that neutral axis, is what actually threatens the beam, and it is directly proportional to the bending moment at that cross-section and the distance from the neutral axis, and inversely proportional to the cross-section's second moment of area -- which is exactly why beams are shaped like an I, with material pushed as far from the neutral axis as possible, rather than a solid rectangle of the same area.

Comparing that peak stress to the material's yield strength -- the stress at which permanent, non-recoverable (plastic) deformation begins -- through a factor of safety (yield strength divided by the actual applied stress) is how an engineer converts "will this survive" into a single number. A factor of safety just above 1 means the design is right at the edge of yielding under its expected load; a factor of safety well below 1 means it is predicted to yield before it is even put into service. Critically, yielding itself is not fracture -- a ductile material keeps carrying load, deforming permanently, well past yield, and only eventually fractures at a higher stress (the ultimate tensile strength) -- which is exactly why factor of safety is normally computed against yield strength rather than against the higher, later fracture point: a design that has already begun permanently deforming has failed its purpose long before it has actually broken.
