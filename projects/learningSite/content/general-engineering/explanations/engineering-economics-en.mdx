Two sums of money are only comparable once they are pinned to the same point in time, because money available now can be invested and grow, while money promised later cannot do anything until it arrives. Engineering economics handles this with discounting: a future cash flow is divided by (1 + r) raised to the number of periods it lies ahead, where r is the discount rate representing what that money could otherwise have earned. This is why "receive 12,000 in three years" is not automatically better than "receive 10,000 today" -- whichever is actually larger depends entirely on the discount rate used, and a high enough rate can flip the comparison either way.

Net present value (NPV) applies this idea to an entire project: discount every future cash flow back to today, sum them, and subtract the initial investment. A positive NPV means the project is expected to return more than the discount rate demanded of it, and is worth doing on financial grounds alone; a negative NPV means the opposite, regardless of how large the undiscounted future numbers look on paper. Payback period asks a related but genuinely different question -- how long until the cumulative cash flow turns positive, ignoring both discounting and everything that happens after that point -- which is why a short-payback project can still have a lower NPV than a longer-payback one whose returns keep compounding well past the point where the first project's cash flow already ran dry.

A breakeven analysis turns a single "which is cheaper" verdict into a conditional one: it finds the specific production volume, discount rate, or other parameter at which two competing options cost exactly the same, so the real answer becomes "cheaper below this threshold, more expensive above it" rather than a single fixed winner. This conditional framing is usually the more useful engineering answer, since the volume or rate a real project actually operates at is rarely known with certainty at the point the comparison has to be made.
