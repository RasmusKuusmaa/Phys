An idealized thermodynamic power cycle is a bookkeeping device: it replaces the messy chemistry and heat transfer of a real engine with a sequence of clean, idealized processes (adiabatic compression, heat addition, adiabatic expansion, heat rejection) that a working fluid is imagined to carry out in a repeating loop, and it is precisely because those steps are idealized that a cycle's efficiency reduces to a formula written in terms of just a few design parameters. The Otto cycle -- the model behind spark-ignition (gasoline) engines -- adds its heat at constant volume, right after the piston has already compressed the fuel-air mixture, and its efficiency formula depends only on the compression ratio r (how much the mixture's volume is squeezed) and gamma, the working gas's ratio of specific heats. Nothing about how much fuel is burned, how much power the engine makes, or how hot the combustion gets appears in that formula at all: a bigger engine running the same compression ratio has exactly the same ideal efficiency as a smaller one.

The Diesel cycle looks almost identical except that heat is added at constant pressure instead of constant volume (a consequence of injecting fuel gradually into already-hot compressed air rather than igniting a pre-mixed charge all at once), which is also exactly why diesel engines can run much higher compression ratios than gasoline engines without the early self-ignition problem gasoline engines face at high compression -- diesel engines want that early ignition, since fuel is injected into air that is already hot enough to ignite it on contact. The Brayton cycle trades the piston-and-cylinder picture for a continuous flow of gas through a compressor, a combustor held at constant pressure, and a turbine, which is the model behind every gas turbine and jet engine; the Rankine cycle goes further still and lets the working fluid actually change phase, boiling into vapor to drive a turbine and condensing back to liquid before being pumped around again, which is the model behind every conventional steam power plant.

Real compression ratios in any of these cycles are not free to climb without limit even though the idealized formula suggests ever-higher r always helps: in a spark-ignition engine, compressing the unburned mixture too far raises its temperature and pressure enough that it self-ignites ahead of the intended spark (engine knock), which is exactly the physical limit -- not a mathematical one -- that keeps real Otto-cycle compression ratios in the 8-to-12 range the idealized efficiency formula on its own says nothing about.
