No manufacturing process is universally best; each one trades a different set of costs for a different set of capabilities, and matching a process to a part's actual requirements -- shape, tolerance, material, and above all production volume -- is itself a real engineering decision, not an afterthought once the design is finished. Casting pours or forces molten (or otherwise flowable) material into a mold shaped like the finished part, which makes highly complex three-dimensional geometry almost as easy to produce as a simple shape; the cost of cutting that mold only pays for itself once it's amortized over enough parts, which is exactly why casting dominates at high volume and rarely appears for a one-off part.

Machining takes the opposite approach, starting from solid stock and cutting material away with a rotating or reciprocating tool until the finished shape remains; because a computer-controlled machine tool can be reprogrammed for a new shape almost instantly, machining carries no comparable tooling cost, and it delivers far tighter dimensional tolerances and better surface finish than casting -- but every chip cut away is wasted material, and very complex internal geometry (deep internal channels, for instance) can be difficult or impossible to reach with a cutting tool at all. Forming reshapes a solid piece of material -- typically metal -- by applying force well below its melting point, exploiting the material's own ductility to bend, stretch, or press it into a new shape without removing or adding material at all, which is why sheet-metal parts are typically formed rather than machined or cast: no material is wasted, and the process can be extremely fast once tooling exists.

Additive manufacturing inverts casting's economics almost completely: a part is built up layer by layer directly from a digital model, with no mold or cutting tool standing between the design and the finished part at all, so tooling cost is essentially zero and geometric complexity -- including internal cavities and lattice structures no other process could produce -- is nearly free. What additive manufacturing does not remove is time and material cost per part: building a part one thin layer at a time is inherently slower than casting or forming a whole part at once, and the feedstock materials themselves often cost more per kilogram than bulk casting alloys or sheet stock, which is exactly why additive manufacturing wins at low volume and high complexity, and casting or forming still wins once volume climbs high enough to amortize their tooling cost.
