Reliability engineering starts from a precise definition: a component's reliability, R, is the probability it performs its intended function for a specified mission time without failing, a number between 0 and 1 (or equivalently a percentage). Averaged over a large population of nominally identical components, the reciprocal of the failure rate is the mean time between failures (MTBF) -- a population-level statistic, not a guarantee about any individual unit, since real components fail according to a probability distribution, not a fixed lifespan; a component with a 10,000-hour MTBF can fail after 500 hours or survive past 20,000, with the stated MTBF describing only the population's average.

How individual component reliabilities combine into a system's overall reliability depends entirely on the system's structure. In a series arrangement -- the default when no redundancy has been deliberately designed in -- any single component failing takes down the whole system, exactly like a chain that breaks wherever its weakest link gives way, so the system survives only if every component survives, and the probabilities of independent events all occurring multiply: R_system = R1 x R2 x ... x Rn. This multiplication has a sobering consequence for complex systems: even a system built entirely from components that are individually 99% reliable has its overall reliability drop below 99% the moment it contains more than one such component in series, and a system with a hundred series components at 99% each has an overall reliability of only about 37%, which is exactly why real complex systems -- aircraft, spacecraft, chemical plants -- cannot simply rely on making every individual part reliable and calling it done.

Parallel redundancy reverses this multiplication. If two components are arranged so that either one alone can perform the function -- a backup system that takes over if the primary fails -- the system as a whole fails only if both fail simultaneously, and for independent failures the combined unreliability, not reliability, is what multiplies: the system's overall reliability becomes 1 - (1-R1)(1-R2), which is higher than either individual R alone. This is precisely why safety-critical subsystems (flight control computers, life-support systems, reactor shutdown systems) are deliberately built with redundant, independently-failing backup paths rather than a single highly-reliable component: redundancy converts a quantifiable engineering budget (the cost, weight, and complexity of an extra parallel path) directly into a quantifiable reliability gain, which is exactly the kind of tradeoff the engineering design process's factor-of-safety and FMEA practices exist to make explicit rather than leave to intuition.
