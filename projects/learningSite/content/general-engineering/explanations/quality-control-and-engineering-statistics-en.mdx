No real manufacturing process produces perfectly identical parts, and that is not itself a failure: some spread around a target value is an unavoidable feature of any physical process, arising from countless small causes (tiny material variations, thermal drift, operator handling) that are individually too small to chase down and collectively add up to what statisticians call common-cause variation. A control chart exists specifically to separate that expected, ever-present scatter from a genuine assignable-cause shift -- a tool wearing out, a bad batch of raw material, a machine drifting out of calibration -- by plotting sampled measurements against control limits derived from the process's own natural variation rather than from the specification. A point that falls within those limits is business as usual; reacting to it as though it were a new fault (overcontrolling a process that hasn't actually changed) tends to make the output worse, not better, by injecting adjustments the process didn't need.

Process capability asks a genuinely different question from control. A process can be perfectly stable -- in control, with no assignable-cause shifts for months -- and still be incapable of meeting what the customer actually specified, if its natural spread is simply too wide relative to the specification limits, or if it is centered off the target. Capability indices compare the process's own natural variation directly against the specification width, which is why a stable process and a capable process are two separate, sometimes contradictory, verdicts about the same set of measurements.

Tolerance stack-up brings the same statistical thinking to an assembly of several individually-toleranced parts. Summing every part's worst-case tolerance gives a valid but pessimistic bound, one that assumes every single part lands at its worst-case extreme at the same time -- a scenario that is technically possible but, if each part's actual deviation behaves like an independent random variable, statistically very unlikely. Treating the individual tolerances statistically (typically by combining their variances rather than their raw magnitudes) gives a substantially tighter, more realistic estimate of how the assembly will actually vary, which is why insisting on worst-case tolerance stacking across a long assembly chain routinely demands far more precision, and expense, than the application genuinely requires.
