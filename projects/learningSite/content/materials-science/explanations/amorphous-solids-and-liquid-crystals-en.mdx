Solid materials are often assumed to be crystalline by default, but a
significant class of technologically important solids — glasses foremost
among them — lack the long-range periodic atomic order that defines a true
crystal. A glass forms when a liquid is cooled fast enough that its atoms
or molecules do not have time to rearrange themselves into the ordered,
periodic lattice a crystal requires before the material becomes too rigid
for further rearrangement; the disordered, liquid-like local structure is
essentially frozen in place. This is why glass is often described as a
supercooled or arrested liquid: structurally, at the level of atomic
arrangement, it more closely resembles a liquid than a crystal, even though
mechanically it behaves as a rigid solid at room temperature, unable to
flow on any practical timescale. Whether a given material forms a glass or
a crystal on cooling depends critically on cooling rate, not composition
alone — cool the very same molten material slowly enough and it can
crystallise instead, given time for atoms to find their way into an
ordered lattice; cool it fast enough and glass formation is favoured. This
kinetic (rate-dependent) origin, rather than a fixed thermodynamic
preference, is the defining feature separating amorphous solids from
crystals.

Liquid crystals occupy a genuinely distinct middle ground between ordinary
liquids and crystalline solids, rather than being either one. Certain
elongated, rod-shaped molecules, under the right temperature and
concentration conditions, can flow and rearrange positionally exactly like
an ordinary liquid — there is no long-range positional order, and the
material has no fixed shape of its own — while still maintaining a partial
orientational order: the long axes of neighbouring molecules tend to point
in roughly the same direction on average, even though the molecules'
centres are free to move past one another. This partial order is
intermediate between a fully disordered liquid (no positional or
orientational order) and a fully ordered crystal (both positional and
orientational order), which is exactly why liquid crystals are described as
a distinct phase of matter (mesophase) rather than as either a liquid or a
solid in the ordinary sense.

This retained orientational order is what makes liquid crystals useful:
because the molecules are already partially aligned, an external electric
field can reorient them collectively, and this collective reorientation
changes how the material interacts with polarised light — the working
principle behind liquid crystal displays, which switch pixels between
light-blocking and light-transmitting states by electrically controlling
the local molecular orientation, exploiting exactly the partial order that
distinguishes a liquid crystal from an ordinary, fully disordered liquid.
