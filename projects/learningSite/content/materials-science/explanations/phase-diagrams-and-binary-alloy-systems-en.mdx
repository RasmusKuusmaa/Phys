A binary phase diagram maps out, across every possible overall composition of a two-component alloy and every temperature, exactly which phase (or combination of phases) is thermodynamically stable. Many such diagrams share a striking common feature: a eutectic point, a specific composition at which the alloy melts at a temperature lower than the melting point of either pure component alone, sometimes dramatically lower. This is not any kind of averaging or interpolation between the two components' individual melting points; it is a genuine thermodynamic minimum arising from how the relative stability of the liquid and solid phases actually changes with composition. This effect is put to direct practical use: eutectic alloys, such as certain tin-lead solders, are deliberately chosen for applications specifically requiring an unusually low melting point.

Reading a phase diagram correctly requires care at compositions and temperatures that fall inside a two-phase region -- a region of the diagram where two distinct phases coexist simultaneously rather than one uniform phase being present. A point inside such a region does not represent one single, blended phase with intermediate properties; it represents an actual physical mixture of two genuinely distinct phases (solid and liquid together, for instance, or two different solid phases together), each of which has its own specific, fixed composition determined by where the diagram's phase boundaries sit at that particular temperature.

Determining the relative amount of each of these two coexisting phases -- not merely their identity -- requires a specific graphical tool called the lever rule, which uses the horizontal distance from the overall composition point to each of the two phase boundaries to calculate the exact proportion (by mass or mole fraction) of each phase present. This quantitative information is essential in practice: two alloys with the same two phases present but different relative proportions of each can have substantially different microstructures and mechanical properties, so correctly applying the lever rule, rather than simply noting which phases are present, is what actually lets a materials scientist predict a real alloy's behavior from its phase diagram.
