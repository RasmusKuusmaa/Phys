An atom occupying a site in a solid crystal lattice is not permanently locked in place; given enough thermal energy, it can occasionally hop into a neighbouring vacant lattice site, one of the point defects that exist at a genuine equilibrium concentration in any real crystal. This vacancy mechanism, rather than atoms directly swapping places with one another, is the dominant pathway for diffusion in most real crystalline solids.

Any single atomic hop is entirely random: an atom has no way of sensing the overall concentration pattern around it and no preference for hopping in one particular direction over another. Net diffusion nevertheless proceeds reliably from a region of higher concentration toward a region of lower concentration, and this is a purely statistical consequence rather than any kind of directed atomic behaviour: a region with more atoms of a given species simply has more atoms available to hop outward across any given boundary than the sparser region on the other side has available to hop back in. Even though each individual hop is undirected, the sheer difference in how many atoms are available to hop each way produces a reliable, macroscopically directional net flow from high to low concentration.

Each individual hop, however, requires the migrating atom to squeeze past its neighbouring atoms to reach the vacant site, a process that costs a genuine energy barrier to overcome. The fraction of atoms with enough thermal energy at any given moment to clear that barrier grows exponentially as temperature rises, which means diffusion rates in solids depend exponentially, not merely proportionally, on temperature. This is why diffusion processes that are negligibly slow and can safely be ignored at room temperature become dramatically, qualitatively faster once a material is heated substantially -- a distinction with real practical consequences, since many materials-processing techniques (case hardening of steel, semiconductor doping, sintering) rely specifically on deliberately heating a material to speed up diffusion that would otherwise take impractically long at room temperature.
