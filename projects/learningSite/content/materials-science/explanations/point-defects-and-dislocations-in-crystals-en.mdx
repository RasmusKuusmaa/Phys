No real crystal is a perfect, uninterrupted repetition of its unit cell throughout its entire volume; every real crystal contains a population of defects. Point defects are localized imperfections confined to a single lattice site: a vacancy is a site where an atom is simply missing, while an interstitial is an extra atom squeezed into a gap between regular lattice sites that is not normally occupied at all. Remarkably, these point defects are not merely unavoidable manufacturing flaws but exist at a genuine thermodynamic equilibrium concentration that actually increases with temperature. This is an entropy effect: distributing even a modest number of vacancies randomly among the enormous number of possible lattice sites in a crystal increases the crystal's entropy substantially, and at any temperature above absolute zero, this entropy gain outweighs the comparatively modest energy cost of creating each defect, making some nonzero equilibrium defect concentration the genuine lowest-free-energy state rather than a correctable imperfection.

Dislocations are an entirely different category of defect: rather than being localized to a single site, a dislocation is a one-dimensional line running through the crystal, along which the regular atomic arrangement is severely misaligned. This different dimensionality corresponds to a genuinely different physical role. Point defects primarily affect properties like diffusion (vacancies provide the empty sites atoms actually hop into as they migrate through a solid) and equilibrium thermodynamic behavior. Dislocations, by contrast, are specifically what makes plastic deformation possible at realistic stresses.

A perfect, dislocation-free crystal would require an entire plane of atoms to slide past an adjacent plane simultaneously in order to deform plastically, breaking every bond across that plane all at once -- a process calculated to require enormous stress, many orders of magnitude higher than what real metals are actually observed to yield at. Real plastic deformation instead proceeds through the motion of dislocations already present in the crystal: as a dislocation glides through the lattice, only one row of bonds needs to break and re-form at any given moment, propagating the local misalignment along rather than requiring the entire plane to move at once. This dramatically lower energy barrier for dislocation glide, compared to sliding a whole plane simultaneously, is exactly why real materials deform plastically at the comparatively modest stresses actually observed in practice.
