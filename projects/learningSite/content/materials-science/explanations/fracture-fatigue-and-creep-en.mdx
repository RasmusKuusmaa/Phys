A component's static strength -- how much stress it can bear in a single application before yielding or breaking -- is only one of several genuinely distinct ways it can fail, and assuming safety under static loading automatically guarantees safety under other loading conditions is a serious engineering mistake. Fatigue failure occurs when a stress well below the material's ordinary static strength is applied repeatedly, cycle after cycle. A microscopic crack can nucleate at a point of stress concentration (a surface scratch, an inclusion, a sharp geometric feature) and grow by a small, incremental amount with each stress cycle, even though no single application of that stress would ever cause visible damage. After enough cycles -- sometimes millions of them -- the crack grows large enough that the remaining load-bearing cross-section can no longer support the load, and the component fails suddenly, often with little prior visible warning.

Creep is a different, genuinely time-dependent failure mode: a slow, continuous deformation that accumulates under a constant stress held for a long period, even at stress levels well below the material's ordinary yield strength. Creep becomes particularly significant at temperatures that form a substantial fraction of the material's melting point, where thermally-activated atomic mechanisms (such as diffusion-assisted dislocation motion) let the material's shape change gradually over time in a way that a brief static strength test, applied and immediately released, would never reveal. This is why components operating at high temperature for long durations, such as turbine blades, must be specifically designed and tested against creep, not merely against ordinary static strength.

Finally, fracture itself -- the actual separation of a material into pieces, whether triggered by a single overload, by fatigue crack growth, or by creep -- can proceed through two qualitatively different modes. Ductile fracture absorbs substantial energy through extensive plastic deformation before the material finally separates, typically giving visible warning (necking, permanent bending) that failure is approaching. Brittle fracture, by contrast, propagates a crack catastrophically through the material with almost no preceding plastic deformation, offering little or no warning before sudden, often dangerous failure. Because fatigue, creep, and brittle fracture can each cause a component to fail well below the stress level its static strength alone would suggest is safe, real engineering design must assess all three failure modes independently rather than relying on static strength testing alone.
