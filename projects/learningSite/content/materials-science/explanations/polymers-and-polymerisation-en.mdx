A polymer is a very large molecule (a macromolecule) built by chemically
joining together many smaller repeating units, called monomers, through a
process called polymerisation. A single polymer chain can contain from
hundreds to millions of monomer units linked end to end by covalent bonds,
and this sheer chain length is what gives polymers their characteristic
combination of properties — flexibility, toughness, and the ability to be
processed into films, fibres, and moulded shapes — that are distinct from
both small-molecule solids and simple liquids.

How a bulk polymer material behaves under heat depends critically on
whether individual chains are cross-linked to one another. In a
thermoplastic, individual polymer chains are not covalently bonded to
neighbouring chains; they are held together only by comparatively weak
intermolecular forces (van der Waals forces, sometimes hydrogen bonding)
between chains that are otherwise free to slide past one another once given
enough thermal energy. Heating a thermoplastic above a characteristic
softening temperature weakens these intermolecular forces enough for chains
to flow past each other, letting the material be melted and reshaped;
cooling restores the intermolecular forces and the material solidifies
again in its new shape, without any covalent bonds having been broken or
formed — this cycle can in principle be repeated many times, which is
exactly why thermoplastics such as polyethylene and polystyrene are
recyclable by remelting.

A thermoset behaves fundamentally differently because its polymerisation
process, called curing, forms permanent covalent cross-links directly
between neighbouring chains, in addition to the covalent bonds within each
chain. Once cured, a thermoset is not really a collection of separate
chains anymore but is closer to a single, enormous three-dimensional
covalent network spanning the entire piece of material. Because flowing
would require breaking covalent bonds throughout this network rather than
merely overcoming weak intermolecular forces, a cured thermoset cannot be
melted and reshaped — heating it further tends to break bonds
destructively, causing charring or decomposition rather than flow, which is
why thermosetting plastics such as epoxy resins and vulcanised rubber are
valued for high-temperature dimensional stability and structural rigidity,
at the cost of not being remeltable or reprocessable in the way
thermoplastics are.
