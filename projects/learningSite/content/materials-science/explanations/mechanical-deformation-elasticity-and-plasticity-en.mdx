When a material is subjected to mechanical stress, it deforms — changes
shape — and how it responds falls into two qualitatively different regimes
separated by a material-specific threshold called the yield point. Below
the yield point, deformation is elastic: the material's atoms are displaced
slightly from their equilibrium positions, storing energy in stretched or
compressed interatomic bonds, and removing the stress lets those bonds pull
the atoms back to their original positions, restoring the material's
original shape completely. Elastic deformation is reversible and, for many
materials over a useful range, approximately proportional to the applied
stress (Hooke's law) — this is the regime that springs, structural beams
under normal loads, and most everyday mechanical components are designed to
operate within.

Beyond the yield point, deformation becomes plastic: some of the atomic
displacement becomes permanent, and removing the stress no longer restores
the original shape. In crystalline materials such as metals, plastic
deformation occurs overwhelmingly through the motion of dislocations — line
defects in the otherwise regular crystal lattice, where a plane of atoms is
partially inserted or offset relative to its neighbours. A dislocation can
move through the crystal by breaking and reforming just one row of atomic
bonds at a time, letting one plane of atoms slip past an adjacent plane at
a much lower stress than would be needed to break every bond across an
entire plane simultaneously, which is why real crystals deform plastically
far more easily than a perfect, defect-free crystal would. This
dislocation-mediated mechanism also explains work hardening: as a metal is
plastically deformed, dislocations multiply and increasingly obstruct each
other's motion, making further plastic deformation progressively harder —
exactly why repeatedly bending a metal wire makes it stiffer and eventually
brittle at the bend.

Pushed further still, beyond the material's capacity for plastic
deformation, the material fractures — bonds break irreversibly and the
material separates into pieces. The yield point and the fracture point are
therefore two distinct thresholds along the same loading process, not the
same event: a ductile material such as most metals shows substantial
plastic deformation between yielding and fracture, absorbing considerable
energy and giving visible warning (necking, bending) before breaking, while
a brittle material such as ceramic or glass fractures with little or no
plastic deformation once its (typically lower) fracture stress is reached,
offering little warning before failure.
