A material's electronic, magnetic, and optical behavior is not an independent add-on layered on top of its mechanical properties; all of these behaviors follow from the same underlying atomic bonding and structure that a materials scientist already selects a material for. Metals, for instance, owe their characteristic ductility to a "sea" of delocalized electrons free to move throughout the metal, and this exact same delocalized electron population is responsible for metals' generally excellent electrical and thermal conductivity. Selecting a metal purely for its mechanical properties therefore inevitably bundles in these electrical and thermal consequences as well, whether or not they were the primary reason for the choice.

This coupling means real materials selection is a genuinely multi-property tradeoff problem rather than a search for one universally best material. Improving a material along one property axis frequently comes at some cost along another, precisely because the properties trace back to shared underlying structure; a material excellent for one application, weighing one particular combination of properties most heavily, can be a poor choice for a different application that weighs the same properties differently. There is no single material that simultaneously maximizes strength, electrical insulation, optical transparency, and magnetic neutrality all at once -- real engineering selection means identifying which combination of properties a specific application actually needs and finding the best available compromise among materials that satisfy those combined requirements.

Magnetic behavior in particular is often mistakenly treated as a narrow specialty relevant only to applications explicitly built around magnets, but magnetic activity (or its absence) matters across a surprisingly wide range of ordinary engineering contexts. An otherwise mechanically ideal material that turns out to be significantly magnetic can interfere with nearby sensitive electronics, sensors, or scientific instruments even in an application that has nothing to do with magnetism as its stated purpose. Evaluating a candidate material's magnetic behavior alongside its mechanical, electrical, and optical properties, rather than setting it aside as a niche concern, is part of what genuine materials selection requires.
