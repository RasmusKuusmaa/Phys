Biocompatibility is often treated as a single fixed property a material either possesses or lacks, so that a material "proven biocompatible" in one context is assumed automatically safe for any implant application. This is genuinely wrong: biocompatibility is specific to the tissue environment, mechanical loading pattern, and duration of contact a given application actually involves. A material that is entirely biocompatible for brief external surface contact -- a wound dressing, say -- can trigger a damaging immune response, chronic inflammation, or accelerated degradation when implanted long-term inside load-bearing internal tissue, where the contact is sustained, the mechanical demands are different, and the body's response to a foreign material persists indefinitely rather than ending when contact is removed.

This context-dependence extends across different implant sites as well. A biomaterial excellent for one internal application, such as a hip joint replacement bearing large compressive and shear loads, is not automatically a good choice for a different site, such as a heart valve experiencing constant cyclic flexing in a blood-contacting environment, or a dental implant embedded in bone under very different loading and biochemical conditions. Each site imposes its own mechanical demands and biochemical environment, so a material's suitability is always relative to a specific site's requirements, not a transferable, absolute property established once and assumed to carry over.

A further genuine difference from most non-biological engineering materials is that a biomaterial's performance inside the body is not static over its service life. The biological environment can actively degrade a biomaterial through corrosion, enzymatic attack, or slow dissolution, so its mechanical and structural performance can change substantially over time in ways a material sitting in a controlled non-biological environment typically would not. Critically, this degradation rate is not simply a flaw to be minimized in every case -- it must be deliberately matched to the intended application. A material meant for a long-term joint replacement should remain essentially inert and stable for decades, while a resorbable suture or a tissue-engineering scaffold is deliberately designed to degrade at a controlled rate as it is gradually replaced by the patient's own natural tissue, making a rapid degradation rate a design success rather than a design failure in that specific context.
