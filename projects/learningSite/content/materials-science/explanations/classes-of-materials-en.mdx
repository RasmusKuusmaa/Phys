The four traditional classes are not an arbitrary filing system — they are
sorted by bonding, and everything else follows. Metals share a sea of delocalised
electrons, which is simultaneously why they conduct heat and electricity, why
they are opaque and shiny, and why they bend rather than snap: the bonds are
non-directional, so planes of atoms can slide past each other without the
structure coming apart.

Ceramics bond ionically or covalently, with strong and *directional* bonds.
That makes them hard, chemically stable and able to survive high temperatures,
and it also makes them brittle — there is no easy way for atoms to slide, so a
crack has nothing to blunt it and runs. The important correction is that
brittle does not mean weak. Ceramics are outstanding in compression, which is
why concrete, brick and stone hold up buildings; they are unreliable in tension,
where a single flaw decides the outcome.

Polymers are long covalent chains held to each other by weak forces, so they
are light, flexible, insulating and cheap to form. "Polymer means soft" is
wrong, though — Kevlar and UHMWPE are polymers with strength-to-weight ratios
beyond steel. Composites then combine classes on purpose so that each phase does
what it is good at: stiff brittle fibres carry the load, a tough matrix
transfers stress between them and stops cracks from running. The result is not
an average of the constituents but better than either, which is precisely why
composites exist.
