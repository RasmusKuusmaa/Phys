Corrosion is often treated informally as a single uniform process -- something a material either resists or doesn't -- but real engineering corrosion is a family of distinct degradation mechanisms, each with its own triggering conditions and its own targeted countermeasure. Uniform (or general) corrosion attacks a surface evenly, is the most predictable form, and is what most generic "corrosion-resistant" coatings and alloys are designed against. Galvanic corrosion is a different mechanism entirely: it occurs when two dissimilar metals are placed in electrical contact in the presence of an electrolyte such as moisture, forming a galvanic cell in which the more active (less noble) metal corrodes preferentially and often rapidly. Pitting and crevice corrosion are further distinct, highly localized mechanisms that concentrate attack in small regions -- often beneath a protective film or within a narrow gap -- rather than spreading evenly across a surface, and stress-corrosion cracking combines a corrosive environment with sustained tensile stress to produce cracks that can propagate with little visible warning.

Because these are genuinely separate mechanisms rather than variations of one process, a countermeasure effective against one provides no guarantee of protection against another, and can sometimes make another mechanism worse. A coating that successfully eliminates uniform surface corrosion does nothing to prevent galvanic corrosion if the design still brings dissimilar metals into direct electrical contact, and a design change intended to eliminate one localized attack point can inadvertently create a crevice that invites a different one. Real corrosion engineering therefore starts with diagnosis: identifying which mechanism, or combination of mechanisms, the specific geometry, material combination, and service environment of a design can actually trigger, and only then selecting countermeasures matched to each identified threat -- not reaching for one generic corrosion-resistant material and assuming the problem is closed.

Galvanic corrosion deserves particular attention because it is often mistakenly treated as an exotic or rare failure mode, when in fact it is one of the most common corrosion failures in ordinary engineering assemblies. It requires nothing more unusual than two dissimilar metals in electrical contact and a common electrolyte like ambient moisture -- conditions met by countless everyday fasteners, fittings, and dissimilar-metal joints. Designers must proactively check for dissimilar-metal contact across a design (and address it with insulating gaskets, compatible fastener materials, or sacrificial anodes) rather than treating galvanic corrosion as a remote possibility to be dealt with only if it happens to appear.
