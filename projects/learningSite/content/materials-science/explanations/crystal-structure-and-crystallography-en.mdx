A crystalline solid is built from one small, repeating three-dimensional building block called a unit cell: a specific arrangement of atoms that, translated and repeated over and over with no gaps or overlaps, tiles all of space to build up the entire macroscopic crystal. Understanding a crystal's structure reduces almost entirely to specifying the geometry and contents of this one small, repeating cell.

A genuinely remarkable mathematical fact underlies this picture: there are only fourteen distinct ways the underlying lattice of repeating points can be arranged in three dimensions while still filling space with perfect translational symmetry, called the fourteen Bravais lattices. This is not a partial or historically accumulated list awaiting further additions; it is a proven, mathematically exhaustive catalog of every genuinely distinct three-dimensional lattice symmetry possible, first established by Auguste Bravais. Every crystalline material's underlying lattice, however chemically different the actual atoms decorating it might be, corresponds to exactly one of these fourteen lattice types.

Crystallographers also need a compact way to specify particular internal planes running through a crystal's atoms, since many physical properties depend on exactly which planes are involved -- which planes atoms slip along during plastic deformation, which planes a crack propagates along during fracture, or which planes produce a particular reflection in an X-ray diffraction pattern. Miller indices provide exactly this: a family of parallel crystal planes is labeled with just three integers, derived directly from how that family of planes intersects the unit cell's three edges (each index being the inverse of the plane's fractional intercept along one edge). This gives every set of Miller indices a precise, direct geometric meaning, letting a material scientist specify exactly which atomic planes a given property depends on using nothing more than three small integers. It is worth noting that this entire framework -- unit cells, Bravais lattices, Miller indices -- describes crystalline solids specifically; many important materials, including ordinary glass and many polymers, are amorphous, lacking the long-range repeating order that defines a crystal, and require a genuinely different structural description entirely.
