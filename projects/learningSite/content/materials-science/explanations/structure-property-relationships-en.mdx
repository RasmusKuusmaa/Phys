Materials science is organised around one relationship: structure determines
properties, processing determines structure, and performance follows from
properties. Almost every question in the field is an instance of that chain,
and the reason it is worth stating explicitly is that it contradicts the natural
assumption that what a material is made of settles what it can do.

Carbon is the standard demonstration. Graphite and diamond are the same element
with nothing else present. Graphite bonds into sheets held together weakly, so
the sheets slide and it works as a lubricant and a pencil. Diamond bonds every
atom to four neighbours in a rigid three-dimensional network and is the hardest
natural material known. Identical composition, opposite properties — because the
structure differs.

Structure has to be read at several scales at once. Bond type sets the
baseline; crystal arrangement decides how atoms stack; defects — vacancies,
dislocations, grain boundaries — control mechanical behaviour; and
microstructure, the pattern of grains and phases, governs bulk performance.
Defects deserve particular attention, because "defect" sounds like damage and
is usually the opposite. A perfect metal crystal would be far too soft to build
with; dislocations let it deform without shattering, and deliberately tangling
them is how steel is strengthened. Heat treatment and cold working change
nothing about composition and transform what the metal can do, which is exactly
the point of the chain.
