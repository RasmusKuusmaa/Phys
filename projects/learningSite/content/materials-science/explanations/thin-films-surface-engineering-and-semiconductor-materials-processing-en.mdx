A thin film deposited on a substrate is often imagined as simply a very thin slab of the same bulk material, with essentially identical microstructure and properties, just at reduced thickness. This is genuinely wrong: the deposition method itself -- physical vapor deposition (evaporation, sputtering), chemical vapor deposition, epitaxial growth, and others -- determines the resulting film's crystal structure, defect density, and lattice relationship to the substrate beneath it. The exact same target material deposited by two different methods, or grown under different conditions within the same method, can end up with substantially different microstructure: one process might produce an amorphous or polycrystalline film with a high defect density, while another produces a highly ordered, nearly defect-free single-crystal film, even though the chemical composition is identical.

Epitaxial growth -- depositing a crystalline film that continues the crystal structure of an underlying substrate -- depends critically on lattice matching between film and substrate. When the film's natural lattice spacing closely matches the substrate's, the film can grow coherently, continuing the substrate's crystal lattice with minimal defects. When there is a significant lattice mismatch, the film experiences strain as it attempts to conform to the substrate, and beyond a certain film thickness this strain is relieved by the formation of interfacial dislocations. For a semiconductor device built on such a film, these dislocations are not a cosmetic flaw -- they act as scattering centers and recombination sites that can directly degrade or destroy the device's intended electronic or optical function. Lattice matching is therefore a first-order design constraint that determines whether a device works at all, not an incidental manufacturing detail to be optimized after the fact.

Because different deposition methods produce genuinely different achievable microstructures, they are not interchangeable choices to be made on cost or convenience alone. A method well suited to a simple protective or decorative coating, where crystallinity and defect density barely matter, may be entirely unsuitable for an epitaxial semiconductor layer that requires precise crystallographic registry with its substrate. Real semiconductor materials processing therefore treats the deposition method and substrate choice as first-order design decisions, selected specifically for the microstructure a given device's function requires, not as incidental manufacturing steps chosen after the material itself has already been decided.
