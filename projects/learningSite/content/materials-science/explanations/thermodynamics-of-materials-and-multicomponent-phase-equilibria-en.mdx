A binary phase diagram is sometimes assumed to be the general case, needing only a bit more bookkeeping to extend to real commercial alloys with three, four, or more alloying elements. This is genuinely wrong: adding even a single additional component fundamentally changes the geometry of the problem. A binary system's equilibrium is captured by curves on a two-dimensional composition-temperature diagram, with tie-lines connecting coexisting phases at a given temperature. A ternary (three-component) system requires an entire additional composition axis, and its equilibrium is described not by tie-lines but by tie-triangles connecting three coexisting phases across a genuinely higher-dimensional composition space. This is not an incremental complication -- it is a qualitatively different geometric problem, and real commercial alloys routinely have five or more alloying elements, pushing far beyond what a diagram, even a three-dimensional one, can visualize directly.

The hand-calculation methods that work for binary systems -- plotting Gibbs free energy curves for each candidate phase as a function of composition and finding common-tangent constructions to identify coexisting phases -- scale extremely poorly as the number of components grows. Extending this approach by hand to five or six components is not merely tedious; it becomes practically intractable, since the number of possible phase combinations and the dimensionality of the composition space both grow rapidly.

This is exactly why real multicomponent alloy design and manufacturing rely on the CALPHAD (Calculation of Phase Diagrams) approach: computational thermodynamics databases that fit thermodynamic models to experimental phase-equilibrium and thermodynamic data gathered from many lower-order binary and ternary subsystems, then combine those fitted models to predict the equilibrium behavior of the full multicomponent system. This is explicitly an empirical, data-fitted approach rather than a purely first-principles theoretical derivation -- the experimental subsystem data is what makes the combined model's predictions reliable, letting engineers predict phase behavior for complex commercial alloys that would be impractical to measure directly in every possible composition and temperature combination.
