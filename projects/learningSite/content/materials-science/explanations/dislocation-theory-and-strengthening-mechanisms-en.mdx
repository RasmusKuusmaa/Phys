A metal's yield strength is often treated as a fixed property determined purely by chemical composition, but this is genuinely wrong: strength is controlled by how easily dislocations can move through the crystal lattice, and dislocation mobility is set by microstructural features that processing can change dramatically without altering chemical composition at all. This is exactly why the same alloy composition can exist in dramatically different strength states -- a coarse-grained, annealed sample and a cold-worked, precipitation-hardened sample of identical composition can differ in yield strength by a large factor, purely because processing has changed how easily dislocations move.

Several genuinely distinct mechanisms raise strength by this same underlying route -- impeding dislocation motion -- but through physically different obstacles. Grain-boundary strengthening (captured by the Hall-Petch relationship) works because grain boundaries block dislocation glide, so finer grains mean more boundaries and higher strength. Solid-solution strengthening works because solute atoms distort the surrounding lattice, creating local stress fields that impede passing dislocations. Precipitation (or dispersion) strengthening works because second-phase particles act as discrete obstacles dislocations must either cut through or bow around. Strain hardening (work hardening) works because plastic deformation itself increases dislocation density, and dislocations impede one another's motion as their density rises.

Because all four mechanisms strengthen through the same underlying route via different physical obstacles, they are not mutually exclusive strategies to choose one of -- they combine, and real alloy design deliberately layers multiple mechanisms together (a grain-refined, precipitation-hardened, cold-worked alloy, for instance) to reach strength levels no single mechanism alone could achieve. This combination is not always a simple linear sum of each mechanism's isolated contribution, however: the mechanisms can interact nonlinearly, since precipitates can alter how dislocation density evolves during subsequent strain hardening, changing how much additional strength that hardening then contributes. Predicting a multi-mechanism alloy's actual strength requires accounting for these interactions, not naively adding each mechanism's contribution as if the others were absent.
