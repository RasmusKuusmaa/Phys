Simulating a real material's behavior is often imagined as a single computational technique that can, in principle, be applied at whatever scale a given question happens to concern. This is genuinely wrong: different length and time scales require genuinely different simulation methods, and no single technique is simply "the" method for a given material. Atomistic methods, such as molecular dynamics, track the motion of individual atoms according to interatomic force fields, typically over nanometer length scales and nanosecond-to-microsecond timescales -- fine enough to resolve individual dislocations, point defects, and atomic-scale diffusion events directly. Continuum methods, such as finite-element modeling, instead treat the material as a smooth, continuous medium described by macroscopic constitutive laws (stress-strain relationships, thermal conductivity, and so on), and can span whole engineered components over timescales of seconds or longer.

A phenomenon visible at one scale is often invisible or misrepresented at another. An individual dislocation's atomic-scale core structure has no representation at all in a continuum model, which only sees its averaged macroscopic consequence (plastic strain); conversely, the overall stress distribution across an entire engineered bracket has no meaningful representation in an atomistic model, which would need to track an astronomically large number of atoms to span that length scale at all. Directly simulating a whole macroscopic component atom-by-atom is not merely expensive -- its computational cost grows so steeply with the number of atoms and simulated time that it remains intractable by many orders of magnitude beyond any realistically foreseeable computing power, so continuum modeling is a genuinely necessary distinct approach for whole-component scales, not a shortcut adopted only because of currently limited resources.

Because neither scale alone answers every question, real computational materials science routinely bridges scales as standard, ordinary practice rather than as an exotic exception. Atomistic simulation results -- for instance, the stress at which a dislocation begins to move past an obstacle -- can be used to inform or parameterize the constitutive laws that a continuum-scale model then applies across a whole component, letting the fine microstructural mechanism's consequences propagate into a component-scale prediction without ever needing to simulate the whole component atom-by-atom.
