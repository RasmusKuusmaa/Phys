A composite material is deliberately built from two or more distinct
constituent materials that remain physically separate and identifiable
within the finished material, rather than dissolving into a single uniform
substance. The goal is to combine the best properties of each constituent
while offsetting the other's weaknesses — a combination that, in a well
designed composite, produces overall properties that exceed what a simple
weighted average of the constituents would predict, particularly for
properties like fracture toughness and damage tolerance that depend
critically on the internal microstructure, not just on bulk composition.

Most structural composites follow a common architecture: a reinforcing
phase, typically stiff, strong fibres or particles, embedded within a
surrounding matrix phase, typically a comparatively softer and tougher
material such as a polymer resin, metal, or ceramic. The reinforcing phase
is chosen for high stiffness and strength, but strong stiff materials such
as carbon or glass fibres are usually also brittle and cannot easily be
shaped into a useful load-bearing structure on their own — a single long
fibre has no ability to be held rigidly at both ends by itself, and a
brittle fibre network alone would be catastrophically vulnerable to any
small flaw or crack propagating unchecked through it. The matrix solves
both problems: it holds the reinforcing phase in a fixed, useful geometric
arrangement, and it transfers mechanical load between individual fibres or
particles through shear at the fibre-matrix interface, so that a crack or
flaw in any one fibre is contained rather than propagating catastrophically
through the entire structure, since the surrounding matrix and neighbouring
fibres continue to carry load around the damaged region.

This division of roles — reinforcement carries load, matrix binds and
distributes it — is exactly what lets a composite exceed the properties of
either constituent alone in the metrics that matter for structural design,
most famously stiffness-to-weight and strength-to-weight ratio: carbon-fibre-
reinforced polymer composites, for instance, achieve stiffness and strength
competitive with structural metals at a fraction of the weight, which is
why they dominate in aerospace, high-performance automotive, and sporting
goods applications where minimising weight without sacrificing mechanical
performance is the central design constraint. The same reinforcement-plus-
matrix logic extends to granular and particulate composites (such as
concrete, reinforced with aggregate particles in a cement matrix) and to
natural composites (such as bone, a composite of stiff mineral crystals
within a tougher collagen protein matrix), showing that the underlying
design principle is a general one, not specific to any single class of
material.
