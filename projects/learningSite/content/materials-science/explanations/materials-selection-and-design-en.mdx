Materials selection is often imagined as a search for the single objectively best material in existence, but no material simultaneously maximizes every desirable property at once. Strength, stiffness, density, cost, corrosion resistance, and thermal and electrical behavior are all genuinely in tension with one another across real material classes, so improving a candidate material along one property axis routinely costs something along another. The engineering task is therefore not to find a universal winner but to run a structured tradeoff process: starting from the specific combination of performance requirements and constraints a given application actually imposes, and searching only within that constrained space for the best available compromise among real, existing materials.

Ashby charts formalize this tradeoff process. By plotting one material property against another (for example, strength against density) across whole classes of materials -- metals, ceramics, polymers, composites -- these charts reveal which combinations of properties are jointly achievable by real materials and which regions of the property space are simply empty, unreachable by any known material. This lets an engineer see at a glance which material class, or specific material within a class, is even a plausible candidate for a given combined requirement (for example, "high stiffness at low weight") before undertaking any more detailed analysis, and just as importantly rules out entire classes of materials that cannot meet a combined requirement no matter how a specific alloy or composition within that class is tuned.

Because different applications weight the same underlying properties completely differently, a material's correctness is always relative to a specific requirement profile, never absolute. A titanium alloy chosen for its excellent strength-to-weight ratio is the right choice for a lightweight, high-strength aerospace bracket precisely because that application weights weight and strength heavily and can tolerate high cost; the same alloy is very likely the wrong choice for a cheap, corrosion-resistant household plumbing fitting, an application that weights low cost heavily and has no meaningful weight constraint at all. This is not a contradiction -- it simply reflects that materials selection must restart its tradeoff analysis for each application's actual requirements rather than reusing a prior application's answer as if "good material" were a property of the material alone rather than a property of the match between material and requirement.
