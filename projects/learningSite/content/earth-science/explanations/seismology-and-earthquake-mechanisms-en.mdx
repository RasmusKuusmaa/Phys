An earthquake is fundamentally a sudden release of accumulated elastic strain energy. Tectonic forces slowly deform rock on either side of a fault over years to centuries, storing energy the way a bent stick stores energy, until the accumulated stress exceeds the friction holding the fault locked, at which point the rock suddenly snaps to a new position and releases that stored energy as seismic waves. Which fault type is involved (normal, reverse, or strike-slip) is a direct signature of the tectonic stress regime at that location: extension produces normal faults, compression produces reverse faults, and horizontal shear produces strike-slip faults.

The released energy propagates as several distinct wave types, each with different physical behavior. P-waves are compressional (particles vibrate parallel to the direction of travel, like a slinky pushed and pulled), travel fastest through any material including liquid, and arrive first at a distant seismic station. S-waves are shear waves (particles vibrate perpendicular to the direction of travel), travel more slowly, and cannot propagate through liquid at all -- a fact seismologists have used to map the boundary of Earth's liquid outer core, since S-waves simply don't arrive on the far side of the planet. Surface waves travel along Earth's surface rather than through its volume, arrive last, travel slowest, but typically cause the most destructive shaking at the surface precisely because their energy stays concentrated in the region where buildings actually are, rather than spreading through the planet's interior.

Earthquake magnitude scales are logarithmic specifically because the actual physical range of earthquake energy is so vast that a linear scale would be unusable -- the weakest recorded tremors and the largest recorded earthquakes differ in energy by many orders of magnitude. On a logarithmic magnitude scale, each whole-number increase corresponds to roughly a 10-fold increase in measured ground-motion amplitude, which works out to roughly a 32-fold increase in the actual radiated energy; since energy release compounds multiplicatively across magnitude steps, a two-point jump in magnitude (5 to 7) means multiplying the energy increase twice (32 x 32 ≈ 1000), not simply doubling or adding a fixed percentage.
