The rock cycle's central insight is that "igneous," "sedimentary," and "metamorphic" are not permanent identities but descriptions of a formation process, and any rock can move from one category to another given the right conditions -- there is no rock that is intrinsically locked into being one type forever.

Igneous rock records crystallization directly from a melt. The critical variable is cooling rate: magma trapped deep underground cools slowly over thousands to millions of years, giving individual mineral crystals time to grow large enough to see with the naked eye (intrusive igneous rock like granite). Lava erupted at the surface cools within hours to days, so crystals barely have time to form at all, producing fine-grained rock like basalt, or, if cooling is fast enough, a glass with no crystal structure at all, like obsidian.

Sedimentary rock forms through a completely different sequence at Earth's surface: weathering breaks existing rock down into loose fragments, erosion transports those fragments elsewhere, deposition lays them down in layers (typically in water), and lithification -- compaction under the weight of overlying layers, plus cementation by minerals precipitating between grains -- turns loose sediment into solid rock. Because this entire process happens at relatively low temperature and pressure at the surface, sedimentary rock is the only rock type that commonly preserves fossils intact; the same fossil would be destroyed by the heat of igneous formation or the heat/pressure of metamorphism.

Metamorphic rock is what happens to any existing rock -- igneous, sedimentary, or even a previously metamorphosed rock -- when it is subjected to enough heat and pressure to recrystallize its minerals into a new, more stable arrangement, without fully melting. That "without fully melting" clause is the whole point: if the rock does melt completely and then re-solidify, it has become igneous rock instead, a different process entirely. Because metamorphism reorganizes existing minerals rather than starting from scratch, metamorphic rock frequently shows banded or foliated textures that are a direct signature of directional pressure squeezing platy minerals into parallel alignment -- and often retains a faint ghost of the original rock's texture underneath.

Because igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rock can each supply the raw material for either of the other two (weathered igneous rock becomes sediment; buried sedimentary rock metamorphoses; metamorphic or sedimentary rock melted deep enough becomes new magma), the rock cycle has no fixed starting point -- it is best understood as a network of possible pathways, not a single one-way sequence.
