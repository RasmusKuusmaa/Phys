Reconstructing Earth's history requires answering two different questions -- "what happened in what order?" and "how long ago, in years?" -- and geologists use a different toolset for each.

Relative dating answers the sequence question using principles that require no measurement of elapsed time at all. Superposition says that in an undisturbed sedimentary sequence, deeper layers are older, simply because new sediment can only ever be deposited on top of whatever already exists. Cross-cutting relationships extends the same logic to features that aren't layers: a fault, a fracture, or an igneous intrusion (like a dike) can only cut across rock that already existed at the time of cutting, so the cutting feature is always younger than everything it cuts through, without exception. Neither principle tells you the layers are "10,000 years old" or "10 million years old" -- only that one thing happened before another.

Absolute dating supplies the missing numbers by exploiting radioactive decay, a process that occurs at a fixed, statistically predictable rate for a given isotope regardless of temperature, pressure, or chemical environment. By measuring how much of an original radioactive "parent" isotope remains versus how much stable "daughter" product has accumulated in a sample, and knowing that isotope's decay rate, geologists calculate an actual age in years.

The geologic time scale is what results from applying both tools, across the entire globe, over more than a century of cumulative work: relative dating establishes local and then global sequences of rock layers, absolute dating anchors specific points in those sequences to real years, and the resulting boundaries between named intervals (eons, eras, periods, epochs) are placed exactly where the global rock and fossil record shows a genuinely major transition -- a mass extinction, the sudden appearance or disappearance of dominant life forms -- rather than at any arbitrary round number.
