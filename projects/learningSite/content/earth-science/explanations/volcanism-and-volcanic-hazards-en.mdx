A volcano's entire personality -- its shape, its eruption style, and the specific hazards it poses -- traces back to one variable: how much silica its magma contains, which directly controls viscosity, the magma's resistance to flowing.

Low-silica magma is runny. It flows readily across the surface before solidifying, spreading out into thin, wide sheets rather than piling up steeply, which over many eruptions builds a broad, gently-sloped shield volcano. Because this magma is fluid, dissolved gas within it can migrate upward and escape gradually and relatively safely as the magma nears the surface, so eruptions tend to be effusive (lava flowing out) rather than explosive. Hawaii's shield volcanoes are the standard illustration.

High-silica magma is thick and sticky -- its silica forms long, interconnected molecular chains that resist flow the way partially set glue resists being stretched. This same stickiness traps dissolved gas that would otherwise escape smoothly, so pressure builds up within the magma until it fails catastrophically, releasing all at once in a violent explosion. The resulting eruptions build steep-sided composite volcanoes (stratovolcanoes) from alternating layers of lava and explosively ejected fragments, or small, short-lived cinder cones built almost entirely from ejected debris.

This same mechanism explains why the actual hazard profile differs so much between volcano types. A slow-moving lava flow from an effusive eruption threatens property but rarely traps people, since it typically moves slowly enough to evacuate ahead of. An explosive eruption is a different order of danger entirely: pyroclastic flows are superheated avalanches of gas and rock debris that move far too fast to outrun, ashfall can travel enormous distances and collapse roofs or disable aircraft engines, and lahars -- mudflows formed whenever water (rain, melting snow, a crater lake) mixes with loose volcanic debris on a volcano's slopes -- can strike with little warning long after the eruption itself has ended.
