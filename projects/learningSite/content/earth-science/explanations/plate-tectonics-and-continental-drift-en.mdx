Plate tectonics organizes almost all of Earth's large-scale geological activity around a single, simple idea: the rigid outer shell (the lithosphere) is broken into plates that move, and what happens at any given location is determined by what kind of boundary sits there. A divergent boundary is where two plates are being pulled apart; magma rises to fill the gap, cools, and becomes new crust, which is why divergent boundaries build mid-ocean ridges underwater and rift valleys on land -- Earth's crust is literally being manufactured at these locations, continuously, in real time.

A convergent boundary is the opposite: two plates moving toward each other. What happens next depends on what kind of crust is involved. When oceanic crust meets continental crust (or another, denser oceanic plate), the denser plate is forced underneath in a process called subduction, generating deep ocean trenches, volcanic arcs, and some of the most powerful earthquakes on the planet. When two continental plates collide, neither is dense enough to subduct easily, so both crumple and thicken instead, building the tallest mountain ranges on Earth -- the Himalayas being the textbook result of India colliding into Asia. A transform boundary is different again: two plates grind past each other sideways, with no new crust created and none destroyed, producing a strike-slip fault that stores up strain over decades and releases it in earthquakes.

None of this was accepted quickly or on weak evidence. The visual fit between South America's east coast and Africa's west coast was noticed early and was suggestive, but coastline shapes can coincidentally resemble each other, so it wasn't decisive on its own. What actually convinced the geological community was a combination of independent lines of evidence: fossil species found on both sides of the Atlantic that could not plausibly have crossed an open ocean, and -- most conclusively -- a symmetric pattern of magnetic stripes recorded in the seafloor rock flanking mid-ocean ridges. As new crust cools at a spreading ridge, magnetic minerals within it lock in the direction of Earth's magnetic field at that moment; because Earth's magnetic field has flipped polarity repeatedly over geological time, the seafloor rock on either side of a ridge carries a mirror-image record of those flips, with identical stripe patterns appearing at matching distances on both sides -- directly, quantitatively confirming that new crust really is being created at the ridge and spreading outward in both directions at a measurable rate.
