Level, unaccelerated flight balances four forces: lift supports weight, and thrust balances drag. A pilot's controls do not command these forces or the aircraft's altitude and heading directly; they move control surfaces — ailerons near each wingtip, an elevator on the horizontal tail, a rudder on the vertical tail — that change the lift each surface produces and therefore the roll, pitch, and yaw moments acting on the aircraft. This is why pulling back on the controls does not, by itself, make an aircraft climb: the elevator changes pitch attitude and angle of attack, and a sustained climb requires thrust or power beyond what level flight needs to convert into additional height; pulling back without adding power at best trades existing airspeed for a brief altitude gain, and can just as easily drive the wing toward a stall instead.

Static stability describes what an aircraft does immediately after something disturbs it from its trimmed attitude — a gust pitching the nose up, say. A statically stable aircraft generates a restoring moment back toward trim on its own, without any pilot input. For a conventional tail-aft design, this restoring tendency comes from the relationship between the center of gravity and the neutral point (closely related to, but not identical to, the center of pressure): when the center of gravity sits forward of the neutral point, any nose-up disturbance increases the tail's angle of attack enough to generate extra tail lift that pushes the nose back down. The distance between the center of gravity and the neutral point, the static margin, sets how strong this restoring tendency is.

Static stability is not simply "more is better." A large static margin makes an aircraft resist disturbances strongly, but it resists the pilot's own deliberate control inputs by the same mechanism, making the aircraft sluggish to maneuver. A very small or negative static margin (center of gravity at or behind the neutral point) makes an aircraft agile but requires constant correction — by the pilot or, in modern fighter and some airliner designs, by a fly-by-wire flight control computer — to remain controllable at all. Highly maneuverable aircraft are therefore often designed with deliberately relaxed or negative static stability rather than the comfortable margin a training aircraft is given, trading hands-off stability for responsiveness.
