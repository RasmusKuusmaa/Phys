The existing aircraft-structures concept treats structural materials at a survey level: strength-to-weight and stiffness-to-weight ratios matter more than absolute strength, and fatigue-driven damage tolerance shapes inspection intervals. Composite laminates take the weight-driven design logic a step further by adding a genuinely new design freedom that an isotropic metal simply does not offer: directional tailoring. A laminate is built from many thin plies of unidirectional fiber (carbon fiber, in most aerospace structures) held in a resin matrix, and because each ply is far stronger and stiffer along its fiber direction than across it, stacking plies at different angles -- a common aerospace layup uses 0, +45, -45, and 90 degree plies relative to the primary load direction -- lets the laminate's overall stiffness and strength be shaped to match wherever the actual load paths run, rather than being forced to the same value in every direction the way a sheet of aluminum is.

This tailorability comes with a real complication: a laminate's mechanical response depends on the order its plies are stacked in, not just on the total amount and type of fiber and matrix present. Adjacent plies at different orientations develop interlaminar shear stresses between them under load, and how those stresses distribute through the laminate's thickness depends on which plies sit next to which -- so two laminates built from an identical set of plies in a different stacking order genuinely differ in strength and stiffness in a given loading direction, and in how resistant they are to certain failure modes like delamination (adjacent plies separating), which is often invisible from the outside after a low-velocity impact that a metal structure would show as a visible dent.

Buckling is the second genuinely new failure mode this concept introduces, and it applies to thin-walled compression members regardless of whether they are metal or composite. A slender or thin-walled member loaded in compression does not necessarily fail when its stress reaches the material's compressive strength; instead, it can suddenly deflect sideways and lose essentially all of its load-carrying capacity at a much lower critical load, an instability driven overwhelmingly by the member's geometry -- its slenderness, thickness, cross-sectional shape, and how well its edges are supported -- rather than by the material's intrinsic strength. This is precisely why aircraft skins and spar webs, which are thin by weight-driven necessity, are routinely stiffened with stringers and ribs: not primarily to add raw strength, but to shorten the unsupported length between supports and raise the critical buckling load, letting a structure that would otherwise buckle at a dangerously low load instead carry its full design load safely.
