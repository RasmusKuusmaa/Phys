Clouds routinely contain liquid water at temperatures well below freezing -- a supercooled state that persists as long as the droplets have nothing solid to freeze onto. An aircraft flying through such a cloud provides exactly that trigger: the moment a droplet strikes the leading edge of a wing, tail surface, or engine inlet, it freezes almost instantly, and successive droplets build the ice up into shapes ranging from smooth, transparent glaze ice to rough, opaque rime ice, depending on droplet size and temperature. The particular danger is not the ice's mass but its shape: a wing's lift and stall behavior depend on air flowing smoothly around a precisely designed cross-section, and even a thin, rough accretion near the leading edge is enough to trip that flow into early separation, producing a stall at a substantially lower angle of attack -- and correspondingly higher airspeed -- than the same aircraft would experience clean, on top of the drag increase and control-surface effects ice can also cause.

Because the trigger is supercooled liquid water, not simply cold air, icing risk cannot be read off the ambient temperature alone -- an aircraft can pick up ice while flying through a cloud at an outside air temperature that is, by itself, only mildly below zero, and conversely a clear, cold sky with no liquid water present poses essentially no icing risk regardless of how low the thermometer reads. This is also why icing is such a fast-acting hazard compared to the environmental degradation processes covered elsewhere on this site: a structural material's fatigue life or corrosion resistance is measured in years of service, while a wing can accrete a stall-inducing layer of ice in a matter of minutes inside the wrong cloud.

Aircraft defend against icing with two distinct strategies. Anti-icing systems act before ice can form at all -- heated leading edges (using engine bleed air or electrical heating elements) keep the surface above freezing, and freezing-point-depressant fluids sprayed on before takeoff serve the same preventive role on the ground. De-icing systems instead accept that some ice will form and remove it afterward: inflatable pneumatic boots on the wing's leading edge crack accumulated ice off by changing shape, and cyclic electrical or bleed-air heating melts the ice's bond to the surface so aerodynamic forces sweep it away. Which strategy (or combination) a given aircraft uses depends on where ice is most likely to form, how much electrical power or bleed air the aircraft can spare, and whether that surface can tolerate a small amount of ice accumulating between de-icing cycles.

Icing is the most immediate of a broader set of environmental effects a real aircraft design has to survive, most of which act far more slowly: ultraviolet radiation gradually degrades composite skins and coatings over years of sun exposure, sand and dust erode leading edges and engine compressor blades especially in desert operations, and a lightning strike -- essentially instantaneous, unlike any of the above -- has to be safely conducted through the airframe's structure and shielded away from flight-critical avionics. Icing earns its own dedicated concept here precisely because, unlike those slower material-level effects, it changes the vehicle's aerodynamic shape and mass distribution in flight, on a timescale of minutes, which is a fundamentally different kind of problem from anything a materials-selection decision alone can solve.
