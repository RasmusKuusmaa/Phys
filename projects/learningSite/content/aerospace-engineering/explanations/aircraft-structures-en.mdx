Early aircraft were built as a fabric-covered truss: a rigid internal skeleton of wooden or steel members carried essentially every load, and the fabric skin stretched over it did nothing structural at all — it simply gave the airframe an aerodynamic shape. Almost every aircraft built since the 1930s instead uses a semi-monocoque structure, in which the outer skin itself is a load-carrying member, working together with longitudinal stringers and transverse frames or ribs to share bending, shear, and torsional loads across the whole assembly. This is why a semi-monocoque fuselage's skin cannot be treated as a weatherproof shell the way a truss airframe's fabric could: cutting it, corroding it, or denting it beyond allowable limits measurably reduces the structure's strength, which is why aviation maintenance treats skin damage as a structural inspection item, not a cosmetic one.

Two forces shape almost every structural decision on an airframe. First, weight is directly opposed to both lift and thrust budgets — every kilogram of structure is a kilogram not available for fuel or payload — so the design metric that matters is strength-to-weight and stiffness-to-weight, not strength in isolation. This is why aluminum alloys and, increasingly, carbon-fiber composites dominate airframes even though steel is stronger in an absolute sense: steel's weight penalty would cost more in performance than its extra strength could ever return. Second, airframes are built around damage tolerance rather than an assumption of perfection: every structural member is expected to eventually develop fatigue cracks under millions of cyclic loads from every takeoff, landing, and gust encountered over a service life measured in decades. Rather than trying (and failing) to prevent cracking altogether, engineers calculate how quickly a crack of a given size can grow under expected loads and set inspection intervals — visual, ultrasonic, eddy-current — short enough to reliably catch any crack while it is still far below the length that would threaten the structure. A crack found on a scheduled inspection is not evidence the airframe was badly designed; it is the intended outcome of a design philosophy that plans for damage rather than assuming it away.
