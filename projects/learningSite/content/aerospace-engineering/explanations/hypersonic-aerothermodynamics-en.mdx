Below hypersonic speed, an aircraft's aerodynamic design problem is overwhelmingly about drag and stability -- shock waves cost energy and complicate control, but the airframe itself is not in serious thermal danger. Somewhere around Mach 5, that changes completely. The bow shock standing ahead of a hypersonic vehicle compresses and decelerates the oncoming air so abruptly that a large share of the vehicle's kinetic energy converts directly into thermal energy within the shock layer, before that superheated gas ever touches the vehicle at all. Stagnation temperature -- the temperature the gas would reach if brought completely to rest, which is approximately what happens at the very nose of the vehicle -- can reach many thousands of kelvin at orbital reentry speeds, hot enough to dissociate and ionize the air itself, well beyond the melting or vaporization point of essentially any structural material. From Mach 5 upward, the central engineering question stops being "how do I minimize drag" and becomes "how do I keep this heat out of the structure."

The single most important, and most counter-intuitive, result in hypersonic vehicle design is that a blunt nose is thermally better than a sharp one, exactly backwards from supersonic practice. A sharp nose holds its shock wave tight against the surface, so the hot, shocked gas flows directly along the skin at high speed, delivering heat efficiently into a small area. A blunt nose instead pushes its bow shock well clear of the surface (a "detached" shock), and the flow that does reach the surface has already been slowed and diverted around the body rather than sliding along it. Heat transfer to a surface scales inversely with the local radius of curvature -- a sharper radius concentrates the same heat load into a smaller area, a blunter radius spreads it thinner -- so a blunt nose trades a real drag penalty for a large reduction in peak heat flux, precisely the tradeoff a reentry capsule wants to make and a cruising aircraft does not. This is why a Mercury or Apollo capsule is nose-first blunt and a Concorde is nose-first sharp: the two vehicles are solving opposite problems.

Whatever heat load does reach the surface still has to go somewhere, and thermal protection systems handle it with one of two fundamentally different strategies. An ablative heat shield is designed to be consumed: its outer material chars, melts, and erodes away layer by layer, carrying absorbed heat with it as it departs the vehicle and leaving a charred but still-attached layer that further insulates what's underneath -- ablation losing material is the system working as intended, not a sign of failure, and it is the strategy behind Apollo, Soyuz, and most robotic planetary-entry vehicles. A reusable thermal protection system, like the ceramic tiles used on the Space Shuttle, instead relies on extremely low thermal conductivity: the outer tile surface gets genuinely hot, radiating a large share of the absorbed heat straight back into the air, while the ceramic's poor conductivity keeps that heat from reaching the aluminum structure a few centimeters underneath during the short time reentry heating lasts. Both strategies solve the same underlying problem -- keep the heat load in the shield, not the structure -- with opposite relationships to their own material: one is designed to leave, the other is designed to stay.
