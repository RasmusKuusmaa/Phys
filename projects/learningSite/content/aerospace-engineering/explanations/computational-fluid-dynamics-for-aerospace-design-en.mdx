The governing equations of fluid flow, the Navier-Stokes equations, have exact analytical solutions only for a handful of highly simplified geometries and flow conditions -- nothing close to a real wing, fuselage, or engine inlet. Computational fluid dynamics gets a usable answer anyway by discretising the problem: the flow domain around the vehicle is divided into a mesh of many small cells, the continuous equations are replaced by algebraic approximations valid on that mesh, and a computer solves the resulting (very large) system iteratively. The result is an approximate flow field -- velocity, pressure, and derived quantities like lift and drag -- built up cell by cell rather than derived in closed form.

The single biggest practical complication is turbulence. Real flow at the Reynolds numbers an aircraft or launch vehicle actually flies at is turbulent across an enormous range of length and time scales, and resolving all of them directly (direct numerical simulation) would require a mesh and time step far beyond any computer available today for a vehicle-scale problem. Practical aerospace CFD instead solves the Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations, which describe the time-averaged flow, and plugs in a turbulence model to approximate the net effect the unresolved fluctuating scales have on that average. These models are calibrated against known reference flows and work well within the regimes they were tuned for, but they are approximations, not a direct computation of the true turbulent field -- which is exactly why they can degrade noticeably for flows well outside their calibration range, such as strongly separated flow behind a stalled wing or highly unsteady wake flow.

This has a direct consequence for how CFD results are actually used in aerospace design: numerical convergence -- the iterative residuals dropping below a chosen tolerance -- only certifies that the discrete equations were solved correctly for the mesh and turbulence model chosen. It says nothing about whether that mesh was fine enough, or that turbulence model appropriate enough, to represent the real physics. Mesh refinement studies (checking that further refining the mesh stops changing the answer, called mesh independence) and validation against wind-tunnel or flight-test data are therefore standard practice, not optional extra rigor -- a beautifully converged CFD run on too coarse a mesh, or with a turbulence model mismatched to the flow regime, can still be confidently, cleanly wrong.
