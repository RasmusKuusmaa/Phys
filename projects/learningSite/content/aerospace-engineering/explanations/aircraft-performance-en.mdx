Aircraft performance analysis separates two related questions that are easy to conflate: how far can the aircraft fly (range), and how long can it stay in the air (endurance). Both depend on how efficiently the aircraft converts stored fuel energy into useful flight, but they are optimized by different cruise conditions -- range wants the best distance covered per unit of fuel burned, while endurance wants the lowest possible fuel-burn rate regardless of how far that time in the air actually covers. For a propeller aircraft these two optima are close together; for a jet aircraft they correspond to genuinely different cruise speeds, which is why long-haul airliners cruise near their best-range speed rather than their best-endurance speed.

The Breguet range equation formalizes the cruise-range relationship for a jet aircraft flying at a roughly constant lift-to-drag ratio: range scales with cruise velocity and with the lift-to-drag ratio, but only logarithmically with the ratio of the aircraft's weight at the start of cruise to its weight at the end of cruise (after burning the cruise fuel). That logarithm is the same mathematical shape the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation gives for a rocket's achievable velocity change, and for the same underlying reason: the fuel being burned to extend the flight is itself mass that must be accelerated and carried for as long as it remains on board, so each additional increment of fuel buys a steadily smaller increment of extra range rather than a proportional one.

This logarithmic relationship also explains the shape of a real aircraft's payload-range diagram, which is never a single straight line. At low range, the aircraft is limited by its maximum structural takeoff weight, so it can carry its full rated payload while only partially filling its fuel tanks -- the payload stays flat as range increases in this region because there is spare weight budget to add fuel without exceeding the structural limit. Past a certain range, the fuel tanks are full and further range can only be bought by removing payload to stay under the same maximum takeoff weight, producing the diagonal payload-range tradeoff line. The diagram's absolute maximum range is reached only at reduced payload, once the tanks are completely full -- a real mission always sits somewhere on this curve, not at the intersection of "maximum payload" and "maximum range" simultaneously.
