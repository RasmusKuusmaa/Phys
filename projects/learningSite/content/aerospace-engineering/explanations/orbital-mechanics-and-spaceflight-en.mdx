An orbiting spacecraft is not somewhere gravity has stopped acting. In low Earth orbit, a few hundred kilometers up, gravity is only a little weaker than at the surface. What makes an orbit possible is that the spacecraft has enough tangential (sideways) velocity that, as gravity pulls it toward the planet, the surface curves away underneath it at the same rate it falls — so it falls forever without ever getting closer to the ground. This is also why weightlessness aboard an orbiting spacecraft has nothing to do with distance from Earth: everything aboard, including the astronauts, is in the same continuous free fall as the spacecraft itself, so nothing presses against anything else.

Mission designers plan any orbital maneuver as a change in this velocity, applied with a rocket burn, and budget the total velocity change a mission will need as its delta-v. The most propellant-efficient way to move between two circular orbits at different altitudes, a Hohmann transfer, uses exactly two burns, both along the spacecraft's direction of travel rather than pointed radially outward: a first prograde burn raises the orbit's far side into an ellipse that just reaches the target altitude, and a second prograde burn at that point circularizes the new orbit there. A radially-outward burn wastes propellant distorting the orbit's shape without efficiently raising its energy the way a prograde burn — applied where the spacecraft is already moving fastest — does.

Reaching a specific destination, rather than just some higher circular orbit, adds a further constraint: a launch window. Rendezvousing with a space station or reaching another planet requires the target's orbital plane and position to align with the launch site and the planned transfer trajectory at the moment of launch, a purely geometric requirement set by orbital mechanics rather than by weather or daylight. This geometry repeats on a predictable schedule — roughly once a day for reaching a particular orbital plane from a fixed launch site, or roughly every twenty-six months for the low-energy Hohmann-style transfer window to Mars — which is why interplanetary missions are planned years in advance around specific launch dates rather than launched whenever a vehicle happens to be ready.
