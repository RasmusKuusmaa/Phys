Every engine that propels an aircraft or spacecraft, whatever its type, produces thrust through the same underlying mechanism: it takes in some mass, accelerates that mass rearward relative to the vehicle, and by Newton's third law receives an equal and opposite push forward. Nothing about this mechanism requires an external medium to react against — the reaction is against the mass being expelled, which is exactly why rocket engines, which carry their own propellant, work in the vacuum of space (and slightly better than at sea level, since there is no ambient back-pressure resisting the exhaust). Thrust scales with both how much mass is expelled per second and how fast it leaves; a jet engine's intake and compressor exist only to capture and prepare air for this process, not to generate thrust by "sucking" air in.

Turbojets, turbofans, and turboprops are all variations on the Brayton cycle: incoming air is compressed, fuel is burned to add heat at roughly constant pressure, and the hot high-pressure gas expands through a turbine (which drives the compressor) before exiting as exhaust. What distinguishes the three is how much of that expanding gas's energy is extracted to drive a large fan or propeller versus left in the exhaust jet itself. A pure turbojet expels a relatively small mass of gas at very high velocity — efficient at supersonic speeds but noisy and fuel-hungry at typical airliner cruise speeds. A turbofan diverts most of its airflow around the engine core through a large fan, accelerating a much larger mass of air to a comparatively modest velocity, which is more propulsively efficient at subsonic speeds and is why virtually every modern airliner uses one.

Rockets, unlike air-breathing engines, carry every kilogram of the mass they will ever expel on board from the start, which is why their achievable velocity change is bounded by the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation: delta-v equals the exhaust velocity multiplied by the natural logarithm of the ratio of the vehicle's initial mass (fully fueled) to its final mass (propellant spent). Because that ratio enters through a logarithm rather than linearly, each additional unit of propellant added buys a steadily smaller increment of delta-v than the one before it — doubling the propellant mass does not double the achievable delta-v. This diminishing-returns relationship, sometimes called the tyranny of the rocket equation, is exactly why reaching large delta-v budgets (escaping Earth's gravity well, for instance) requires initial-to-final mass ratios that devote the overwhelming majority of a rocket's launch mass to propellant rather than payload or structure.
