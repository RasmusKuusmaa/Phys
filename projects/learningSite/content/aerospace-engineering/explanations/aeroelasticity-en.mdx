Every real aircraft structure deforms under aerodynamic load, and that deformation is not a side effect to be analyzed separately from the aerodynamics -- it changes the aerodynamics. A wing that bends and twists under lift shifts its own local angle of attack, which changes the local lift it generates, which changes the load causing the bending and twisting in the first place. Aeroelasticity is the study of exactly this feedback loop, and depending on the structure's stiffness, mass distribution, and speed, the loop can settle into one of two qualitatively different kinds of instability.

Divergence is the simpler of the two, because it involves no oscillation at all. As airspeed increases, the aerodynamic twisting moment generated by a given amount of wing twist grows, while the structure's own restoring torsional stiffness stays fixed. Below a critical speed, the structure's stiffness always wins and the wing settles at some finite twist angle for a given load; above that critical divergence speed, the aerodynamic moment generated by the twist exceeds what the structure can resist at any twist angle, so the wing keeps twisting further and further under purely steady loading until it fails structurally -- a runaway static process, not a vibration.

Flutter is a genuinely dynamic phenomenon and needs at least two vibration modes coupled together, most commonly a wing's bending mode and its torsion mode. At low speed these two modes have distinct natural frequencies and any disturbance simply damps out. As airspeed increases, the aerodynamic forces increasingly couple the two modes together, and their frequencies converge; above the critical flutter speed, the coupled motion extracts more energy from the airstream on each cycle than structural damping dissipates, so any small disturbance grows into a violent, self-sustaining oscillation with no external forcing required to keep it going -- the airflow itself is now supplying the energy. This is precisely why flutter is a stiffness-and-mass problem rather than a strength problem: a wing engineered to be extremely strong (able to carry a very large static load before breaking) can still have a low flutter speed if it is torsionally flexible or has its mass distributed unfavorably relative to its elastic axis, which is why aircraft designers use techniques like mass-balancing control surfaces -- adding weight ahead of a control surface's hinge line specifically to shift its center of mass forward and decouple its motion from the wing's bending mode -- purely to raise flutter speed, with no structural-strength benefit at all. Every certified aircraft has a documented flutter speed and divergence speed, both kept safely above its maximum operating speed by a substantial margin, as two independent, separately-verified limits rather than a single number.
