A wing's cross-section, its airfoil, is shaped and tilted to deflect the airflow passing around it. Air arriving at the leading edge splits, flows over the curved upper surface and the flatter lower surface, and rejoins (approximately) behind the trailing edge, but not before the airfoil's shape and its angle of attack — the angle between the oncoming air and the wing's chord line — have bent that airflow's overall direction downward and behind the wing. By Newton's third law, pushing a mass of air downward and backward means the air pushes back on the wing with an equal and opposite force, upward and forward: this is lift, and the reaction-force view is the mechanistically complete explanation, not a simplification of it.

The same physical situation can equivalently be described through pressure: the airfoil's shape and angle of attack impose a net circulation on the flow around it, which speeds the air up as it passes over the upper surface and slows it over the lower surface. By Bernoulli's principle, faster-moving air exerts lower pressure, so the upper surface sees lower pressure than the lower surface, and this pressure difference integrated over the wing's area is lift. Both descriptions — momentum deflection and the pressure difference from circulation — describe the same flow and give the same force; neither one is the "real" explanation while the other is a shortcut, and a popular but wrong shortcut of its own (the equal-transit-time argument) claims the two surfaces' flows must reunite simultaneously at the trailing edge, which measured flow fields flatly contradict.

Both lift and drag grow roughly with the square of airspeed and increase with angle of attack — up to the critical angle of attack, beyond which the flow can no longer follow the upper surface's increasingly sharp curvature and separates from it. This separation collapses the pressure difference driving lift almost entirely, a stall, and it depends only on the angle of attack actually being flown, not on airspeed or engine thrust in themselves: an aircraft can stall at high speed pulling hard into a turn, and cannot stall, no matter how slow it flies, so long as its angle of attack stays below critical.
