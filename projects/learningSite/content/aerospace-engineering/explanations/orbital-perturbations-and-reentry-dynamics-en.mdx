The introductory orbital-mechanics-and-spaceflight concept treats an orbit as a fixed Keplerian ellipse, which is an excellent approximation over a single pass but not over the weeks, months, or years a real mission actually operates. The dominant correction for most Earth satellites comes from the fact that Earth is not a perfect sphere: its equatorial bulge exerts a slightly different gravitational pull than a point mass would, and the resulting J2 perturbation causes the orbital plane itself to slowly rotate about Earth's axis (nodal regression), at a rate that depends predictably on the orbit's altitude and inclination. Far from being a nuisance to be corrected out, this effect is deliberately engineered around: a sun-synchronous orbit is one whose inclination is chosen specifically so its J2-driven nodal regression rate exactly matches Earth's own roughly one-degree-per-day motion around the Sun, so the satellite's orbital plane keeps pace with the Sun's apparent motion and the satellite crosses the equator at the same local solar time on every single orbit -- extremely valuable for Earth-observation missions that need consistent lighting conditions to compare images taken months apart.

At the altitudes where atmospheric density is no longer negligible -- low Earth orbit, roughly below a few hundred kilometers -- a second, dissipative effect dominates: atmospheric drag continuously removes a small amount of orbital energy on every pass, causing the orbit to shrink. Because atmospheric density increases roughly exponentially as altitude decreases, this process feeds on itself: a slightly lower orbit experiences denser air, which removes energy faster, which lowers the orbit further, producing an accelerating spiral of decay that plays out over weeks to years (depending on the satellite's altitude, mass, and cross-sectional area) rather than a sudden event, right up until the final descent into the denser lower atmosphere causes rapid heating and breakup.

Reentry, when deliberately planned rather than accidental, exploits precisely this same drag mechanism rather than fighting it with propulsion. Cancelling a spacecraft's entire orbital velocity with a rocket burn -- roughly 7.8 kilometers per second for low Earth orbit -- would require an enormous amount of propellant, comparable to what launched the vehicle in the first place. Instead, a deorbit burn only needs to be large enough to lower the orbit's perigee down into the upper atmosphere; from that point on, atmospheric drag over the course of the descending pass does the overwhelming majority of the work, converting the spacecraft's enormous remaining kinetic energy into heat. This is precisely the same aerothermodynamic mechanism the hypersonic-aerothermodynamics concept describes for the extreme stagnation temperatures a returning vehicle experiences -- reentry heating is not a side effect that a bigger braking burn could have avoided, it is the direct, unavoidable consequence of using atmospheric drag, rather than propellant, to remove nearly all of a spacecraft's orbital energy.
