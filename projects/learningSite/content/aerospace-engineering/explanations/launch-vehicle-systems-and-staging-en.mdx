A single-stage rocket carries a fundamental penalty: every bit of structure and every engine it needs at liftoff has to keep being accelerated all the way to the end of the burn, even the empty tank walls of propellant that was consumed minutes ago. The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation already shows why this caps a single stage's achievable delta-v -- the mass ratio between full and empty vehicle can only grow so large before the vehicle is almost entirely propellant and almost no payload, and even then, real structural mass fractions and specific impulses limit a single stage to a few kilometers per second of delta-v, well short of the roughly 9-10 km/s a vehicle needs to reach low Earth orbit once gravity and drag losses are included.

Staging breaks this limit by treating the vehicle as a sequence of independent rockets stacked on top of each other. Each stage burns its own propellant using its own engines, and the moment it is spent, the entire stage -- its now-empty tanks, its engines, its plumbing -- is jettisoned. Every stage above it then only ever has to accelerate its own mass plus the payload, never again paying the price of hauling dead stage-one structure to orbital velocity. Because delta-v adds linearly across stages (each stage contributes its own Tsiolkovsky-equation delta-v based on its own mass ratio and specific impulse), a two- or three-stage vehicle reaches a total delta-v that no single-stage vehicle built from the same total mass and propellant fraction could ever match. This is precisely why, decades of engineering effort toward single-stage-to-orbit vehicles notwithstanding, every orbital launch vehicle flown to date has been staged.

Deciding how to split a fixed total mass across stages is a real optimization problem, not a free choice. A vehicle with very few, very large stages wastes propulsion for a long time hauling structure that has become dead weight; a vehicle with many small stages avoids that waste but pays for it with the mass, complexity, and reliability cost of extra engines, tank walls, and stage-separation hardware -- interstage structures, separation motors, and umbilical connections all add mass and failure points of their own. Real launch vehicle designers balance these two penalties against each other, which is why almost every successful orbital rocket in history has settled on two or three stages rather than either extreme, with the specific split of mass and delta-v between stages tuned by trajectory optimization for the particular mission.
