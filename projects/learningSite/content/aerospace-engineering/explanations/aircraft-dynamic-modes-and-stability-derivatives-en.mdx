Static stability, covered in the introductory flight-dynamics-and-stability concept, answers only one question: after a disturbance, does a restoring moment exist at all? Dynamic stability answers a much more detailed question -- given that a restoring tendency exists, what does the resulting motion actually look like over time -- and the answer, for a conventional airplane, decomposes remarkably cleanly into a handful of characteristic eigenmodes, each dominated by a different combination of aerodynamic stability derivatives (the partial derivatives of lift, drag, and moment coefficients with respect to angle of attack, sideslip, and their rates).

Longitudinally, two modes dominate, on very different timescales. The short-period mode is fast (typically a few seconds per cycle) and, in a well-designed aircraft, heavily damped: it is essentially an angle-of-attack and pitch-rate oscillation at nearly constant airspeed, governed by how strongly pitching moment responds to angle of attack and to pitch rate itself. The phugoid, by contrast, is slow (tens of seconds to minutes per cycle) and often only lightly damped: it is an exchange between altitude and airspeed at essentially constant angle of attack, driven by the comparatively weak coupling between speed and the drag/thrust balance rather than by the fast pitch derivatives that dominate the short period. A pilot can usually fly straight through a lightly damped phugoid without much difficulty precisely because it is so slow, while a poorly damped short period would make the aircraft feel dangerously twitchy.

Laterally, the picture is dominated by dutch roll: a coupled oscillation in roll and yaw together, out of phase with each other, arising from the interplay between weathercock stability (the yaw-restoring tendency from a positive directional-stability derivative) and dihedral effect (the roll moment a positive dihedral derivative generates whenever the aircraft sideslips). These two derivatives can be tuned almost independently by the airframe's geometry, which is precisely why a healthy, positive directional-stability derivative does not by itself guarantee a well-damped dutch roll -- an aircraft with strong weathercock stability but weak dihedral effect (or the two badly mismatched) can have an uncomfortably lightly damped, or in extreme cases even divergent, dutch roll mode, and this is a real, historically documented design problem, not a theoretical curiosity: several early swept-wing jet designs needed a yaw damper -- an automatic flight-control system that adds artificial damping specifically to this mode -- to be acceptable to fly.
