Mach number is the ratio of a flow's speed to the local speed of sound, not a fixed speed in its own right. The speed of sound depends on the temperature of the medium it travels through, so it is noticeably lower in the cold air at high altitude than at sea level — a consequence of this is that an aircraft flying at a constant ground speed passes through different Mach numbers as it changes altitude, and "the speed of sound" is never a single universal number to compute Mach against.

Flow behaves qualitatively differently in four regimes as Mach number rises: subsonic (below about Mach 0.8, where compressibility effects are small enough to mostly ignore), transonic (roughly Mach 0.8 to 1.2, where some of the flow around an aircraft is locally supersonic even though the aircraft's overall speed is not, and where shock waves first begin to form), supersonic (above Mach 1, entirely governed by shock-wave physics), and hypersonic (above roughly Mach 5, where extreme heating and chemical effects in the shock-compressed air become significant). The "sound barrier" is a real physical phenomenon rooted in the transonic regime — a sharp rise in wave drag as shock waves begin forming on the aircraft — but it is not a wall an aircraft passes through and leaves behind; every speed above Mach 1 remains genuinely supersonic flow with its own shock-dominated aerodynamics, not easier subsonic-like behavior resumed once "broken."

Several intuitions from subsonic flow reverse once flow is supersonic. In subsonic flow, a converging duct speeds the flow up and a diverging duct slows it down — the everyday intuition behind a garden hose nozzle. In supersonic flow, this reverses: a converging duct decelerates the flow, and a diverging duct accelerates it further. A rocket engine's converging-diverging nozzle, the de Laval nozzle, is shaped precisely to exploit both regimes in sequence: it narrows to accelerate initially subsonic combustion gas up to exactly Mach 1 at its narrowest point, the throat, and then widens to keep accelerating the now-supersonic flow to a much higher exhaust velocity than a simple converging nozzle could ever reach.
