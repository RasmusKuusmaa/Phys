A spacecraft's payload -- the telescope, the sample-return canister, the communications transponder that generates the mission's actual science or service -- gets all the attention, but it only works because a set of unglamorous supporting subsystems, collectively called the spacecraft bus, keep it alive, powered, correctly pointed, and within its working temperature range. Three of these subsystems illustrate just how different spaceflight engineering is from anything on the ground.

Pointing the spacecraft precisely is the job of the attitude determination and control subsystem, which senses orientation with star trackers, sun sensors, and gyroscopes, and actuates it chiefly with reaction wheels: electric motors spinning an internal flywheel, entirely enclosed within the spacecraft. Speeding up the flywheel in one direction rotates the spacecraft body in the other, by conservation of angular momentum -- no mass leaves the spacecraft, so no propellant is spent making the turn. The catch is that a reaction wheel can only store so much angular momentum before it hits its maximum spin rate; if the spacecraft keeps needing to counter a momentum bias in the same direction (from residual solar radiation pressure, for instance), the wheel eventually saturates, and only then does the spacecraft fire small thrusters briefly to dump that stored momentum and reset the wheel toward the middle of its range.

Thermal control has no everyday intuition to lean on, because there is no air in space to carry heat away by convection -- the only way a spacecraft loses (or gains) heat is by radiation. A spacecraft in direct sunlight can see its sun-facing surfaces reach well over 100°C while a shaded surface just meters away sits below -100°C, both at the same time, on the same spacecraft. Multi-layer insulation blankets (many thin, reflective layers with vacuum gaps between them) limit radiative heat transfer where it isn't wanted, radiators reject excess heat from electronics that would otherwise cook themselves, and small heaters keep components that would otherwise freeze above their minimum operating temperature -- all three techniques typically operating on the same spacecraft simultaneously, on different surfaces, rather than one strategy being chosen for the whole vehicle.

The electrical power subsystem generates power with solar arrays sized to the mission's total power budget, but solar arrays alone are not a complete power system: every orbit that passes through the planet's shadow, the arrays produce nothing at all, so batteries sized to carry every other subsystem through the longest expected eclipse are a mandatory, load-bearing design element, not a contingency reserve. Getting any one of these three subsystems wrong -- losing attitude control, cooking or freezing the payload, or running the batteries flat during an eclipse -- ends a mission exactly as completely as a failure of the headline instrument would.
