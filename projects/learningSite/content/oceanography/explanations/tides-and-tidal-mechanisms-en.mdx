The key to understanding tides is recognizing that the Moon's pull creates two bulges, not one, and both are real, simultaneous consequences of the same gravitational fact: gravity weakens with distance, so the Moon pulls more strongly on whichever side of Earth happens to be closer to it at any moment. On the near side, this stronger pull stretches ocean water toward the Moon, creating a bulge there. On the far side, Earth itself is pulled toward the Moon more strongly than the ocean water on that side is, which effectively leaves that water behind relative to the solid Earth, creating a second bulge pointing away from the Moon. As Earth rotates once every 24 hours, any given coastline passes through both bulges, experiencing two high tides and two low tides in that time -- not because the Moon visits twice, but because the bulge pattern itself has two peaks fixed relative to the Moon's direction.

The Sun adds a real tidal effect of its own, roughly half the Moon's strength despite the Sun's far greater mass, because tidal force depends on distance far more steeply than ordinary gravitational attraction does, and the Sun's much greater distance more than offsets its greater mass. When the Sun, Earth, and Moon line up -- at new moon and full moon -- the solar and lunar tidal bulges reinforce each other, producing the month's largest tidal range, called a spring tide (nothing to do with the season). When the Sun and Moon instead pull at right angles to each other, around the first and last quarter moon, their bulges partially cancel, producing the smallest tidal range, a neap tide.

This idealized two-bulge picture describes the open ocean reasonably well, but real coastlines rarely show it in pure form, because local geography reshapes how the tidal bulge actually plays out against land. A narrow bay or strait can amplify the tidal range dramatically as water is funneled into a smaller space; the resonant properties of a particular basin's shape and depth can favor one tidal frequency over another. The result is that real-world coastlines fall into distinct tidal pattern categories: semidiurnal coastlines show two nearly equal high tides a day, diurnal coastlines show essentially just one, and mixed semidiurnal coastlines show two high tides of noticeably different heights -- all consistent with the same underlying lunar-solar tidal forcing, just filtered through very different local geography.
