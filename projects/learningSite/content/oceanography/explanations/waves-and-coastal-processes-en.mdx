A traveling ocean wave is best understood as energy moving through water, not water moving with the energy. As a wave passes any fixed point, the water there traces out a roughly circular orbit -- rising and moving forward on the crest, then falling and moving backward in the trough -- and after one full wave period, a water particle ends up close to where it started, having gained essentially no net forward displacement. What propagates across the ocean is the wave's shape and the energy it carries, not the water itself, which is exactly why a floating cork or ship rides up and down with each passing wave rather than being swept steadily toward shore.

This orbital motion doesn't extend indefinitely downward: it shrinks rapidly with depth, becoming negligible below a depth of roughly half the wave's wavelength, a threshold called the wave base. This is why a submarine or a diver at sufficient depth can be almost completely unaffected by even violent storm waves at the surface directly above -- the orbital disturbance simply doesn't reach that deep.

Everything changes as a wave approaches shore and the water depth shrinks below the wave's own wave base. The orbital motion, no longer able to complete its circle in the shallower water, is forced to flatten into a more elongated, elliptical path, and the wave itself slows down and its height increases (since the same energy is being squeezed into a shorter wavelength). Eventually the wave becomes too steep to sustain itself and breaks, releasing its accumulated energy against the shoreline.

Because most waves approach a beach at some angle rather than head-on, that breaking energy has a component running parallel to the shore, driving a longshore current. This current picks up and carries sand along the coast in a process called longshore drift, and this single process is responsible for both major coastal outcomes that look, at first glance, like opposites: erosion, where the current removes more sand from a stretch of coast than arrives, and deposition, where sand accumulates to build sandbars, spits, and new beach material elsewhere along the same coastline. A coastline's shape at any given moment is really just a snapshot of an ongoing, dynamic balance between these two outcomes of the same underlying process, not a fixed boundary.
