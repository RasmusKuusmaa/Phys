Seawater's chemistry is dominated by two overlapping systems: a remarkably stable ionic composition, and a much more dynamic dissolved-gas chemistry that is currently shifting under human influence. Salinity -- the total dissolved salt content, averaging about 35 grams per kilogram -- is set mostly by sodium and chloride ions, but what makes it a genuinely useful measurement is that the ratio between all the major dissolved ions (sodium, chloride, magnesium, sulfate, calcium, potassium) stays essentially constant across the open ocean, even though the total concentration varies somewhat with evaporation and freshwater input. This constancy of ratios (sometimes called the principle of constant proportions) is what lets oceanographers infer full salinity from a single, easily measured quantity like electrical conductivity, rather than needing to separately measure every dissolved ion.

Dissolved gases behave very differently from dissolved salts, because unlike sodium or chloride, gases like oxygen and carbon dioxide actively participate in ongoing chemical and biological reactions. Dissolved CO2 doesn't just sit inertly in solution -- it reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which then dissociates into bicarbonate and carbonate ions in a reversible equilibrium. This carbonate system acts as a chemical buffer: historically, as CO2 entered or left the ocean, the equilibrium shifted to absorb much of that change without large pH swings, which is exactly why ocean pH has stayed within a narrow range for a very long geological time.

That buffering system is now under sustained pressure from the ocean's absorption of a large fraction of the CO2 humans have added to the atmosphere. As more CO2 dissolves, the carbonate equilibrium shifts to produce more bicarbonate at the expense of carbonate ions, and pH falls as a direct chemical consequence -- this is ocean acidification. The word "acidification" describes that direction of change (becoming less basic), not a claim that the ocean has crossed from basic to acidic; the open ocean's average pH, around 8.1 and slowly falling, remains solidly basic. But the direction of the shift still matters immensely for marine life: many organisms, including corals and many shellfish, build their calcium carbonate shells and skeletons directly from dissolved carbonate ions, and a falling carbonate-ion concentration makes that construction measurably harder even while the water around them is nowhere close to actually acidic.
