Ocean circulation splits cleanly into two systems that share the same ocean but almost nothing else. Surface currents respond directly to the wind: the persistent trade winds and westerlies that blow across each ocean basin drag the upper layer of water along with them, and because the Coriolis effect deflects moving water to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere, that wind-driven flow curls into a large, roughly circular gyre rather than moving in a straight line -- clockwise in the north, counterclockwise in the south, consistently, in every ocean basin. Because this circulation is driven by a force (wind) that is constantly renewed, surface currents move relatively fast, capable of carrying water across an entire ocean basin in a matter of months.

Thermohaline circulation works on a completely different principle. In a small number of specific high-latitude locations -- the North Atlantic near Greenland and parts of the Southern Ocean around Antarctica being the best-known examples -- surface water loses heat to the cold polar atmosphere, and where sea ice forms, the ice excludes salt as it freezes, leaving the remaining liquid water saltier than before. Cold and salty together make that water denser than everything beneath it, so it sinks, in some places all the way to the ocean floor. Once submerged, this dense water spreads slowly along the bottom of the ocean basins, driven now by comparatively tiny density differences rather than a constantly renewed wind force, and only gradually mixes back upward and returns to the surface elsewhere, often thousands of kilometers and centuries later. A single loop through this deep "conveyor belt" -- sinking in the North Atlantic, spreading through the deep Pacific and Indian Oceans, and eventually upwelling again -- is estimated to take on the order of a thousand years.

The two systems are connected, not independent: surface currents deliver water into the specific regions where deep-water formation happens, making thermohaline circulation partly dependent on wind-driven surface flow to supply it. But the mechanisms driving each -- wind force versus density difference -- and the resulting timescales are genuinely, mechanistically different, not two views of a single process.
