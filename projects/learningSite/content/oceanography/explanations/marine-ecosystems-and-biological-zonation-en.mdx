Marine ecosystems are organized along two largely independent physical axes, and confusing them (treating "benthic" as if it meant "deep," for instance) is one of the most common ways to misread how ocean habitats are actually classified. The first axis is about light: the photic zone is the layer -- at most a couple hundred meters deep even in the clearest open ocean water -- where enough sunlight penetrates for photosynthesis to occur at all, while the aphotic zone is everything below that, permanently dark regardless of depth. Because photosynthesis is the entry point for nearly all of the energy that powers marine food webs, the photic zone punches enormously above its weight ecologically despite being a thin sliver of the ocean's total three-dimensional volume; almost every calorie consumed anywhere in the ocean, including in the permanently dark aphotic zone, ultimately traces back to photosynthesis that happened in that thin surface layer, sinking down as marine snow or moving through the food web from organisms that migrate between zones.

The second axis is entirely independent of light and concerns where in the water an organism actually lives: the pelagic zone is the open water column itself, home to everything from drifting plankton and jellyfish to fast-swimming fish, sharks, and whales that never touch the bottom, while the benthic zone is the seafloor and the organisms that live on, in, or immediately above it, from burrowing worms to reef-building corals to the crabs and tube worms clustered around deep hydrothermal vents.

Because these two axes are independent, most named marine habitats are really defined by which combination of the two they occupy. A coral reef sits in shallow water and is attached to the seafloor, so it is photic and benthic. The open ocean surface, where plankton drift and tuna hunt, is photic and pelagic. The deep ocean floor, far below where sunlight reaches, is aphotic and benthic -- and supports a radically different community (often dependent on sinking detritus, or in vent ecosystems, on chemosynthesis rather than sunlight at all) than a sunlit reef does, despite both technically being "benthic." Understanding a marine community means specifying both axes, not just one.
