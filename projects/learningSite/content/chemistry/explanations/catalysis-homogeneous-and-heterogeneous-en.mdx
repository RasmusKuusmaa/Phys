A catalyst increases a reaction's rate without being permanently consumed
and without shifting the reaction's equilibrium position — it changes how
fast equilibrium is reached, not where the equilibrium lies, since both the
forward and reverse reaction rates are accelerated by exactly the amount
needed to keep the equilibrium constant unchanged. The mechanism behind
this rate increase, in every case, is the same: a catalyst provides an
alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy than the
uncatalysed route. Recalling the Arrhenius equation, a lower activation
energy means a substantially larger fraction of molecular collisions have
enough energy to react, which is exactly why even a small reduction in
activation energy can produce a dramatic increase in rate.

Catalysts are classified by whether they share a phase with the reactants.
In homogeneous catalysis, the catalyst is dissolved or dispersed in the
same phase as the reactants — typically the same liquid solution — and
interacts with reactant molecules through ordinary solution-phase
collisions and complex formation. Enzymes, the biological catalysts that
accelerate nearly every reaction inside living cells, are a prominent
example: an enzyme dissolved alongside its substrate in the cell's aqueous
interior binds the substrate at an active site shaped to stabilise the
reaction's transition state, dramatically lowering the effective activation
energy for that specific reaction, often by many orders of magnitude in
rate.

In heterogeneous catalysis, the catalyst exists in a different phase from
the reactants, most commonly a solid catalyst acting on gas- or
liquid-phase reactants. The catalytic action in this case typically
proceeds through adsorption: reactant molecules first adsorb onto the solid
catalyst's surface (governed by the same Langmuir-type equilibrium that
describes gas-solid adsorption generally), where the surface's local
electronic and geometric structure weakens particular bonds in the adsorbed
molecule or brings reacting species into favourable proximity and
orientation, lowering the activation energy for the surface-bound reaction
compared with the same reaction occurring between free-flying gas
molecules. After reacting on the surface, the products desorb, freeing the
site for further catalytic cycles. Industrial heterogeneous catalysis —
solid catalysts in car exhaust converters, in ammonia synthesis, and in
petroleum refining — exploits exactly this surface-mediated pathway, and
because reaction occurs specifically at the catalyst's surface, maximising
surface area (fine powders, porous supports) is a standard and direct way
to increase a heterogeneous catalyst's overall effectiveness.
