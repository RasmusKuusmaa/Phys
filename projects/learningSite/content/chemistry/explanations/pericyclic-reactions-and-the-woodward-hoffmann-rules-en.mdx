Most organic reaction mechanisms proceed in discrete steps, passing through a charged (carbocation or carbanion) or radical intermediate along the way. Pericyclic reactions are genuinely different: they proceed through a single concerted step, in which several bonds break and form simultaneously around a closed loop of continuously overlapping orbitals, with no discrete intermediate ever forming at all. The Diels-Alder reaction, joining a diene and a dienophile into a new six-membered ring in one step, is the archetypal example: two new sigma bonds form and one pi bond shifts, all at once, around a cyclic array of overlapping p-orbitals.

Because pericyclic reactions proceed through this continuous, orbital-overlap pathway rather than through discrete intermediates, their feasibility is governed by a genuinely different kind of rule than ordinary reaction mechanisms: orbital symmetry. The Woodward-Hoffmann rules, derived by analyzing how the symmetry of the relevant molecular orbitals changes continuously along the reaction coordinate as the reactants' orbitals evolve into the product's orbitals, predict whether a given concerted pathway is symmetry-allowed or symmetry-forbidden under specific conditions. Crucially, exciting an electron to a different orbital -- exactly what happens when a molecule absorbs light in photochemical conditions -- changes which orbital symmetry pattern is actually available along the reaction pathway. The rules predict, with striking and repeatedly verified precision, that a given pericyclic pathway which is symmetry-allowed and proceeds smoothly under ordinary thermal conditions is symmetry-forbidden under photochemical conditions, and vice versa: the two conditions systematically favor opposite outcomes for the same reaction type, not merely different rates of the same pathway.

This is not an empirical pattern noticed after the fact but a genuine theoretical prediction, derived directly from orbital-symmetry conservation principles before much of the confirming experimental evidence existed. Its subsequent, repeated experimental verification across many different pericyclic reaction types (electrocyclic ring closures, cycloadditions like the Diels-Alder reaction, and sigmatropic rearrangements alike) is precisely why the Woodward-Hoffmann rules are regarded as one of the most successful theoretical predictions in the history of organic chemistry, earning Robert Woodward and Roald Hoffmann a Nobel Prize for the underlying theory.
