Hydrogen sits uniquely in the periodic table: it has a single electron and
can either lose it to form H⁺ (behaving somewhat like an alkali metal
cation) or gain one to form H⁻ (behaving like a halide anion), and this
dual character shows up directly in the compounds it forms. With highly
electropositive metals such as the alkali and alkaline-earth metals, the
large electronegativity gap lets hydrogen accept an electron, forming ionic
hydrides (such as NaH) containing the discrete hydride anion, H⁻ — these
compounds are salt-like, high-melting solids that react vigorously with
water, releasing hydrogen gas. With nonmetals such as carbon, nitrogen,
oxygen, and the halogens, hydrogen instead shares electrons, forming
covalent hydrides (such as CH4, NH3, H2O, and HCl) whose properties range
continuously across the periodic table, from flammable gases to strongly
acidic solutions in water. Hydrogen's compounds with transition metals form
a third, more variable category, often described as interstitial or
metallic hydrides, where hydrogen atoms occupy gaps within the metal
lattice.

Electrolysis is the process of using electrical energy to drive a
non-spontaneous chemical reaction, most commonly the decomposition of water
into hydrogen and oxygen gas, or the decomposition of a molten ionic
compound into its elements. An external power source forces electrons onto
one electrode (the cathode) and pulls them away from the other (the anode);
at the cathode, the surplus of electrons drives reduction — in water
electrolysis, hydrogen cations (effectively H3O⁺ or H2O) gain electrons and
are reduced to hydrogen gas — while at the anode, the deficit of electrons
drives oxidation — water molecules or hydroxide ions lose electrons and are
oxidised to oxygen gas. This electrode assignment for electrolysis is worth
fixing firmly: reduction always happens at the cathode and oxidation always
happens at the anode, in both electrolytic and galvanic (spontaneous) cells,
even though which electrode is positively or negatively charged differs
between the two cell types.

Electrolysis and hydrogen chemistry meet directly in fuel cell technology, a
device that runs the electrolysis of water in reverse: hydrogen gas is
oxidised at one electrode and oxygen gas is reduced at the other, with the
overall reaction releasing electrical energy directly rather than heat,
making hydrogen fuel cells an actively researched route to clean energy
conversion.
