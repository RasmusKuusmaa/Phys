The Schrodinger equation for a molecule with more than one electron cannot be solved exactly, because every electron's motion is genuinely coupled to every other electron's through their mutual electrostatic repulsion -- moving one electron changes the force felt by all the others simultaneously. The Hartree-Fock method makes this problem computationally tractable through one specific, deliberate approximation: instead of tracking each electron's instantaneous repulsion with every other individual electron, it replaces that repulsion with the electron's interaction with an averaged, smeared-out charge distribution built from all the other electrons. This converts one impossibly coupled many-electron problem into a set of much simpler one-electron problems, each electron moving in an averaged field created by the rest.

Because each electron's averaged field depends on the orbitals of all the other electrons -- which are themselves exactly what is being solved for -- the resulting equations must be solved iteratively rather than directly. Starting from a reasonable initial guess for the orbitals, the averaged field is computed, new orbitals are solved for using that field, and the cycle repeats: the newly obtained orbitals are used to recompute an updated averaged field, which produces yet another round of orbitals, and so on. This procedure continues until the orbitals stop changing meaningfully from one cycle to the next, at which point the field used to generate the orbitals and the orbitals themselves are mutually consistent -- which is precisely why the method is called self-consistent field theory.

Hartree-Fock recovers the great majority of a molecule's total electronic energy this way, typically well over 99%, but it leaves out a specific, systematic contribution called electron correlation energy: the averaging approximation cannot capture electrons' genuine tendency to actively avoid each other beyond what the smeared-out picture describes. Though correlation energy is a small fraction of the total, it is frequently the chemically decisive fraction, since it governs the comparatively small energy differences between competing molecular structures, reaction barrier heights, and bond dissociation energies far more sensitively than it affects the large total energy itself. Recovering this missing correlation energy, through various different strategies, is exactly the goal of the post-Hartree-Fock methods and density functional theory that build directly on the Hartree-Fock foundation.
