Benzene's ring of six delocalised pi electrons is, in one sense, exactly the
kind of electron-rich target that attracts electrophiles, just as an
ordinary alkene's pi bond does. Yet where an alkene reacts with an
electrophile by addition — the pi bond breaking and both carbons gaining
new substituents — benzene instead reacts by substitution, in which one
hydrogen on the ring is replaced by the electrophile while the aromatic ring
itself survives intact. Understanding why requires following the mechanism
through both of its steps and comparing the energetic cost of each possible
outcome.

The first step, common to both reaction types, is the same: an electrophile
is attracted to the ring's electron density and forms a new bond to one
ring carbon, using two of the six delocalised pi electrons. This breaks the
full aromatic delocalisation and produces a positively charged intermediate
(sometimes called an arenium ion or Wheland intermediate) in which the
remaining four pi electrons are delocalised over the other five carbons,
and the carbon bonded to the electrophile is now sp3-hybridised, bearing
both the electrophile and its original hydrogen.

At this point the two reaction types diverge. If the ring behaved like an
ordinary alkene, a nucleophile would simply attack this cationic
intermediate, permanently converting one ring carbon to sp3 and destroying
aromaticity for good. Instead, the intermediate has an available path back
to full aromaticity: losing the hydrogen still attached to the
electrophile-bearing carbon, as a proton, regenerates a carbon-carbon pi
bond there and restores the complete six-electron delocalised ring — the
same enormous stabilisation energy (aromatic stabilisation) that made
benzene so unreactive toward addition in the first place is recovered by
taking this path, and essentially never recovered by the addition path, so
substitution dominates overwhelmingly under normal conditions. The net
transformation replaces one hydrogen with the electrophile while the
aromatic ring itself — the six-membered ring with its full complement of
delocalised electrons — is regenerated exactly as it started, just with one
new substituent in place of one hydrogen.
