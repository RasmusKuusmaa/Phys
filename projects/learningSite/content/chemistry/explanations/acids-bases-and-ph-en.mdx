The workable definition of an acid is a proton donor and of a base a proton
acceptor. In water this shows up as the concentration of hydrogen ions, and
because that concentration ranges over more than fourteen powers of ten, it is
reported on a logarithmic scale: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. Neutral water sits at 7, acids
below, bases above.

The logarithm is where intuition goes wrong. pH 3 is not "twice as acidic" as
pH 6 — the difference of three units means a factor of 10³, so it is a thousand
times more acidic. Small-looking pH changes are large chemical changes, which is
why blood is held between 7.35 and 7.45 and why a drop of one unit in ocean pH
is a serious event rather than a minor one.

Strength and concentration are also routinely conflated, and they are
independent. Strength describes how completely an acid dissociates: hydrochloric
acid gives up essentially every proton and is strong, acetic acid gives up a
small fraction and is weak, regardless of how much of either is present.
Concentration describes how much is dissolved. A dilute strong acid can easily
have a higher pH than a concentrated weak one. Finally, the 0–14 range is a
convenience, not a rule: concentrated strong acids reach negative pH and
concentrated bases exceed 14, since the definition is just a logarithm and
nothing in it stops at either end.
