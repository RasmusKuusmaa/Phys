Every standard amino acid shares a common core: one central carbon atom bonded simultaneously to an amine group, a carboxylic acid group, a hydrogen atom, and one additional group called the side chain. What distinguishes the twenty standard amino acids from one another is entirely this side chain, which varies enormously -- from a single hydrogen atom to bulky aromatic rings, from electrically neutral to positively or negatively charged, from strongly water-attracting to strongly water-repelling. These are real, substantial chemical differences, not arbitrary labels, and they are exactly what gives each amino acid distinct chemical behavior within a protein chain.

Joining two amino acids together forms a peptide bond: the carboxylic acid group of one amino acid reacts with the amine group of the next, releasing a water molecule as the two groups combine into an amide linkage. This is a genuine condensation reaction with a real byproduct, not simply attaching two units together with no other chemical consequence, which is exactly why breaking a protein back down into its constituent amino acids (hydrolysis) requires adding water back in at each linkage. A long chain of amino acids joined this way, called a polypeptide, has a defined chemical directionality: one end retains a free amine group, the other a free carboxylic acid group, and the chain is conventionally read from the amine end to the carboxylic acid end.

The exact sequence of side chains along this chain -- which of the twenty standard amino acids occupies each position, in what order -- is a protein's primary structure, and it is nothing more or less than that one-dimensional sequence. Yet this sequence alone is what fundamentally determines everything that follows: the specific combination of side-chain properties (polarity, charge, size, hydrogen-bonding capacity) at each position dictates which three-dimensional fold is energetically favorable for that particular chain, which is why changing even a single amino acid at one position can substantially alter or destroy a protein's folded structure and, with it, its biological function. Primary structure is therefore the genuine starting point from which a protein's entire three-dimensional shape, and ultimately its function, follows.
