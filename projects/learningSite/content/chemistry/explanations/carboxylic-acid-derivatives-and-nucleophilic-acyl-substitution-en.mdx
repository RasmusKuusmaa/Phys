Carboxylic acid derivatives — acid chlorides, anhydrides, esters, and
amides — all share a common structural core: a carbonyl carbon bonded to an
oxygen (the carbonyl oxygen) and to a second substituent that can, in
principle, leave as a stable anion or neutral molecule (a chlorine in acid
chlorides, a carboxylate in anhydrides, an alkoxy group in esters, an amine
in amides). This shared structure gives them a shared, distinctive reaction
pathway with nucleophiles: nucleophilic acyl substitution.

The mechanism begins exactly like nucleophilic addition to a simple
ketone: a nucleophile is drawn to the electron-poor carbonyl carbon and
attacks it, using the C=O pi electrons to form a new carbon-oxygen bond
(building on the same polarisation logic as ordinary carbonyl chemistry),
producing a tetrahedral intermediate. For a ketone, this tetrahedral
intermediate, once protonated, is the final product. For a carboxylic acid
derivative, the tetrahedral intermediate instead has a leaving group still
attached, and rather than persisting, it collapses: the carbon-oxygen pi
bond re-forms, expelling the leaving group as the carbonyl carbon returns
to its flat, sp2-hybridised form. The overall transformation therefore
replaces the original leaving group with the incoming nucleophile while
regenerating the carbonyl group — an addition followed immediately by an
elimination, together amounting to a net substitution at the carbonyl
carbon, distinct from the simple addition seen with ketones and aldehydes
that lack any leaving group to expel.

How readily a given carboxylic acid derivative undergoes this substitution
depends almost entirely on how good a leaving group it carries — a better
leaving group (one that is more stable once it has departed, typically
because its negative charge or lone pair is better stabilised) is expelled
more easily, and this maps directly onto a clear reactivity order. Acid
chlorides, with chloride as an excellent, weakly basic leaving group, are
the most reactive, reacting readily even with weak nucleophiles. Anhydrides
are somewhat less reactive, with a carboxylate leaving group. Esters, with
an alkoxide leaving group, are markedly less reactive still. Amides, with a
nitrogen-based leaving group that is a comparatively poor leaving group
(amide nitrogen holds its lone pair tightly, both because nitrogen is less
electronegative than oxygen and because that lone pair is delocalised into
the carbonyl, strengthening the C-N bond), are the least reactive of the
common derivatives, which is exactly why amide (peptide) bonds are
biologically stable enough to hold proteins together under everyday
conditions, requiring dedicated enzymatic catalysis to hydrolyse rapidly.
