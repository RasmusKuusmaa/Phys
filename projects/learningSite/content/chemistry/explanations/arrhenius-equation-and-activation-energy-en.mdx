Reaction rates are strikingly sensitive to temperature — a common
rule-of-thumb estimate is that many reaction rates roughly double for every
10°C rise, a far larger effect than can be explained by the modest increase
in how often molecules collide as they move faster at higher temperature.
The Arrhenius equation, k = A·e^(−Ea/RT), explains this sensitivity by
identifying its real source: not collision frequency, but the fraction of
those collisions energetic enough to actually react.

Collision theory holds that a reaction between two molecules can occur only
if they collide with the correct orientation and with combined kinetic
energy exceeding a threshold called the activation energy, Ea — a minimum
energy required to distort and partially break existing bonds on the way to
forming new ones, corresponding to the energy needed to reach the
transition state, the highest-energy configuration along the reaction
pathway. Even at a fixed temperature, molecules in a gas or liquid have a
spread of kinetic energies (described by the Maxwell-Boltzmann
distribution), and only the high-energy tail of that distribution, the
fraction of molecules with energy above Ea, is actually capable of
reacting on collision.

This is exactly where the exponential term in the Arrhenius equation comes
from: the Boltzmann factor, e^(−Ea/RT), gives the fraction of molecules
with energy at least Ea at absolute temperature T, and this fraction grows
exponentially, not linearly, as temperature rises — a modest temperature
increase can substantially enlarge the high-energy tail of the distribution
even though the average molecular speed increases only modestly. The
pre-exponential factor A accounts for how often molecules collide with the
correct orientation, a comparatively weak, roughly temperature-independent
contribution by comparison. Because Ea sits in the exponent, reactions with
a large activation energy are far more temperature-sensitive than those
with a small one — a useful practical consequence is that plotting the
natural logarithm of measured rate constants against 1/T gives a straight
line (ln k = ln A − Ea/RT), from which both the activation energy and the
pre-exponential factor can be extracted experimentally, without needing any
prior theoretical knowledge of the reaction mechanism.
