A mass spectrometer identifies molecules by measuring the mass-to-charge
ratio (m/z) of ions derived from a sample, and the instrument's basic
logic proceeds in three stages. First, the sample is ionised — commonly by
bombarding gas-phase molecules with a beam of electrons energetic enough to
knock an electron out of a molecule, producing a positively charged
molecular ion. This ionisation step is essential precisely because
everything that follows relies on manipulating the resulting ions with
electric and magnetic fields, which exert no force on neutral particles.
Second, the ions are accelerated and separated according to their m/z
ratio — in a magnetic sector instrument, ions moving through a magnetic
field are deflected onto curved paths whose radius depends on m/z, so ions
of different mass-to-charge ratios physically separate; other instrument
designs (time-of-flight, quadrupole) achieve the same separation by
different physical means. Third, a detector records the abundance of ions
arriving at each m/z value, producing the mass spectrum: a plot of relative
abundance against m/z.

The peak corresponding to the intact, unfragmented ionised molecule (the
molecular ion peak) gives the compound's molecular mass directly and
precisely — often precisely enough to distinguish between molecular
formulas that would otherwise be indistinguishable by ordinary mass
measurement. But the ionisation process frequently supplies enough excess
energy to break some of the molecular ions apart into smaller charged
fragments before they are separated and detected, and these fragment ions
appear as additional peaks at lower m/z values throughout the spectrum.
Rather than being noise to filter out, this fragmentation pattern is
itself richly informative: because a molecule tends to break apart at its
weaker bonds and in ways that produce more stable fragment ions, the
specific pattern of fragment masses acts as a structural fingerprint,
often allowing a chemist to work out which functional groups and
substructures were present in the original molecule, in addition to
knowing its overall mass — which is exactly why mass spectrometry is used
throughout organic chemistry, biochemistry, and environmental and forensic
analysis both to identify unknown compounds and to confirm the structure
of known ones.
