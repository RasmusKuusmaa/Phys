Raoult's law describes how a solution's vapour pressure relates to the
vapour pressures of its pure components: for an ideal solution, the partial
vapour pressure contributed by each volatile component equals that
component's vapour pressure in its pure form, multiplied by its mole
fraction in the solution. For a solution of a non-volatile solute in a
volatile solvent, this means the solvent's vapour pressure above the
solution is simply reduced in proportion to how much the solute's presence
has diluted the solvent's mole fraction — a purely statistical dilution
effect: adding solute particles reduces the fraction of the liquid surface
occupied by solvent molecules capable of escaping into the vapour phase.

This single dilution effect turns out to be the common root of an entire
family of colligative properties — properties of a solution that depend
only on the number (concentration) of dissolved solute particles, not on
what those particles chemically are. Because the solvent's vapour pressure
is lowered, the temperature must be raised further than for the pure
solvent before the vapour pressure reaches the external pressure required
for boiling, producing boiling point elevation. By the same underlying
logic, applied to the solid-liquid equilibrium instead, dissolved solute
lowers the temperature at which the solution's chemical potential matches
that of the pure solid, producing freezing point depression — this is
exactly why salt is spread on icy roads, lowering the freezing point of the
resulting salt-water mixture below the ambient temperature. A third
colligative property, osmotic pressure, arises when a solution is separated
from pure solvent by a membrane permeable only to solvent molecules:
solvent flows from the pure side toward the solution to reduce the solution's
comparatively low solvent chemical potential, and the pressure that must be
applied to the solution side to exactly stop this net flow is the osmotic
pressure, which likewise depends only on solute particle concentration.

The critical qualifier "particle concentration," not "solute concentration
as chemically defined," has a directly measurable consequence: a solute
that dissociates into multiple ions in solution, such as sodium chloride
splitting into Na⁺ and Cl⁻, contributes roughly twice as many dissolved
particles per mole of compound as a molecular solute like glucose that does
not dissociate at all, and produces correspondingly larger colligative
effects — freezing point depression, boiling point elevation, and osmotic
pressure — per mole of compound dissolved, precisely because colligative
properties track the total count of dissolved particles, not the identity or
formula-unit count of the original solute.
