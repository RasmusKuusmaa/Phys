When light passes through a sample containing a substance that absorbs at
the wavelengths used, some of that light is absorbed and the beam emerges
weaker than it entered. UV-Vis spectroscopy measures this absorption as a
function of wavelength, and because molecules absorb most strongly at
wavelengths matching the energy gaps between their electronic states, the
resulting absorption spectrum acts as a fingerprint that identifies the
absorbing species and, quantitatively, how much of it is present.

The Beer-Lambert law puts this quantitative relationship on a precise
footing: absorbance A (defined as the logarithm of the ratio of incident to
transmitted light intensity) equals ε·c·l, where ε is the molar
absorptivity (a constant specific to the absorbing species and the
wavelength used), c is the concentration of the absorbing species, and l is
the path length the light travels through the sample. Two proportionalities
are packed into this single equation, and both matter for practical
measurement. First, absorbance is directly proportional to concentration at
fixed path length — this is the basis of essentially all quantitative
UV-Vis analysis, since measuring the absorbance of a series of standard
solutions with known concentrations and plotting absorbance against
concentration produces a straight calibration line, from which an unknown
sample's concentration can be read directly once its absorbance is
measured. Second, absorbance is directly proportional to path length at
fixed concentration, which is why the cuvettes used to hold samples in a
spectrophotometer have a precisely fixed, known path length (very commonly
1 cm), so that path length can be treated as a constant and concentration
extracted cleanly from a single absorbance reading.

The Beer-Lambert law holds well at low to moderate concentrations, but real
measurements deviate from strict linearity at high concentration — closely
packed absorbing molecules begin to interact with each other, and
instrumental effects such as stray light become proportionally more
significant, both of which curve the absorbance-concentration relationship
away from the ideal straight line. Reliable quantitative analysis therefore
requires keeping sample concentrations within the range where the law's
linear approximation genuinely holds, typically verified by confirming the
calibration curve remains straight across the concentrations of interest.
