Certain atomic nuclei, including hydrogen-1 and carbon-13, possess a nuclear spin, and placed in a strong external magnetic field, that spin can align in one of two orientations with slightly different energies. A nucleus can absorb a photon of exactly the right radiofrequency to flip from the lower-energy orientation to the higher-energy one, and this resonant absorption is what nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy detects. Crucially, the exact resonant frequency for a given type of nucleus is not universal: the surrounding electron cloud partially shields the nucleus from the full external field, and how much shielding occurs depends on the local chemical environment (what the nucleus is bonded to, how electronegative its neighbours are). This shifts the resonant frequency by a small but chemically diagnostic amount, reported on a standardized scale as the chemical shift, so hydrogens in different chemical environments within the same molecule show up as distinct, identifiable signals.

A second layer of structural information comes from spin-spin coupling: a hydrogen's nuclear spin creates a tiny local magnetic field felt by nearby, non-equivalent hydrogens (typically on an adjacent carbon), splitting what would otherwise be a single sharp signal into a recognizable multiplet pattern. The number of peaks in this pattern follows the n+1 rule, directly counting how many non-equivalent neighbouring hydrogens are present -- equivalent hydrogens on the same carbon, by contrast, do not split each other's signal at all, since they experience identical chemical environments.

Taken together, an NMR spectrum's three independent pieces of information -- chemical shift (revealing each hydrogen's local chemical environment), splitting pattern (revealing the number and arrangement of its neighbouring hydrogens), and signal intensity (revealing the relative number of chemically equivalent hydrogens of each type) -- provide enough independent structural evidence to deduce a genuinely unknown molecule's actual connectivity from scratch, not merely to confirm a suspected identity against a reference spectrum. This combination of information is what makes NMR spectroscopy one of the single most powerful and widely used tools for structure elucidation in modern organic chemistry.
