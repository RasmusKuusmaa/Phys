The word "oxidation" is a historical fossil. It was coined for reactions with
oxygen, but the underlying process is electron loss, and oxygen is merely a
common and enthusiastic electron acceptor. Sodium burning in chlorine, iron
dissolving in acid and a battery discharging are all oxidation with no oxygen
anywhere. The modern definition — oxidation is loss of electrons, reduction is
gain — is the one that generalises.

Because electrons are neither created nor destroyed, the two halves are
inseparable. Every electron lost by one species is gained by another, so
oxidation and reduction occur together and in exactly matched amounts. It is
often useful to write the two half-reactions separately and then scale them so
the electrons cancel, which is also how redox equations get balanced.

The terminology inverts in a way that reliably catches people. The *oxidising
agent* is the substance that causes oxidation in something else — which it does
by taking electrons, so the oxidising agent is itself reduced. The reducing
agent is oxidised. Agent names describe what a species does to its partner, not
what happens to it. Keeping that straight matters because the whole of
electrochemistry is built on it: in a battery, separating the two half-reactions
and forcing the electrons to travel through an external wire is exactly what
turns a chemical reaction into a usable current.
