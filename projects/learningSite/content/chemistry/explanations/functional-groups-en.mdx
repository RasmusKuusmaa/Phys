Organic chemistry could in principle mean memorising the reactions of every one
of the millions of known carbon compounds. It does not, because reactivity in
an organic molecule is almost entirely local: it lives in small clusters of
atoms called functional groups — the –OH of an alcohol, the C=O of a carbonyl,
the –COOH of a carboxylic acid — and the rest of the molecule, the carbon
skeleton the group is attached to, is largely a spectator.

This is why ethanol and a fatty alcohol with twenty carbons both fizz with
sodium metal, releasing hydrogen: both carry an –OH group, and that group is
what reacts. The chain length changes physical properties — boiling point,
solubility, viscosity — substantially, but it does not change *which*
reactions occur. Learning "alcohols react with sodium" is learning a fact about
every alcohol at once, not about ethanol specifically.

This locality is what makes organic chemistry learnable rather than an
impossible memorisation task. A first course is organised around perhaps a
dozen functional groups precisely because that dozen accounts for the
overwhelming majority of reaction types a molecule can undergo. Recognising
which functional groups are present in an unfamiliar molecule, then, is the
single most useful skill in the subject: it converts "I have never seen this
exact compound" into "I know how each of its pieces behaves."
