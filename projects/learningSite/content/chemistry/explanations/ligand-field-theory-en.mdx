Crystal field theory explains a key feature of transition metal chemistry: surrounding ligands split a metal's five otherwise energetically identical d-orbitals into two (or more) distinct energy groups, since some orbitals point directly at the ligands and feel more repulsion than others that point between them. The model treats each ligand as nothing more than a point negative charge, and this purely electrostatic picture correctly predicts the qualitative splitting pattern -- which orbitals go up in energy and which go down -- for a given geometry of ligands around the metal.

Crystal field theory fails, however, at a specific and important quantitative question: predicting how strongly different ligands actually split the d-orbitals. The experimentally observed ranking of ligands by splitting strength, the spectrochemical series, does not track ligand charge the way pure electrostatics would predict. Some neutral ligands, such as carbon monoxide, split the d-orbitals far more strongly than some negatively charged ligands do, a result the point-charge model has no way to explain, since a neutral point charge should produce less electrostatic repulsion than a negatively charged one, not more.

Ligand field theory resolves this by replacing the purely electrostatic picture with a genuine molecular-orbital treatment: metal d-orbitals and ligand orbitals of matching symmetry combine to form new bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals, exactly as in ordinary molecular orbital theory. Covalent orbital overlap, not simple electrostatic repulsion, now contributes directly to the resulting energy splitting, and a ligand's position in the spectrochemical series is explained by how effectively its orbitals overlap covalently with the metal's d-orbitals -- carbon monoxide, despite being neutral, has orbitals exceptionally well suited to strong covalent overlap with metal d-orbitals (including a specific back-bonding interaction), which is exactly why it splits the d-orbitals so strongly despite carrying no charge at all. Ligand field theory is therefore not merely an alternative name for crystal field theory but a strictly more general and accurate framework, one that reduces to something resembling the older electrostatic picture only in the limiting case where covalent overlap happens to be negligible.
