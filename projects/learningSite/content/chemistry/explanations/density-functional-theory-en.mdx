The many-electron wavefunction is a formidable object: it depends simultaneously on the coordinates of every electron in a molecule, so its complexity grows explosively with the number of electrons. The Hohenberg-Kohn theorems established a genuinely surprising alternative: a molecule's ground-state electron density -- a far simpler object, a single function of just three spatial coordinates, describing how much electron charge sits at each point in space -- contains, in principle, exactly the same complete information as the full wavefunction for determining every one of the molecule's ground-state properties, including its total energy. This is a rigorous mathematical equivalence between density and wavefunction for ground-state properties, not a simplifying approximation that discards information.

Density functional theory (DFT) reformulates quantum chemistry around this fact, searching for the unknown functional -- the precise mathematical rule -- that connects a given electron density to the corresponding ground-state energy. Crucially, though, the Hohenberg-Kohn theorems are an existence proof: they guarantee such an exact functional exists without revealing what its explicit form actually is. Practical DFT therefore necessarily works with approximate functionals, developed and refined over decades of research, rather than the unknown exact one -- which is exactly why different approximate functionals can give somewhat different numerical answers for the same molecule, and why choosing an appropriate functional for a given chemical problem is itself an important practical skill.

Where DFT most clearly earns its keep relative to Hartree-Fock is in how it handles electron correlation. Hartree-Fock approximates the wavefunction directly by averaging away instantaneous electron-electron repulsion, systematically omitting correlation energy entirely. DFT's approximate functionals, by contrast, are specifically constructed to include an exchange-correlation term that captures a substantial fraction of this same correlation energy, recovering much of what Hartree-Fock leaves out, often at only a modest increase in computational cost over Hartree-Fock itself and at a small fraction of the cost of the most accurate wavefunction-based post-Hartree-Fock methods. This favorable balance of accuracy and computational cost is what has made DFT the dominant practical tool across much of modern computational chemistry.
