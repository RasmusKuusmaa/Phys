The carbonyl group, C=O, found in ketones, aldehydes, carboxylic acids, and
their derivatives, is one of the most reactive functional groups in organic
chemistry because of a single structural fact: oxygen is considerably more
electronegative than carbon, so the shared electron density of the double
bond sits unevenly, pulled toward oxygen. This leaves the carbonyl carbon
bearing a partial positive charge and the oxygen a partial negative charge —
the bond is strongly polarised rather than the roughly nonpolar carbon-carbon
double bond found in alkenes.

This polarisation directly sets up the carbonyl group's dominant reaction
mode: nucleophilic addition. A nucleophile is drawn to the electron-poor
carbonyl carbon (not the oxygen, which is already electron-rich) and forms a
new bond there, using the carbon-oxygen pi bond's electron pair, which
moves entirely onto the oxygen. The result is a tetrahedral intermediate —
the carbon, previously flat and sp2-hybridised as part of the planar C=O
group, becomes sp3-hybridised and bonded to four different groups, with the
oxygen now bearing a full negative charge (an alkoxide) that is typically
protonated in a subsequent step. This mechanism, nucleophile attacks
carbon, pi electrons move to oxygen, underlies reactions ranging from
simple addition of water or alcohols across the carbonyl to more elaborate
reactions with organometallic reagents used to build new carbon-carbon
bonds in synthesis.

The same carbonyl polarisation opens a second, complementary reactivity
mode centred not on the carbonyl carbon itself but on the carbon
immediately adjacent to it, the alpha carbon. A C-H bond on the alpha
carbon is unusually acidic — far more acidic than an ordinary C-H bond
elsewhere in the molecule — because removing that proton produces a
carbanion whose negative charge is not stuck on one carbon but can
delocalise into the adjacent carbonyl group, spreading onto the more
electronegative oxygen and gaining substantial stabilisation in the
process; this delocalised, resonance-stabilised species is called an
enolate. The neutral, protonated form of this same delocalised structure is
the enol, existing in a dynamic equilibrium with the ordinary "keto" form —
keto-enol tautomerism — and this alpha-carbon acidity and enol/enolate
chemistry underlies a large family of carbon-carbon bond-forming reactions
in organic synthesis, including aldol condensation.
