An enzyme-catalyzed reaction proceeds through two steps: the enzyme first binds its substrate reversibly, forming an enzyme-substrate complex, before that complex proceeds onward to release product and regenerate free enzyme. This two-step mechanism produces a reaction rate with a distinctive dependence on substrate concentration. At low substrate concentration, most enzyme molecules sit unoccupied at any given moment, waiting for a substrate molecule to bind, so the reaction rate rises roughly in direct proportion to substrate concentration -- more substrate simply means more frequent binding events, exactly as an ordinary first-order reaction would behave.

As substrate concentration continues to rise, however, an ever-larger fraction of the enzyme's fixed total population becomes occupied at any given moment, and the rate's further increase slows. Eventually, at sufficiently high substrate concentration, essentially every enzyme molecule is continuously bound to substrate and actively working -- a condition called saturation -- and the reaction rate levels off completely at a maximum value, since the fixed total amount of enzyme present, not the still-increasing substrate concentration, is now the limiting factor. This genuine saturation behavior, with no counterpart in an ordinary uncatalyzed first-order reaction, is exactly what the Michaelis-Menten equation captures mathematically.

Two parameters fully characterize this behavior for a given enzyme acting on a given substrate. The maximum rate is the rate approached as substrate concentration becomes very large, directly proportional to the total amount of enzyme present (doubling the enzyme doubles the number of active sites available, and so doubles the maximum rate). The Michaelis constant is the substrate concentration at which the reaction rate reaches exactly half that maximum, reflecting the effective balance between how readily the enzyme-substrate complex forms and falls back apart versus how readily it proceeds on to product -- a fixed characteristic of that particular enzyme-substrate pair, not a measure of how much substrate happens to be present in any one specific reaction mixture. Together, these two parameters describe an enzyme's catalytic behavior far more completely than any single rate constant could, and comparing them across different enzymes or different substrates is a standard, central tool of biochemical analysis.
