Oxygen forms compounds, oxides, with almost every other element, and how an
oxide behaves in water follows a clear trend tied to the periodic table.
Oxides of strongly electropositive metals, such as sodium oxide (Na2O),
dissolve in water to give basic (alkaline) solutions — the metal's low
electronegativity means it readily gives up electron density to oxygen,
leaving the oxide ion free to accept a proton from water and generate
hydroxide ions. Oxides of strongly electronegative nonmetals, such as sulfur
trioxide (SO3), instead dissolve to give acidic solutions — the nonmetal
holds electron density tightly, and the resulting molecule reacts with water
by accepting a hydroxide-like fragment and releasing a proton. Between these
extremes, elements near the metal-nonmetal boundary of the periodic table,
such as aluminium and zinc, form amphoteric oxides that react with both
strong acids and strong bases, a direct chemical signature of an element
sitting on that boundary.

Oxoacids extend this same logic to acids built around a central atom bonded
to one or more oxygen atoms, at least one of which also bears an ionisable
hydrogen (such as sulfuric acid, H2SO4, or nitric acid, HNO3). Two factors
control an oxoacid's strength. First, for a fixed number of oxygen atoms,
acid strength increases with the central atom's electronegativity, since a
more electronegative central atom pulls electron density away from the O-H
bond, weakening it and making the proton easier to release. Second, and
often more dramatically, for the same central element in different
oxidation states, acid strength increases sharply with the number of
additional (non-hydrogen-bearing) oxygen atoms attached — compare
hypochlorous acid (HClO, chlorine oxidation state +1) with perchloric acid
(HClO4, chlorine oxidation state +7): each additional highly electronegative
oxygen atom withdraws further electron density from the O-H bond and also
stabilises the resulting anion by spreading its negative charge across more
oxygen atoms, both effects favouring proton loss and making perchloric acid
one of the strongest common acids while hypochlorous acid is comparatively
weak.
