Chromatography encompasses a whole family of separation techniques -- gas chromatography, liquid chromatography, thin-layer chromatography, and others -- that all apply the same underlying physical principle in different practical forms. A mobile phase (a flowing gas or liquid) continuously carries a mixture of compounds past or through a stationary phase (a fixed solid, or a liquid coated onto a solid support). Each component in the mixture interacts with the stationary phase to a different degree -- adsorbing to it, dissolving into it, or otherwise being retained by it more or less strongly -- and this differing relative affinity, not any other physical property directly, is what drives the separation.

A component that interacts strongly with the stationary phase spends more of its time retained there and correspondingly less time being swept along by the mobile phase, so it travels through the system more slowly. A component that interacts only weakly with the stationary phase, by contrast, spends most of its time in the flowing mobile phase and moves through quickly. Repeated over the length of a chromatography column (or the distance across a thin-layer plate), these small differences in relative retention accumulate into a substantial physical separation, with components eventually emerging at different times (or ending up at different positions) purely as a consequence of their differing affinities for the two phases. Crucially, this separation mechanism has nothing directly to do with molecular weight or size: two components of very similar molecular weight can separate cleanly if their chemical affinities for the stationary phase differ substantially, while two components of quite different molecular weight might separate poorly if their affinities happen to be similar.

A further important feature is that chromatography requires no prior knowledge of what the mixture's components actually are. The physical separation itself depends only on each component's relative affinity for the two phases, not on the components already being individually identified beforehand; identifying each separated component (often by applying a second, complementary technique such as mass spectrometry to the separated fractions) is a genuinely distinct step that follows the physical separation, rather than being required before it. This ability to separate a genuinely unknown mixture into its physically distinct components, with identification handled afterward as a separate task, is exactly what makes chromatography one of the most general-purpose and widely used separation and purification techniques throughout modern chemistry.
