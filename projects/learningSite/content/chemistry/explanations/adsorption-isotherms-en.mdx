Adsorption is the accumulation of gas or dissolved molecules onto a solid
(or liquid) surface, distinct from absorption, in which molecules are taken
up into the bulk of a material rather than sticking only to its surface. A
naive model might expect the amount adsorbed to simply grow in proportion
to gas pressure, but real adsorption data consistently shows the amount
adsorbed rising steeply at low pressure and then levelling off toward a
maximum at high pressure — a saturation behaviour that a simple
proportional model cannot explain, but that the Langmuir isotherm accounts
for directly.

The Langmuir model rests on a small set of physically motivated
assumptions: the solid surface offers a fixed number of identical
adsorption sites, each site can hold at most one adsorbed molecule
(monolayer coverage), and adsorbed molecules do not interact with each
other. Crucially, the model treats adsorption as a dynamic equilibrium
rather than a one-way process: molecules continuously adsorb onto empty
sites at a rate proportional to both the gas pressure and the fraction of
sites still empty, while adsorbed molecules continuously desorb at a rate
proportional to the fraction of sites currently occupied. Setting the
adsorption and desorption rates equal at equilibrium and solving for the
fractional surface coverage θ yields the Langmuir isotherm equation,
θ = KP/(1 + KP), where P is the gas pressure and K is an equilibrium
constant reflecting how strongly the gas binds to the surface.

This equation reproduces exactly the saturation behaviour seen
experimentally. At low pressure (KP much less than 1), the isotherm
reduces to θ ≈ KP — coverage increases roughly linearly with pressure,
since plenty of empty sites remain available and adsorption dominates over
desorption. At high pressure (KP much greater than 1), the isotherm
approaches θ ≈ 1 — coverage saturates near complete monolayer occupation,
since essentially every site is filled and further pressure increases can
add very little more. This finite-sites, saturating behaviour is the
qualitative signature the Langmuir model is built to capture, and though
real surfaces often deviate from its simplifying assumptions (multiple
adsorption layers, non-identical sites, interactions between adsorbed
molecules — addressed by more elaborate isotherms such as the BET model),
the Langmuir isotherm remains the essential starting point for
understanding gas-solid adsorption, with direct application to
heterogeneous catalysis, where the reaction rate on a catalyst surface
often depends directly on how much of that surface is covered by reactant
molecules.
