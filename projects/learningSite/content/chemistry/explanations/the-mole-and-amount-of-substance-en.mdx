Chemistry happens between individual atoms and molecules, but nobody can count
them out one at a time — a visible speck of anything contains a number of
particles with more than twenty digits. The mole bridges that gap. It is a
counting unit, like "dozen" but vastly larger: one mole is 6.022 × 10²³
entities, the Avogadro number.

The point of fixing that particular number is what it does to masses. The
molar mass of a substance in grams per mole is numerically the same as the
mass of one of its particles in atomic mass units. Carbon atoms have mass 12 u,
so a mole of carbon weighs 12 g. Water molecules have mass 18 u, so a mole of
water weighs 18 g. A balance, which measures grams, therefore becomes an
instrument for counting molecules — and that is the whole reason chemists can
work quantitatively at all.

Two confusions are worth heading off. A mole is not an amount of mass: a mole
of helium weighs 4 g and a mole of iron 56 g, but both contain the *same
number* of atoms, which is the point. And molar mass (grams per mole, a
property of a bulk sample) is not the mass of one molecule (atomic mass units,
a property of a single particle) — the numbers coincide, deliberately, but the
units and the meanings do not. Nor does state of matter change anything: a mole
is a mole whether the substance is gas, liquid or solid.
