Group 15 of the periodic table — nitrogen, phosphorus, arsenic, antimony,
and bismuth — shows the same top-to-bottom shift from nonmetallic to
metallic character seen across many groups, but with an unusually dramatic
range of chemical behaviour along the way. Nitrogen at the top is a
strongly electronegative, gaseous nonmetal; phosphorus and arsenic are
nonmetals or metalloids that exist as solids with several structural forms;
antimony and bismuth at the bottom show increasingly metallic properties,
including metallic lustre and electrical conductivity.

Nitrogen's chemistry deserves particular attention because it is central to
both industrial chemistry and biology, and because it displays an
unusually wide span of oxidation states — essentially every integer from -3
to +5. In ammonia (NH3), nitrogen is at oxidation state -3, having gained
electron density from three hydrogen atoms in each covalent bond; in nitric
acid (HNO3), nitrogen is at +5, its electron density pulled heavily toward
three oxygen atoms. Between these extremes, nitrogen forms compounds at
essentially every intermediate oxidation state, including nitrous oxide
(N2O, +1), nitric oxide (NO, +2), and nitrogen dioxide (NO2, +4), among
others — a range matched by very few other elements and central to the
nitrogen cycle in the environment, industrial fertiliser production, and
atmospheric chemistry.

Elemental nitrogen gas, N2, is a striking exception to nitrogen's general
reactivity: despite nitrogen atoms individually being highly electronegative
with five valence electrons eager to form bonds, the two nitrogen atoms in
N2 are joined by an exceptionally strong triple bond, among the strongest
single chemical bonds known. Breaking this bond requires a large input of
energy, which is why nitrogen gas, despite making up roughly 78% of the
atmosphere and being thermodynamically capable of many exothermic reactions,
remains kinetically almost inert at ordinary temperatures — industrial
nitrogen fixation (the Haber process, converting N2 and H2 into ammonia)
requires high temperature, high pressure, and a catalyst specifically to
overcome this kinetic barrier.
