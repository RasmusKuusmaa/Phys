The standard electrode potential of a half-reaction, tabulated at standard
conditions (1 M concentration, 1 atm pressure, a fixed reference
temperature, and referenced against the standard hydrogen electrode), is a
fixed, well-defined quantity — but a real electrochemical cell operating
under ordinary, non-standard conditions has a voltage that generally
differs from the value calculated by simply combining standard electrode
potentials, and that voltage is not constant over time as the cell
discharges. The Nernst equation supplies the correction: E = E° −
(RT/nF)ln(Q), where E° is the standard cell potential, n is the number of
electrons transferred in the balanced cell reaction, F is Faraday's
constant, and Q is the reaction quotient — the same ratio of product to
reactant concentrations (or activities) that appears in the equilibrium
constant expression, but evaluated at the cell's actual, instantaneous
concentrations rather than at equilibrium.

This single equation explains two related, practically important facts
about real cells. First, as a battery discharges, reactants are consumed
and products accumulate, so Q grows continuously, and because Q sits
inside a logarithm subtracted from E°, a growing Q steadily pulls the cell
potential down from its initial value — this is exactly why a battery's
voltage sags gradually rather than staying constant until it abruptly runs
out. Second, and more fundamentally, the Nernst equation reveals what "cell
potential" actually measures: it is a measure of how far the cell's overall
redox reaction is from equilibrium, in the same sense that Gibbs energy
measures distance from equilibrium for any reaction. As the reaction
proceeds and Q approaches the equilibrium constant K, the potential E
approaches zero, and E = 0 exactly when Q = K — that is, a "dead" cell that
can no longer do electrical work is not out of material in some vague
sense, but has specifically reached chemical equilibrium in its cell
reaction, at which point there is no remaining thermodynamic driving force
to push electrons through an external circuit.

The Nernst equation's dependence on concentration also makes it a practical
measurement tool: since E responds predictably to the concentration of a
species involved in the electrode reaction, measuring a cell's potential
provides a way to determine an unknown concentration (or activity,
dissociation constant, or solubility product) — the basis of
potentiometric measurement techniques including pH electrodes and
ion-selective electrodes used throughout analytical chemistry.
