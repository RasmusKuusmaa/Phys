A single strand of DNA is built from repeating nucleotide units, each consisting of a sugar, a phosphate group, and one of four nitrogenous bases (adenine, thymine, guanine, or cytosine). Successive nucleotides link sugar-to-phosphate into a continuous backbone, and this backbone, like a polypeptide chain, has a defined chemical direction: one end (conventionally called the five-prime end) differs chemically from the other (the three-prime end). A single strand alone is simply a linear sequence of bases strung along this directional backbone.

DNA's iconic double helix arises from a separate, additional chemical fact: each of the four bases can form stable hydrogen bonds with only one specific complementary partner. Adenine pairs stably only with thymine, and guanine pairs stably only with cytosine -- this strict rule is enforced by the precise hydrogen-bonding pattern each pair's chemical structure provides and by the geometric fit needed to keep the double helix's width perfectly uniform along its entire length; other base combinations simply do not fit correctly or cannot form matching hydrogen bonds. Because of this exact pairing rule, the two strands of a double helix are always found running in opposite chemical directions, called antiparallel: where one strand's backbone runs from its five-prime end toward its three-prime end, its partner runs the opposite way.

This strict, one-to-one complementarity has a profound structural consequence: knowing the complete base sequence of just one strand automatically and exactly determines the sequence of its complementary partner, position by position, with no ambiguity at all. This is not a loose correlation observed after the fact but an exact structural requirement built directly into each base pair's chemistry. It is precisely this property that makes DNA replication possible: when the two strands of a double helix separate, each original strand can serve directly as a template, specifying exactly which base must be added at each position to build a new, exact complementary partner -- the chemical foundation underlying how genetic information is faithfully copied every time a cell divides.
