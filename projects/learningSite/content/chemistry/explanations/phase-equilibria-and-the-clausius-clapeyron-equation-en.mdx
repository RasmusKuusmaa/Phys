A pure substance's phase diagram maps out which phase — solid, liquid, or
gas — is thermodynamically stable at each combination of temperature and
pressure, and the lines separating these regions are not arbitrary: each
boundary line is the set of temperature-pressure pairs at which two phases
have exactly equal molar Gibbs energy, and can therefore coexist in
equilibrium. Move off the boundary in either direction and one phase's
Gibbs energy drops below the other's, and the system converts entirely to
the lower-energy phase; this is exactly why a pure substance's melting or
boiling point is not a single fixed number but a whole curve of
temperature-pressure pairs — "the boiling point of water" implicitly means
"at standard atmospheric pressure," and at any other pressure the
liquid-vapour boundary specifies a different equilibrium temperature.

The Clausius-Clapeyron equation makes this pressure-temperature relationship
quantitative by working out the slope of the phase boundary directly from
the requirement of equal Gibbs energy along it. For the liquid-vapour
boundary specifically, treating the vapour as an ideal gas and assuming its
molar volume vastly exceeds the liquid's (an excellent approximation, since
gases are far less dense than liquids), the equation simplifies to
d(ln P)/dT = ΔHvap/(RT²), relating the slope of the vapour pressure curve to
the molar enthalpy of vaporisation, ΔHvap. Integrating this relation shows
that vapour pressure rises with temperature, exponentially in the simplest
approximation — which is exactly consistent with the everyday fact that
liquids evaporate faster and boil at a lower external pressure requirement
as temperature increases, and, read the other way, why boiling point rises
with increasing external pressure, as in a pressure cooker, and falls at the
reduced pressure of high altitude.

The magnitude of the enthalpy of vaporisation directly controls how steeply
vapour pressure changes with temperature: substances with weak
intermolecular forces and a small ΔHvap have vapour pressure curves that
respond only mildly to temperature changes, while substances with strong
intermolecular forces and a large ΔHvap show vapour pressure that changes
dramatically over the same temperature range — a direct, quantitative link
between a substance's microscopic intermolecular forces and its macroscopic
phase behaviour.
