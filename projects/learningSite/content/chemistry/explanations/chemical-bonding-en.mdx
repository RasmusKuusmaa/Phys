Atoms bond because the bonded arrangement is lower in energy than the separated
one. That single fact settles the most persistent misconception in chemistry:
breaking a bond always *costs* energy, and forming one always releases it.
Reactions give out heat when the bonds formed in the products are stronger than
the bonds broken in the reactants — never because breaking bonds released
something.

The textbook categories are ionic and covalent: electrons transferred outright
versus electrons shared. But these are the two ends of one continuum, not two
separate species. The controlling quantity is electronegativity difference. A
large difference pulls the electron across completely and gives ions; no
difference gives equal sharing; anything between gives a polar covalent bond,
shared but lopsided. Water is the important middle case — the O–H bonds are
covalent yet strongly polar, which is what makes water an excellent solvent for
salts and the reason ice floats.

That position on the continuum is what actually predicts behaviour. Ionic
solids form rigid lattices, so they melt high, dissolve in water and conduct
once molten or dissolved. Covalent molecular substances are held to each other
only by weak intermolecular forces, so they melt low and rarely conduct. The
octet rule is a useful pattern behind much of this, but it is a rule of thumb
with real exceptions — boron is content with six electrons, phosphorus and
sulfur routinely exceed eight — and treating it as a law causes more errors than
it prevents.
