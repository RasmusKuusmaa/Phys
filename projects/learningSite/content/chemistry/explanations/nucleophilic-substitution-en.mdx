A nucleophile is electron-rich — it has a lone pair or a region of negative
charge looking for somewhere to go — and it attacks a carbon that is
electron-poor, typically because a more electronegative atom nearby has pulled
density away from it. Attaching to that carbon displaces whatever was there
before, the leaving group, which departs taking the bonding electrons with it.
That is the whole reaction in outline: electron-rich attacks electron-poor,
something else leaves.

The mechanistic detail is where it gets interesting, because there are
genuinely two distinct pathways. In one, the nucleophile arrives at the same
moment the leaving group departs — a single concerted step, with no
intermediate ever existing. In the other, the leaving group departs first,
producing a short-lived carbocation intermediate, and the nucleophile attacks
that afterwards, in a separate second step. These are not two names for the
same thing: the two-step pathway is governed by how stable the intermediate
carbocation is, so it is favoured at more substituted carbons where that
intermediate is stabilised, while the one-step pathway is favoured at simpler,
less hindered carbons and depends on how strong and available the nucleophile
is.

Knowing which pathway is operating is what lets a chemist predict rate,
stereochemistry and product identity correctly, rather than guessing. It is
also worth separating nucleophile from base: both are electron-rich, and the
same species — hydroxide, for instance — can act as either, but a nucleophile
attacks carbon while a base removes a proton, and which role dominates in a
given reaction depends on the substrate and conditions, not on some fixed
property of the reagent alone.
