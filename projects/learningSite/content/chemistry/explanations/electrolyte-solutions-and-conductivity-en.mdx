An electrolyte is a substance that produces ions in solution, and
electrolytes fall into two categories that behave very differently. A
strong electrolyte, such as hydrochloric acid or sodium chloride,
dissociates essentially completely into its constituent ions when
dissolved, so a solution's ion concentration can be calculated directly
from the nominal concentration of dissolved compound. A weak electrolyte,
such as acetic acid or ammonia, dissociates only partially, reaching a
dynamic equilibrium between the undissociated molecule and its ions, with
the equilibrium position (and hence the actual fraction dissociated)
governed by the electrolyte's dissociation constant and the solution's
concentration. Because a weak electrolyte's dissociation is genuinely an
equilibrium, its degree of dissociation changes with concentration in a
way a strong electrolyte's does not.

This distinction is directly measurable through a solution's conductivity —
its ability to carry electric current, which requires mobile charge
carriers (ions) to be present. Molar conductivity, the conductivity
normalised per mole of dissolved electrolyte, isolates how efficiently each
mole of compound contributes to conduction, independent of concentration.
For a strong electrolyte, molar conductivity decreases only mildly as
concentration increases, reflecting the fact that essentially all the
compound is already dissociated at any concentration — the small decrease
that does occur is due to increasing ion-ion electrostatic interactions
at higher concentration slowing ion movement, not a change in how much has
dissociated. For a weak electrolyte, molar conductivity drops far more
sharply as concentration increases, because increasing concentration
actually shifts the dissociation equilibrium toward the undissociated form
(consistent with Le Chatelier's principle applied to the dissociation
equilibrium), reducing the fraction of the compound that is actually
ionised and therefore able to carry current. Extrapolating molar
conductivity to infinite dilution, where ion-ion interactions vanish and,
for weak electrolytes, dissociation becomes essentially complete, gives the
limiting molar conductivity — a quantity that, for strong electrolytes, can
be measured directly, and that for weak electrolytes must be obtained
indirectly (via Kohlrausch's law of independent ion migration) since full
dissociation is never actually reached at any finite concentration.
