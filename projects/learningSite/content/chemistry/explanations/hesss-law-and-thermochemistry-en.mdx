Enthalpy, the heat content chemists track for reactions run at constant pressure, is a state function: its value depends only on the identity of a system's current state (its composition, temperature, and pressure), not on the specific path or sequence of steps used to reach that state. This has a striking direct consequence for chemical reactions: the total enthalpy change between a fixed set of starting reactants and a fixed set of final products is exactly the same no matter which route, mechanism, or sequence of intermediate steps is actually used to get from one to the other.

Hess's law exploits this fact directly as a calculational tool: the overall enthalpy change for converting reactants into products can be calculated by adding together the enthalpy changes of any convenient sequence of steps connecting the same starting reactants to the same final products, even a sequence of entirely hypothetical reactions that the real chemical process never actually passes through mechanistically. Because enthalpy depends only on the initial and final states, this substitution is entirely legitimate: any correctly balanced sequence of steps beginning at the same reactants and ending at the same products, added together with appropriate signs and stoichiometric coefficients, must give exactly the true overall enthalpy change, regardless of whether those intermediate steps correspond to anything physically real.

This gives chemists a genuinely practical tool, not merely a theoretical curiosity. Many chemically important reactions are too slow, too dangerous, produce mixtures of products rather than one clean product, or are otherwise simply impractical to measure directly by calorimetry. Hess's law lets chemists calculate such a reaction's enthalpy change indirectly instead, by combining the measured enthalpies of other, more convenient reactions -- often reactions with tabulated, well-known enthalpies of formation -- that connect the same starting reactants and final products through an entirely different, experimentally accessible pathway.
