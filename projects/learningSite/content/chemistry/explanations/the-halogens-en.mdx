Every halogen atom has seven outer electrons and needs exactly one more to
complete a stable shell, which is why the group is so uniformly reactive — and
also why it is not uniform in *how* reactive. Reactivity in this group means
how eagerly an atom pulls in that one electron, and that pull weakens
predictably as the group is descended: fluorine's outer shell sits close to a
strongly felt nucleus, while iodine's sits several shells further out, shielded
and less attracted. Fluorine is consequently the most powerful oxidiser known in
ordinary chemistry; iodine is comparatively mild.

That gradient has a direct, testable consequence: a more reactive halogen
displaces a less reactive one from its compounds. Bubble chlorine through
potassium bromide solution and chlorine takes the electron bromide had, forming
chloride and releasing free bromine — never the reverse. The displacement series
follows the group exactly, top to bottom, which is what makes it a genuine test
of relative reactivity rather than an arbitrary list to memorise.

Free halogens are diatomic — Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ — because a lone halogen atom, one
electron short of stability, immediately pairs with another to share the
missing electron covalently. They are essentially never found as isolated
atoms in nature, and the compounds they form with metals, the halides, are
generally stable ionic salts, since a metal readily gives up the electron a
halogen wants. This combination — extremely reactive as an element, extremely
stable once combined — is characteristic of the whole group and is the reason
halogens turn up as compounds throughout the periodic table's chemistry.
