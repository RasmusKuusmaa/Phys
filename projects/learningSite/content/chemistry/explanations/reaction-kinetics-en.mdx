Thermodynamics says which direction a reaction *would* go; kinetics says
whether it will get there this century. The two are independent, and the gap
between them is why diamond does not turn into graphite while you watch even
though doing so would release energy. The conversion is favourable and
effectively infinitely slow, because rearranging the carbon lattice demands an
enormous activation energy that room temperature never supplies.

Rate comes from collisions. Reactant particles must meet, hit hard enough to
clear the activation barrier, and be oriented correctly when they do. Every
practical way of speeding a reaction acts on one of those three. Higher
concentration or pressure means more frequent collisions. Larger surface area —
powder instead of a lump — exposes more particles to collide. Higher temperature
does the most work, not by making collisions slightly more frequent but by
pushing far more molecules into the high-energy tail of the distribution that
can actually clear the barrier.

Catalysts work differently again: they open a new pathway with a lower
activation barrier, so a much larger fraction of collisions succeeds. A catalyst
takes part in the mechanism — it is not a bystander — but is regenerated by the
end, so it is not consumed and a tiny amount can process an unlimited quantity.
And note what it cannot do: lowering the barrier speeds both directions equally,
so a catalyst never changes where equilibrium lies or makes an unfavourable
reaction favourable. Finally, rate laws are found by experiment, not read off
the balanced equation — the coefficients describe the overall stoichiometry, not
the slow step that actually controls the rate.
