A carbon atom bonded to four different substituent groups (a stereocentre) has no internal mirror plane, meaning its mirror image is a genuinely distinct spatial arrangement that cannot be rotated to superimpose exactly onto the original -- much like a left hand and a right hand look alike but can never be rotated to overlap perfectly. Such a molecule is called chiral, and its two mirror-image forms are called enantiomers. Chemists specify exactly which spatial arrangement a given enantiomer has using the R/S naming convention: the four groups attached to the stereocentre are ranked by a fixed priority rule, and the direction (clockwise or counterclockwise) in which priority decreases when viewed from the appropriate side determines whether the centre is labeled R or S.

Enantiomers are identical in essentially every ordinary physical property -- melting point, boiling point, density, solubility, and their infrared or ordinary NMR spectra are all the same, because these properties arise from interactions that cannot distinguish a molecule from its own mirror image. The one property that does differ is optical activity: each enantiomer rotates plane-polarized light passing through it by the same angle but in opposite directions, which is why polarimetry, not any ordinary physical test, is the classic experimental way to detect and distinguish enantiomers.

A crucial consequence follows from this symmetry: an ordinary chemical reaction carried out with no chiral influence present anywhere in the system (no chiral catalyst, no chiral starting material, no chiral solvent) has nothing available to favour one mirror-image outcome over the other, and so produces both enantiomers in exactly equal amounts, a racemic mixture. Producing a genuine excess of one specific enantiomer (an enantioselective synthesis) requires deliberately introducing a chiral influence, typically a chiral catalyst, and remains a genuinely challenging synthetic goal. This same underlying asymmetry has real, sometimes life-or-death consequences in biology and medicine: biological receptors, enzymes, and other proteins are themselves inherently chiral molecules, so one enantiomer of a drug can fit a biological binding site precisely, the way a specific hand fits a specific glove, while its mirror-image partner fits poorly, binds a completely different site, or produces an entirely different (sometimes harmful) effect -- all despite the two enantiomers being chemically indistinguishable by any ordinary achiral laboratory measurement.
