Mendeleev built his table by atomic mass, and it worked well enough that he
could leave gaps and predict the missing elements. But mass ordering has
inversions — argon is heavier than potassium yet clearly belongs before it —
and the modern table is ordered by atomic number, the proton count. That is not
a technicality: the proton count fixes the electron count, and the electrons are
what chemistry is made of.

The table's shape follows from how those electrons fill shells. Elements in the
same *group* (column) have the same number of outer electrons, and since bonding
is an outer-electron affair, they behave alike — the alkali metals all react
violently with water, the noble gases all sit inert with a full outer shell.
Elements in the same *period* (row) share only a shell number and are chemically
unrelated: sodium and chlorine are neighbours in period 3 and could hardly
differ more.

The trends are what make the table predictive rather than merely tidy. Across a
period the nuclear charge rises while electrons enter the same shell, so the
pull inward strengthens and atoms get *smaller*, not larger — an unintuitive
result that catches many people. Ionisation energy rises with it, and metallic
character falls. Down a group each element adds a whole new shell, so atoms grow,
outer electrons are held more loosely, and ionisation energy falls. Those two
directions together explain most of descriptive inorganic chemistry without
memorising it element by element.
