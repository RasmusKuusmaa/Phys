Every organic reaction begins with a bond breaking somewhere, and there are
exactly two fundamentally different ways a covalent bond's shared electron
pair can be divided between the two atoms that were bonded. In homolytic
cleavage, the two bonding electrons split evenly, one going to each atom,
producing two neutral species that each carry an unpaired electron — these
are called radicals. Radicals are highly reactive because an unpaired
electron is energetically unfavourable; radicals typically react further by
seeking out another unpaired electron to pair with. Homolysis is favoured
by conditions that supply energy without directing it toward a particular
atom, such as heat or ultraviolet light, and by weak, nonpolar bonds.

In heterolytic cleavage, both bonding electrons go to just one of the two
atoms, producing a cation (the atom that lost both electrons) and an anion
(the atom that gained both). This is the pathway that dominates ionic
organic chemistry, and it is favoured by polar bonds, where the electron
density is already unevenly distributed toward one atom before the bond even
breaks, and by polar solvents that can stabilise the resulting charged
species.

The species produced by heterolytic cleavage divide into two functional
roles that drive the overwhelming majority of ionic organic reaction
mechanisms. A nucleophile ("nucleus-loving") is an electron-rich species —
carrying a lone pair of electrons, a pi bond, or a negative charge — that
seeks out and donates electron density to a region of positive or partial
positive charge, forming a new bond in the process. An electrophile
("electron-loving") is the complementary electron-poor species — often
carrying a partial or full positive charge, or an empty orbital — that
accepts the electron pair a nucleophile donates. Almost every ionic organic
mechanism, from nucleophilic substitution to electrophilic addition, can be
described as a nucleophile attacking an electrophile, which is exactly why
correctly identifying which reacting species is the nucleophile and which is
the electrophile is the first and most essential step in predicting how an
organic reaction will proceed.
