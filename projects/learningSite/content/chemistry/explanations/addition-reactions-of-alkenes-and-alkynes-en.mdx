A carbon-carbon double bond consists of two different kinds of bonds
overlaid on each other: a sigma bond, formed by direct end-on orbital
overlap along the axis between the two nuclei, and a pi bond, formed by
sideways overlap of unhybridised p-orbitals above and below that axis. The
pi bond is weaker than the sigma bond and, because it is formed by
sideways rather than direct overlap, its electron density sits more exposed
above and below the plane of the molecule rather than tucked directly
between the two nuclei. This exposed, relatively loosely held electron
density is exactly what makes alkenes (and, more so, alkynes with their two
pi bonds) attractive targets for electrophiles — an electron-poor species
approaching the molecule is drawn toward this available electron density
long before it gets close to the more tightly held sigma-bond electrons.

The general mechanism for electrophilic addition proceeds in two main
steps. First, the electrophile is attracted to the pi bond and forms a new
sigma bond to one of the two alkene carbons, using the pi bond's electron
pair; this converts the alkene into a positively charged carbocation
intermediate at the other carbon, since that carbon lost its share of the pi
electrons without gaining a new bond. Second, a nucleophile — often the
counterion released alongside the electrophile — attacks this electron-poor
carbocation, forming a new bond and completing the addition. The net result
is that the carbon-carbon double bond becomes a single bond, and each of the
two carbons that were part of the double bond has gained one new
substituent, exactly matching the addition of one electrophile and one
nucleophile across the original pi bond.

This same two-step logic — electrophile attacks the pi bond, nucleophile
attacks the resulting cationic intermediate — extends to a wide family of
alkene reactions, including addition of hydrogen halides, addition of
halogens (Br2, Cl2), and, with water as the ultimate nucleophile and an
acid catalyst, hydration of alkenes to alcohols. Alkynes, with two pi bonds
available, can in principle undergo addition twice, first converting a
triple bond to a double bond and then, with a second equivalent of reagent,
converting that double bond to a single bond — though the intermediate
alkene product can often be isolated by controlling the reaction conditions.
