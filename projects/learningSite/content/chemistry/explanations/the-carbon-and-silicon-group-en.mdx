Group 14 — carbon, silicon, germanium, tin, and lead — shows the familiar
top-to-bottom trend from nonmetallic to metallic character, but the group's
chemistry is dominated by a striking contrast between its first two members
that shapes two entirely different domains of chemistry.

Carbon's defining property is catenation: an exceptional ability to form
strong, stable bonds with other carbon atoms, in chains, branches, and
rings, combined with strong bonds to hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and many
other elements, and the ability to form single, double, and triple bonds.
This combination of bonding versatility is what makes carbon the structural
backbone of essentially all organic chemistry and biological molecules —
no other element comes close to matching the sheer diversity of stable
compounds carbon can form. Silicon, directly below carbon, can also
catenate to a limited degree (silicon chains exist but are far less stable
than carbon chains, since silicon-silicon bonds are weaker and more
reactive), but silicon's chemistry is dominated instead by an exceptionally
strong bond to oxygen. This single fact reshapes silicon's entire chemical
identity: rather than forming small molecular oxides like carbon dioxide,
silicon reacts with oxygen to form silicon dioxide (SiO2), in which every
silicon atom bonds to four oxygen atoms and every oxygen bridges two silicon
atoms, building an extended three-dimensional covalent network rather than
discrete molecules — this is why quartz and other silica-based minerals are
hard, high-melting solids rather than gases, and why silicate minerals,
built on the same silicon-oxygen network principle, make up the large
majority of the Earth's crust.

Moving further down the group, germanium is a metalloid with properties
intermediate between nonmetal and metal, important in semiconductor
technology, while tin and lead show progressively more metallic character —
both are true metals with metallic lustre, malleability, and electrical
conductivity, though they retain some group 14 chemical signatures, such as
forming compounds in both the +2 and +4 oxidation states.
