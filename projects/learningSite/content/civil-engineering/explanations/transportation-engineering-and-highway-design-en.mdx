Highway capacity is governed by traffic flow theory, and the central relationship in that theory -- how flow rate (vehicles passing a point per unit time) depends on vehicle density (vehicles per unit length of road) -- is genuinely nonlinear, not the simple "more lanes, more throughput" intuition suggests. At low density, adding more vehicles increases flow rate almost proportionally, since vehicles can maintain both close spacing and high speed. But as density keeps rising, drivers must leave more following distance and reduce speed for safety, and past a critical density, flow rate actually starts to fall even as more vehicles try to use the road -- which is the underlying mechanism behind a "phantom" traffic jam that has no accident, lane closure, or other discrete cause: the flow-density relationship itself can produce a self-sustaining slowdown once density crosses that threshold, amplified by the fact that a driver braking slightly forces the driver behind to brake more, propagating a wave of slower speed backward through the traffic stream.

Pavement design solves an entirely different engineering problem: getting a vehicle's concentrated wheel load safely into the ground without exceeding the underlying soil's bearing capacity. A single stiff surface layer directly on soft soil would concentrate stress right at the point of contact, likely exceeding what the soil beneath can bear. Real pavement is instead built as a layered system -- a strong, stiff surface course, one or more intermediate base/sub-base layers of decreasing stiffness, and finally the natural subgrade -- each layer spreading the load over a progressively wider area so that by the time it reaches the weakest material (the subgrade soil), the stress per unit area is low enough for that soil to carry safely.

Geometric design -- how sharply a road curves, how far ahead a driver needs to see before a hazard, how on-ramps merge with through traffic -- is not an aesthetic or land-use choice; it is calculated directly from a driver's physical reaction time and a vehicle's braking performance at the road's design speed. A minimum stopping sight distance, for instance, is derived from how far a vehicle travels during a driver's reaction time plus how far it travels while braking to a stop, both evaluated at the design speed -- which is exactly why a curve that looks visually similar to another can have a very different, code-mandated minimum radius if it is designed for a higher speed.
