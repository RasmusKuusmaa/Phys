Concrete's fundamental structural limitation is an asymmetry between its two failure modes: it resists compressive crushing extremely well, but its tensile strength -- its resistance to being pulled apart -- is only roughly a tenth of that compressive strength. A beam under gravity load naturally develops both: the top fiber shortens under compression while the bottom fiber stretches under tension, and it is the tension side that fails first, cracking at a load far below what the same concrete could withstand in pure compression. Reinforced concrete exists entirely to fix this one asymmetry, and it does so by dividing the structural work between two materials along their own strengths rather than trying to make concrete itself behave symmetrically.

Steel reinforcing bars embedded specifically in the tension zone of a bending member pick up the tensile force concrete cannot reliably carry, while the surrounding concrete continues to carry the compressive force it was always good at -- the bond between the two materials is what transfers load back and forth as the member deforms under load. This is why placement matters as much as quantity: rebar embedded on the wrong side of a member (the compression side, where concrete already performs well) does almost nothing useful, since it never experiences the tension it exists to resist.

Two further facts make this partnership actually durable rather than just structurally clever on paper. First, concrete and steel share an almost identical coefficient of thermal expansion, so the composite member expands and contracts together as temperature changes -- if the two materials expanded at meaningfully different rates, ordinary daily or seasonal temperature swings would create internal stresses and cracking on their own, independent of any applied structural load. Second, concrete's high internal alkalinity passivates the embedded steel, forming a thin protective oxide layer that dramatically slows corrosion compared to bare steel exposed to air and moisture -- but only as long as the concrete cover surrounding the rebar remains intact. Once that cover cracks or carbonates enough to lose its alkalinity, the passivating protection is lost and the embedded steel becomes vulnerable to the same corrosion any exposed steel would face, which is exactly why concrete cover thickness and crack control are treated as durability issues, not merely cosmetic ones.
