A structural designer's real task is never "make it strong enough" against a single number -- it is holding a structure safe against several genuinely different load categories that each behave differently and can act at the same time. Dead load is the easiest to reason about: it is the structure's own permanent weight, fixed the moment the design is finalized, and present for the structure's entire service life. Live load is fundamentally different in character -- it is the variable, occupancy-driven weight a structure carries (people, furniture, stored goods), and because it changes over time and location, it is described statistically (a design value chosen to cover the vast majority of realistic occupancy scenarios) rather than as one fixed number the way dead load is.

Wind load and seismic load introduce a genuinely different problem: direction. Both are driven by external environmental events rather than the structure's own use, and critically, both act laterally as often as vertically -- wind pushes sideways on a building's face, and ground shaking during an earthquake accelerates a structure's mass horizontally. A structure engineered only to carry its own weight downward into its foundation can be entirely unprepared for a comparable force pushing sideways, which is exactly why lateral-load-resisting systems (shear walls, braced frames, moment-resisting connections) are a standard, universal part of structural design wherever wind or seismic risk exists, not a specialized concern reserved for unusually tall or exotic buildings.

Because dead, live, wind, and seismic loads can all act on a structure simultaneously -- a fully occupied building during a windstorm, for instance -- checking each load type against the structure in isolation would understate the real risk. Building codes address this directly with load combinations: prescribed weighted sums of multiple load types applied together, each combination representing a genuinely plausible simultaneous scenario, and a structure must be shown to survive the worst such combination, not merely the worst single load type checked on its own. Layered on top of every one of these calculations is a safety factor, which exists for a reason distinct from load combinations themselves: even a perfectly executed design rests on estimated loads (live load is inherently a statistical prediction, not a certainty) and on material properties that vary somewhat from their nominal, tested values -- the safety factor is the structure's margin against that irreducible uncertainty, on top of whatever margin protects against ordinary human error during construction.
