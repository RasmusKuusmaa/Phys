A bridge's essential job never changes -- carry load across a gap to its supports -- but the structural mechanism used to do it varies genuinely between bridge types, and that mechanism, not just the shape or the material, is what actually defines a bridge type. A truss bridge breaks the load path into an assembly of straight members arranged in triangles, and the geometry of a triangle is what makes this work: a triangular arrangement is rigid on its own, and each member within it ends up loaded in pure axial tension or pure axial compression rather than bending, which lets comparatively slender, lightweight members carry a load that would require a much heavier single beam to resist through bending alone.

A beam bridge takes the opposite approach: a single continuous member spans between its supports and resists the applied load through bending, developing compression along one face and tension along the other, exactly the mechanism a reinforced-concrete beam uses at building scale. Bending is a genuinely less efficient way to carry load than pure axial tension or compression, which is why beam spans are comparatively limited -- past a certain length, the beam itself must grow so deep or heavy to resist its own bending that the design becomes impractical, and other bridge types take over for longer spans.

An arch bridge solves the span problem differently again: its curved shape is chosen specifically so that, under its design load, internal forces run along the arch's curve as pure compression, with essentially no bending. This is precisely why masonry arch bridges built of stone -- a material with excellent compressive strength but almost no tensile strength -- could be built successfully millennia before any material with real tensile capacity existed; an arch simply never asks stone to do the one thing it cannot do.

A suspension bridge inverts that entire logic. Rather than compression along a curve, the roadway hangs from cables loaded in pure tension, which carry that tension up over towers and down into massive anchorages at each end. Because steel cable's tensile strength per unit weight so dramatically exceeds what any practical material achieves in compression, hanging the load in tension lets a suspension bridge span distances -- often well over a kilometer -- that no truss, beam, or arch built from the same modern materials could approach, which is exactly why the longest bridge spans in the world are, without exception, suspension or cable-supported designs.
