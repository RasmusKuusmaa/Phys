Every building or bridge, no matter how carefully its structural frame is engineered, ultimately rests on soil, and soil is nothing like a manufactured structural material -- it is a naturally occurring, highly variable substance whose ability to carry load (its bearing capacity) depends on particle size, density, moisture content, and how it is layered, all of which can differ substantially between two sites just a short distance apart, or even between two depths at the same site. This is why the geotechnical engineer's first task on any project is soil classification and site investigation, not a generic assumption about "the ground."

Coarse-grained soils (sand and gravel) and fine-grained soils (silt and clay) behave in genuinely different ways under load. Granular soils drain water quickly and, once compacted, gain strength almost immediately -- their bearing capacity is relatively predictable and stable. Clay-rich soils behave very differently: they can be quite strong when dry, but saturating them can sharply reduce their load-carrying capacity, and even after a structure is built, water trapped in the clay's microscopic pore spaces continues to be squeezed out slowly under the sustained weight above -- a process called consolidation that can produce measurable settlement for years after construction finishes, not just during it.

This variability is exactly why foundation type is a decision driven by the soil, not by the building. A shallow foundation -- a spread footing or a slab bearing directly on near-surface soil -- works wherever that near-surface soil has adequate, reasonably uniform bearing capacity. Where the near-surface soil is too weak, too variable, or prone to excessive long-term settlement, a deep foundation (driven piles, drilled shafts, or caissons) instead transfers the structure's load past that unreliable layer entirely, down to stronger soil or bedrock that may lie many meters below the surface. Two structurally identical buildings can therefore legitimately require completely different foundation systems if the ground beneath them is different enough -- the building's design loads matter, but the site's soil profile is what actually decides how those loads reach the earth.
