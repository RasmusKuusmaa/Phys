A watershed is defined entirely by topography: it is the land area within which every drop of precipitation, once it falls, drains toward a single common outlet -- a stream, river, or lake. The civil engineer's core hydrologic task on any site is estimating how much of the precipitation falling on that watershed becomes surface runoff (as opposed to infiltrating into the soil or evaporating), and how quickly that runoff reaches the outlet, since both the total volume and the timing determine how severe a flood peak actually is and how large a stormwater or flood-control system must be built to handle it.

Land development changes both of those quantities dramatically. Natural, vegetated ground absorbs a substantial fraction of rainfall and slows the rest as it works its way across and through the soil toward a stream. Pavement and rooftops do neither: essentially all rainfall on an impervious surface becomes runoff, and it reaches a storm drain or stream far faster than the same rainfall would have moved across natural ground. The combined effect -- more total runoff, arriving faster -- reliably increases both the peak flow rate of a flood event and how often flooding of a given severity occurs downstream, even in a watershed where average rainfall hasn't changed at all; the land itself, not the climate, is what changed.

Stormwater management exists to counteract exactly this effect, and its underlying strategy is not "move water away as fast as possible" -- doing that would simply deliver a larger, faster flood peak to whatever is downstream, shifting the problem rather than solving it. Instead, detention ponds, retention basins, and permeable pavement all work by temporarily storing runoff and releasing it more slowly than it arrived, spreading a sharp peak flow out over a longer time so the downstream channel never has to carry more than it safely can. Hydraulic structures like dams and culverts apply the identical underlying logic at a larger, permanent scale: a dam stores and releases water on a controlled schedule for flood control, water supply, or power generation, and a culvert beneath a road is deliberately sized to a calculated design flow (the flow rate expected during a storm of a chosen statistical frequency) so the road doesn't overtop and the channel upstream doesn't back up during that design event.
