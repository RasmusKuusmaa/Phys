Every competitive market has two independent sides that a supply-and-demand diagram puts on the same axes. The demand curve is drawn from buyers' side: at a high price, only the buyers who value the good most will pay, so quantity demanded is low; as price falls, more buyers find it worth their while, so quantity demanded rises -- hence the downward slope. The supply curve is drawn from sellers' side: at a low price, only the lowest-cost producers can profitably sell, so quantity supplied is low; as price rises, higher-cost producers can enter profitably too, so quantity supplied rises -- hence the upward slope.

Equilibrium is simply the one price where these two independently-determined quantities happen to match. It is not imposed by anyone; it emerges from the two sides of the market pulling against each other. Push the price above equilibrium and sellers, collectively, want to sell more than buyers want to buy -- the resulting unsold surplus gives sellers a direct incentive to cut price to move it, which pulls the market back down. Push the price below equilibrium and buyers want more than sellers are offering -- the resulting shortage gives buyers (and sellers, seeing lines out the door) a direct incentive to bid the price back up. This self-correcting pull is why a competitive market's price tends toward equilibrium even though no single participant is trying to set it there.

It's essential to keep two very different kinds of change straight. A change in the good's own price moves buyers and sellers along their existing curves -- this is not a "shift." A shift happens when something else changes: average income rising shifts the whole demand curve outward (more is demanded at every price, not just the current one); a new, cheaper production technology shifts the whole supply curve outward (more is supplied at every price). Shifts move the equilibrium itself to a new price-quantity combination; movements along a curve are just the market's response to that new equilibrium, not a separate cause.
