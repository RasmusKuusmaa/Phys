Market structure is really a spectrum defined by one underlying question: how much control does any single seller have over the price? At one end, perfect competition assumes so many identical sellers that no single one's output decision moves the market price at all -- each firm is a "price-taker," simply accepting whatever price the overall market sets and choosing how much to produce given that fixed price. This is why a wheat farmer can't meaningfully raise their own asking price above the going market rate: buyers would simply buy from the innumerable other wheat farmers instead.

Monopoly sits at the opposite end. With only one seller and no close substitute, that seller faces the market's entire downward-sloping demand curve directly, which means -- unlike a price-taker -- it cannot sell more without lowering price on every unit it sells. This changes the profit-maximizing calculation fundamentally: a monopolist deliberately produces less than a competitive market would, holding price higher than a price-taker ever could. The result isn't merely "buyers pay more, seller earns more" with the same total value simply moved around -- some mutually beneficial trades (a buyer willing to pay more than it costs the firm to produce one more unit) simply never happen under monopoly, a genuine loss of total economic value called deadweight loss, not just a redistribution.

Oligopoly occupies the middle ground, and its defining feature is neither price-taking nor unilateral price-setting but strategic interdependence: with only a few large firms sharing the market, each firm's pricing or output decision visibly affects its rivals' profits, so every firm must factor in how competitors are likely to respond before acting. A price cut that would be ignored in a market of thousands of tiny competitors can trigger a price war among a handful of large rivals, which is exactly the kind of strategic calculation neither perfect competition (too fragmented for any single firm's move to matter) nor monopoly (no rivals to react at all) ever needs to make.
