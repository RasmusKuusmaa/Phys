GDP is often treated in casual conversation as a stand-in for "how well a country is doing," but its actual definition is much narrower and more mechanical than that: the total market value of final goods and services produced within a country's borders over a period. Every word in that definition is doing real work. "Market value" means anything that doesn't pass through a market transaction -- a parent's unpaid childcare, a neighbor fixing your fence for free, a large informal/cash economy -- simply doesn't appear in the number, regardless of how much real economic activity it represents. "Final goods" means intermediate inputs (the steel that went into a car) aren't counted separately from the finished product they became part of, specifically to avoid counting the same value more than once.

Because GDP only tracks market output, it says nothing directly about distribution (a rising GDP is fully compatible with most of the gains flowing to a small share of the population) or about sustainability (GDP has no mechanism for subtracting environmental damage or resource depletion, and can even count cleanup spending after a disaster as a positive contribution). None of this means GDP is useless -- it's a genuinely useful, comparable measure of market production -- but conflating it with overall wellbeing is a category error the number itself was never designed to answer.

Inflation matters because GDP is measured in money, and money's value doesn't hold still. If prices doubled but nobody produced a single additional physical good, nominal GDP would still show 100% growth -- a pure illusion. This is why economists separate real GDP (adjusted to strip out the effect of price changes, using a base year's prices) from nominal GDP (measured in current prices): real GDP growth is the meaningful signal of whether an economy is actually producing more, while nominal GDP growth conflates that with inflation.

Unemployment rounds out the core trio, and it has its own specific blind spot: the official rate counts only people who are both jobless and actively searching for work. Someone who has searched for months without success and simply stops looking is reclassified as "not in the labor force" rather than "unemployed," which means the official rate can paint an artificially rosy picture during a prolonged downturn, precisely when discouraged-worker dropout is at its highest. Each of these three indicators -- output, price stability, employment -- captures something the other two miss entirely, which is why economists track all three rather than any single one as a complete health check on an economy.
