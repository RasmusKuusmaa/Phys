It seems obvious that a country which is more efficient than its neighbor at producing literally everything has nothing to gain from trading with that neighbor -- why trade for something you can already make better yourself? Comparative advantage is the insight that this intuition, however natural, is wrong, and the reason is a shift in what "efficiency" should even be compared against.

The relevant comparison isn't absolute output per worker or per hour across countries; it's opportunity cost within each country -- how much of good B a country must give up to produce one more unit of good A. Because opportunity cost is a ratio between two goods inside one economy, every country has a lowest opportunity cost at something, even a country that is absolutely worse at producing everything than its trading partner. If Country A is enormously better at both wheat and cloth but only slightly better at wheat specifically, then Country A's opportunity cost of producing wheat (in terms of cloth given up) is lower than Country B's -- Country A has the comparative advantage in wheat, and by the same logic, Country B, despite being absolutely worse at both, has the comparative advantage in cloth.

Specialization following comparative advantage, rather than absolute advantage, is what makes trade a genuine positive-sum outcome. Each country produces more of the good it gives up the least to make, trades for the other good, and both countries end up able to consume more of both goods combined than either could have produced entirely alone -- not because one side's gain came from the other's loss, but because specializing according to relative (not absolute) efficiency expands what the combined economies can produce in total.

Exchange rates then determine the actual terms at which this trade happens in practice: the price of one currency in terms of another, which sets how much foreign good a given amount of domestic currency will actually buy. Exchange rates move with shifts in the relative demand for each currency -- driven by trade flows themselves, differences in interest rates, and investor expectations about the future -- and a currency strengthening or weakening changes those trading terms (making imports cheaper or more expensive, exports more or less competitive) without touching the underlying comparative-advantage structure of real production tradeoffs at all; that structure is about physical resources and technology, not currency valuation.
