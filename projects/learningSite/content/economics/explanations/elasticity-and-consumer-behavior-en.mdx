Price elasticity of demand puts a number on something every seller already senses intuitively: raising price on some goods barely dents sales, while raising price on others sends buyers running to a competitor. The formula captures this directly -- it's the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. A value greater than 1 in magnitude means demand is elastic (quantity responds more than proportionally to price); a value less than 1 means demand is inelastic (quantity barely budges).

What actually determines where a good falls on this spectrum is substitutability, not price level or vague "importance." A specific brand of a common product has many close substitutes sitting right next to it on the shelf, so a price increase sends buyers straight to a competitor's version -- highly elastic demand. A good with no good substitute -- insulin for a diabetic, salt in a diet -- keeps being bought even as price rises, because there's genuinely nowhere else to turn, or because it's such a small share of the budget that a price change barely registers -- inelastic demand.

This has an immediate, practical consequence for anyone setting a price: the effect of a price change on total revenue (price times quantity) depends entirely on which side of that elasticity divide a good sits on. Raise price on an elastic good and the resulting drop in quantity sold outweighs the higher price per unit -- total revenue falls. Raise price on an inelastic good and quantity falls by proportionally less than price rose -- total revenue rises. This is precisely why firms selling near-inelastic goods can profitably raise prices in ways firms selling highly substitutable goods cannot.

Underneath all of this sits consumer-choice theory: a rational consumer is modeled as allocating a fixed budget across every good they might buy to maximize total satisfaction, weighing each additional unit of spending on one good against what that same money could buy elsewhere. Elasticity is, in this framework, simply a measurable symptom of how easily a consumer can reallocate that spending away from a given good when its price rises -- the easier the reallocation, the more elastic the demand.
