A national economy has two genuinely different levers that can be pulled to speed it up or slow it down, run by two different kinds of institution. Monetary policy belongs to the central bank, typically an institution deliberately kept somewhat independent from day-to-day politics, and its primary tool is the short-term interest rate. Cutting rates makes borrowing cheaper across the economy -- mortgages, business loans, credit -- which tends to encourage both consumer spending and business investment, providing stimulus when growth is sluggish. Raising rates does the reverse: more expensive borrowing cools spending and investment, which is the standard response when an overheating economy is pushing inflation too high. Central banks also influence the money supply more directly through open-market operations -- buying or selling government bonds -- though the interest-rate channel is usually the headline tool.

Fiscal policy belongs to the elected government, working through an entirely different channel: direct spending and taxation. Increased government spending injects money into the economy directly (a new infrastructure project puts money in workers' and contractors' pockets immediately), while tax cuts leave more money in households' and firms' hands to spend or invest themselves. Spending cuts or tax increases work the same logic in reverse, withdrawing money from the economy to cool it. Crucially, this is a mechanistically distinct channel from monetary policy's interest-rate lever -- it changes disposable income and direct government purchasing rather than the cost of borrowing.

Because these are different tools run by different institutions, they don't automatically move together, and sometimes work at cross purposes: a central bank might be cutting rates to stimulate growth at the very moment a government is raising taxes to reduce a budget deficit, each pulling the economy in a different direction. Neither lever is a free dial that can simply be cranked toward "more stimulus" without cost. Push expansionary monetary or fiscal policy too hard and the economy can overheat, with spending power outstripping what the economy can actually produce -- the classic recipe for inflation. Push contractionary policy too hard in the other direction and the resulting drop in spending and investment can tip a slowing economy into outright recession. Getting the balance right, and coordinating (or at least not actively working against) the other lever, is the genuine difficulty both kinds of policy face.
