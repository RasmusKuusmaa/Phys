Jean Piaget's theory of cognitive development rests on a genuinely radical claim for its time: children are not simply smaller, less-informed adults gradually filling up with facts, but reason in qualitatively different ways at different points in development, passing through a consistent sequence of stages. In the sensorimotor stage (roughly birth to age 2), infants lack object permanence -- the understanding that an object continues to exist even when out of sight -- and understand the world almost entirely through direct sensory and motor interaction with it. The preoperational stage (roughly ages 2-7) brings symbolic thought and language, but children at this stage still struggle with logical operations like conservation (the classic finding that a child insists a tall, narrow glass holds "more" water than a short, wide one holding the identical volume, because they're reasoning from a single perceptual dimension at a time) and struggle to take another person's perspective.

The concrete operational stage (roughly ages 7-11) is where conservation and other logical operations click into place, but reasoning stays tied to concrete, tangible situations. Only in the formal operational stage (from around age 12 onward) does abstract, hypothetical reasoning about possibilities that aren't concretely present become available. The key claim distinguishing this from a simple "more knowledge over time" story is that a preoperational child isn't missing a fact about volume -- they're missing the logical operation (conservation) that would let them reason about it correctly at all, however many times the demonstration is repeated.

Attachment theory addresses an entirely separate developmental question: not how children reason, but how their earliest relationships shape later social and emotional functioning. An infant whose caregiver responds consistently and sensitively to their needs tends to develop a secure attachment style, associated with confident exploration of the environment and, later in life, with more stable close relationships; inconsistent or unresponsive caregiving is associated with one of several insecure attachment patterns instead. Crucially, attachment style is a probabilistic tendency established early but continually reshaped by significant relationships across the lifespan, not a permanent verdict handed down in infancy.
