Social psychology's classic studies converge on a single, uncomfortable theme: situational forces shape behavior far more powerfully than most people intuitively expect, often overriding both private judgment and personal character. Solomon Asch demonstrated this with a deceptively simple task -- judging which of three comparison lines matched a reference line's length, a question with an obvious correct answer when judged alone. When surrounded by confederates who unanimously gave an incorrect answer, a substantial share of real participants conformed to the group's wrong answer at least once, despite the task's simplicity. Post-experiment interviews revealed many conforming participants privately knew the group's answer was wrong but complied publicly anyway, which is itself an important distinction: conformity here was often social compliance under pressure, not necessarily a genuine shift in private belief.

Stanley Milgram's obedience studies raised the stakes considerably: participants were instructed by an experimenter in a lab coat to deliver what they believed were increasingly severe (and eventually life-threatening) electric shocks to another person for wrong answers on a memory task. A clear majority of participants continued to the maximum shock level when the experimenter calmly insisted they continue, despite audible protests from the (actually unharmed, scripted) "victim." The standard interpretation is not that most people are secretly cruel -- follow-up variations showed obedience rates dropped sharply when the authority figure's legitimacy was reduced, when the victim was made more visible/proximate, or when another participant visibly refused first, showing the effect is driven by specific, manipulable situational factors rather than a fixed trait.

Attribution theory examines a related question with its own systematic bias: how people explain the causes of behavior, whether their own or someone else's. The fundamental attribution error names a robust asymmetry: people tend to explain other people's behavior by appeal to stable personality or character ("she's an inconsiderate driver") while readily explaining their own identical behavior by appeal to the surrounding situation ("I had no choice, given the emergency"). This asymmetry isn't a claim that situational or dispositional explanations are individually wrong -- both genuinely operate -- but that people systematically apply them unevenly depending on whose behavior is being explained.
