The single most important distinction in research methodology is between a correlational study and a true experiment, and it comes down to one design feature: random assignment. In a correlational study, a researcher simply measures two variables as they naturally occur (coffee consumption and anxiety, say) and checks whether they move together. Even a strong, statistically robust correlation leaves at least three explanations on the table: the first variable causes the second, the second causes the first, or some third variable (chronic stress, for instance) independently drives both. No amount of statistical significance resolves which of these is true, because significance measures how unlikely the observed pattern is to be pure chance, not which causal story produced it.

A true experiment breaks this ambiguity by having the researcher directly manipulate the proposed cause (assigning some participants to a coffee condition and others to a no-coffee condition) and using random assignment to decide who goes where. Randomization is doing real statistical work here: with a large enough sample, it makes the two groups equivalent on average across every other variable that might matter -- stress levels, sleep habits, personality -- so that any subsequent difference in anxiety between the groups can be attributed to the one thing that was deliberately varied.

Before any design question, though, comes a more basic one: how do you even measure something like "anxiety" or "intelligence," which can't be observed directly the way height or reaction time can? Operationalization is the process of committing to a specific, measurable stand-in for the underlying construct -- a validated anxiety questionnaire's score, say. Whether that specific operationalization actually captures what "anxiety" means is a question of validity, and it's entirely separate from whether the experimental design itself is sound; a perfectly randomized experiment measuring the wrong thing is still a flawed study.

Psychology faces two further wrinkles less central to, say, chemistry. Demand characteristics arise because human participants, unlike chemical reagents, can guess what a study is testing and change their behavior accordingly -- sometimes to "help" the researcher confirm the hypothesis, sometimes to defy it. And because so much of the field's historical evidence base was drawn from WEIRD populations -- a narrow, demographically unusual slice of humanity -- a finding's generalizability beyond that specific population is a real, testable question, not something that can simply be assumed.
