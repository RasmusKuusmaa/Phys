The trait approach to personality represents a specific, hard-won shift away from typological thinking. Rather than sorting people into a handful of discrete categories, decades of factor-analytic research -- statistically examining how hundreds of personality-descriptive words and questionnaire items cluster together across huge samples -- converged repeatedly on roughly five broad, continuous dimensions: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism (often remembered by the acronym OCEAN). Each dimension is a spectrum everyone occupies some position along, not a box someone is sorted into; a person isn't "an extravert" in the way they might be "a redhead," they're simply higher or lower than average on that one continuous dimension, often alongside being high, low, or middling on the other four independently.

Behavioral genetics addresses where these individual differences come from, using a clever natural experiment: comparing identical twins (who share essentially 100% of their genes) to fraternal twins or adopted siblings (who share less genetic overlap but often a similar upbringing). If identical twins resemble each other on a trait substantially more than fraternal twins do, that extra resemblance is attributed to shared genetics rather than shared environment. This method consistently finds heritability estimates in the 40-60% range for most personality traits across many independent studies.

The single most commonly misunderstood point about this number is what it's actually a statistic about. Heritability describes how much of the variation across a specific studied population is statistically attributable to genetic variation within that group -- it is not a description of any one individual's personality, and it cannot be sensibly divided up into "this much genetic, this much environmental" for a single person, any more than saying a plant's height is "60% due to genetics" tells you anything about how much water any one particular plant needs. It's also worth noting what typically accounts for the non-genetic share of the variation: research consistently points to each individual's unique, non-shared experiences (their particular friendships, specific life events) as a bigger contributor than the shared family environment two siblings grow up in together.
