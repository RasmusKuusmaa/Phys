The multi-store model of memory proposes that information passes through up to three qualitatively different systems, not one uniform storehouse. Sensory memory holds an almost-complete but fleeting copy of raw sensory input -- for vision, well under a second -- just long enough for attention to select what matters. What gets attended to moves into short-term (or "working") memory, which holds a handful of items actively but only for roughly seconds to a couple of minutes unless deliberately rehearsed. Only material that is further processed and consolidated makes it into long-term memory, which has vastly greater capacity and can retain information for a lifetime. Because information must pass through the earlier, more limited stores to reach the durable one, a brief distraction at the wrong moment can wipe out something that was never given the chance to consolidate.

Getting information into long-term memory (encoding) and later finding it again (retrieval) are separate processes, and each can independently fail -- a memory can be well-encoded but temporarily unretrievable (the "tip of the tongue" experience), or poorly encoded in the first place regardless of how good someone's retrieval strategies are. Retrieval itself is not a simple readout: it is reconstructive, actively rebuilding a memory from stored fragments rather than replaying an untouched recording, which is why retrieved memories can be subtly altered by information encountered afterward, by current mood, or by suggestive questioning.

Forgetting itself has at least two genuinely distinct causes. Decay is the straightforward idea that a memory trace simply weakens over time if it isn't used. Interference is a different mechanism entirely: other memories, particularly similar ones learned close together in time, actively compete for retrieval and disrupt access to the target memory. Interference is exactly why cramming two similar subjects back-to-back the night before an exam often produces worse long-term retention than spacing similar material out over several days -- the two memory traces are fighting each other for retrieval, not simply fading at the same natural rate.
