Two foundational learning mechanisms explain how experience changes behavior, and they operate in genuinely different ways. Classical conditioning, demonstrated by Ivan Pavlov's famous work with dogs, is learning by association between stimuli. A neutral stimulus (a bell, initially producing no relevant response) is repeatedly paired with a stimulus that already automatically triggers a response (food triggering salivation, an unconditioned reflex). After enough pairings, the previously neutral stimulus alone comes to trigger that same response -- salivation to the bell with no food present. Critically, the learner isn't doing anything active here; an involuntary, reflexive response is simply being attached to a new trigger through repeated pairing.

Operant conditioning, developed principally by B.F. Skinner, works on an entirely different kind of behavior: voluntary actions an organism emits, not automatic reflexes elicited from it. Here, the behavior's consequences shape how often it happens again. Reinforcement increases future frequency of the behavior it follows, and punishment decreases it -- and crucially, both can work either by adding something (positive reinforcement: a reward follows the behavior; positive punishment: an aversive consequence follows it) or by removing something (negative reinforcement: an ongoing aversive stimulus is removed when the behavior occurs, which increases the behavior, despite the word "negative"; negative punishment: a desired stimulus is taken away).

Beyond whether and how reinforcement happens, the specific pattern or schedule on which it's delivered has powerful, independent effects on behavior. A fixed-ratio schedule delivers reinforcement after a set, predictable number of responses. A variable-ratio schedule delivers it after an unpredictable number of responses that averages out to the same rate. Despite delivering identical total reinforcement on average, variable-ratio schedules reliably produce the highest response rates of any schedule and the greatest resistance to extinction (the behavior persists longest even once reinforcement stops entirely) -- precisely because the unpredictability itself keeps the organism responding in case "this next one" is the rewarded response. This is exactly the reinforcement architecture slot machines and many mobile-game reward systems are deliberately built around.
