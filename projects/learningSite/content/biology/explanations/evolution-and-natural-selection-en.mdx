Natural selection requires only three ingredients, and wherever all three are present, evolution by natural selection is not merely possible but mathematically inevitable. First, heritable variation: individuals in a population differ in some trait, and that difference is passed from parent to offspring. Second, differential survival and reproduction: individuals with certain variants of that trait survive and/or reproduce more successfully than individuals with other variants, in a given environment. Third, time across generations: because the advantaged variant is passed on more often, its frequency in the population rises with each successive generation. Nothing in this process requires an organism to "want" or "try" to change, and nothing requires new variation to appear on demand -- the process works entirely on variation that already exists for unrelated reasons (ultimately arising from mutation, recombination, and other sources of genetic variation covered elsewhere in this site's genetics content).

The evidence that this process has actually produced the diversity and design of life on Earth comes from several genuinely independent directions, which is what makes the case for evolution as strong as it is: no single line of evidence would be as convincing alone. The fossil record shows earlier, simpler forms giving way over deep time to a succession of later forms, exactly the temporal pattern common descent predicts. Comparative anatomy reveals homology -- structures like the forelimb bones of a bat's wing, a whale's flipper, and a human arm share the same underlying skeletal arrangement despite radically different functions, which makes sense only if all three inherited that arrangement from a shared ancestor and then modified it for different uses (as opposed to each independently evolving the same complex arrangement from scratch for unrelated functions). Molecular biology adds an entirely independent line of evidence: the degree of genetic similarity between any two species, measured directly from their DNA sequences, closely tracks the family relationships already inferred from fossils and anatomy long before DNA sequencing existed. And speciation -- new species arising from existing ones -- has been observed directly, both in the wild and in controlled laboratory populations over many generations, closing the gap between "evolution as an inference about the past" and "evolution as an observable, ongoing process."

A crucial point that separates natural selection from a folk-biology misunderstanding of it: selection has no foresight and no mechanism to manufacture a trait a population needs. It cannot look ahead to a future environmental challenge and generate the right adaptation in response; it can only act on whatever heritable variation happens to already be present, for reasons unrelated to any future need. A population lacking the right variation simply does not adapt to a new challenge, no matter how urgently that adaptation would help -- which is exactly why extinction, not adaptation, is evolution's most common outcome when environments change faster than existing variation allows a population to keep up.
