Photosynthesis runs as two physically separate but chemically coupled sets of reactions inside the chloroplast, and keeping the two straight is the key to understanding the whole process. The light-dependent reactions happen across the thylakoid membrane and do exactly what the name says: light striking chlorophyll excites electrons to a higher energy state, and the plant harvests that excitation in two ways -- splitting water molecules to replace the excited electrons (releasing O2 as a byproduct, which is where essentially all atmospheric oxygen ultimately traces back to) and using the electron's energy, via an electron transport chain, to pump protons across the thylakoid membrane and build a gradient that drives ATP synthesis. The net output of the light-dependent reactions is simply ATP and NADPH -- both energy-carrying molecules, neither one itself a sugar.

The Calvin cycle, in the chloroplast's stroma, takes those two energy carriers and uses them to do the actual carbon-fixing work: the enzyme RuBisCO grabs atmospheric CO2 and attaches it to an existing five-carbon sugar, and the ATP/NADPH from the light reactions power the subsequent steps that eventually regenerate that starting sugar and produce a net gain of fixed carbon that can be built into glucose. Because none of these steps directly require photons, the Calvin cycle is called "light-independent" -- but "independent of light" is not the same as "happens in darkness"; it depends entirely on ATP and NADPH the light reactions just supplied, so in the overwhelming majority of plants it proceeds during daylight, running in step with the light reactions rather than waiting for nightfall.

RuBisCO has a real limitation that becomes a serious problem in hot, dry environments: a plant conserves water by closing its stomata (the pores that let CO2 in and water vapor out), but closing them also lets internal oxygen concentration rise as CO2 is consumed, and RuBisCO can mistakenly bind O2 instead of CO2, triggering a wasteful process (photorespiration) that costs the plant energy without fixing carbon. Two independent evolutionary solutions to this same tradeoff exist. C4 plants add a spatial fix: they fix CO2 first in outer mesophyll cells using a different, oxygen-insensitive enzyme, then shuttle the resulting compound into inner bundle-sheath cells where it releases CO2 at high local concentration right where RuBisCO needs it, keeping RuBisCO's local environment CO2-rich even with mostly-closed stomata. CAM plants use a temporal fix instead: they open their stomata only at night when it's cooler and less water evaporates, fixing CO2 into a storage compound then, and run the Calvin cycle during the day with stomata closed, drawing on the stored carbon rather than needing open stomata in the heat. Both are genuine adaptive solutions to the identical water-vs-CO2 tradeoff C3 photosynthesis faces, not defective variants of it.
