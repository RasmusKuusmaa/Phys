Biological classification exists to answer one specific question consistently across all of biology: given any two organisms, how are they related? The modern answer is built on a strict nested hierarchy -- domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species, from most to least inclusive -- and the criterion for membership in each named group is, wherever the science allows it to be determined, shared evolutionary ancestry rather than convenient visual similarity.

The domain level is the broadest and, historically, the most surprising: Bacteria and Archaea look almost indistinguishable through a microscope (both single-celled, both lacking a nucleus), which is exactly why they were originally lumped together as one category of "prokaryote." Molecular sequencing revealed that this visual similarity was misleading -- Archaea differ from Bacteria at a deep molecular level (their cell membrane lipids are built differently, their RNA polymerase is structurally closer to the eukaryotic version, and several other core cellular processes diverge), and in several of these respects Archaea are actually closer to Eukarya (organisms with a nucleus, including all animals, plants, and fungi) than to Bacteria. This is a clean illustration of why classification built on deep molecular/genetic evidence can overturn classification built on surface appearance.

Binomial nomenclature -- the two-part Latin genus-species name every organism receives (Homo sapiens, Escherichia coli) -- solves a real, practical problem: common names vary by language and region, sometimes refer to multiple unrelated organisms, and sometimes fail to distinguish organisms that badly need distinguishing. A single, universally agreed scientific name lets researchers anywhere in the world know with certainty which organism is being discussed.

The deepest organizing rule in modern taxonomy is that a named group should be monophyletic: it must contain a common ancestor and every single one of that ancestor's descendants, no exceptions. This standard is what forces revision of classifications that were built for convenience rather than evolutionary accuracy -- the traditional class "Reptilia," as most people learned it, excludes birds, but phylogenetic analysis shows birds are nested within the same lineage as crocodilians (both descend from a shared archosaur ancestor more recently than that ancestor shared with lizards and snakes), which makes the traditional bird-excluding "reptile" grouping non-monophyletic, and modern cladistic classification handles this by either including birds within Reptilia or abandoning "reptile" as a formal taxonomic rank altogether.
