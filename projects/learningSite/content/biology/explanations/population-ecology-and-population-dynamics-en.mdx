Population ecology asks a deceptively simple question -- how does the number of individuals in a population change over time -- and the answer depends entirely on how much the population's own growth is limited by the resources available to it. When resources are effectively unlimited relative to how many individuals are present (a small population in a large, rich, empty habitat; bacteria in the first hours of a fresh culture), each individual reproduces at roughly the same per-capita rate regardless of population size, so the population's absolute growth rate (individuals added per unit time) keeps climbing as the population itself grows -- more individuals, each contributing the same per-capita rate, means a bigger absolute number added. This is exponential growth, and plotted over time it produces the characteristic J-shaped curve: slow at first, then visibly accelerating.

No real environment has infinite resources, though, and this is where carrying capacity enters: the maximum population size a given environment's food, space, water, and other resources can sustain indefinitely. As a growing population approaches that ceiling, individuals increasingly compete with each other for the now-limiting resources, and that competition reduces the per-capita growth rate -- fewer offspring survive to reproduce, or individuals reproduce less often, purely because there is less to go around. This produces logistic growth: growth that starts out looking essentially exponential while the population is small relative to carrying capacity, then visibly decelerates as the population approaches carrying capacity, and levels off to essentially zero net growth once the population reaches it. Plotted over time, this produces the characteristic S-shaped (sigmoid) curve, and the logistic growth equation, N(t) = K / (1 + ((K-N0)/N0)·e^(-rt)), captures exactly this: population size N approaching carrying capacity K asymptotically, with r setting how fast that approach happens.

A few refinements matter for interpreting real populations correctly. First, exponential and logistic growth are not two separate models assigned to different species -- they are two regimes of the same underlying process, and the very same population can look essentially exponential while it's far below carrying capacity and then visibly logistic as it approaches it. Second, carrying capacity is a property of a specific environment at a specific time, not a fixed, unchangeable number stamped on a species -- it shifts if resources, climate, competing species, or the habitat itself change. Third, real populations can genuinely overshoot carrying capacity temporarily, particularly when there's a time lag between resource depletion and the population's growth response (as when a population keeps growing for a generation after food has already started running short), producing an overshoot followed by a decline or oscillation around carrying capacity rather than a perfectly smooth approach to a flat plateau -- a real ecological pattern, not evidence that the basic logistic framework has failed.
