A biological community is simply all the populations of all the species sharing a given habitat, and community ecology asks how those populations affect each other. The organizing question behind every category of interaction is a fitness ledger: does an individual's survival and reproduction go up, down, or stay the same because of the interaction, and does the same hold for the individual on the other side of it?

Competition is the category where both participants' fitness is reduced (or at best unaffected) because they need the same limiting resource. Interspecific competition -- between different species -- can be intense enough that one species locally excludes another from a resource entirely (the competitive exclusion principle: two species cannot indefinitely coexist while relying on the exact same limiting resource in the exact same way). Intraspecific competition -- between individuals of the same species -- is easy to overlook precisely because it isn't visually dramatic, but it is frequently the single strongest force limiting a population's own growth, since no other organisms in the community need resources as similar as members of the same species do (the same food, the same nest sites, the same mates); this within-species crowding effect is exactly what drives the density-dependent growth slowdown captured by the logistic growth model.

Predation is the clearest win/lose relationship: one organism, the predator, kills and consumes another, the prey. Because a predator's own population size depends on how much prey is available, and prey population size depends in turn on how much predation pressure it faces, predator and prey populations very often show linked, cyclical dynamics -- a predator population boom depletes the prey supply, which then causes the predator population to crash from starvation, which lets the prey population recover, restarting the cycle.

Symbiotic relationships -- sustained close association between two different species -- split into three categories based purely on that same fitness ledger. Mutualism benefits both parties (a pollinating insect gains food while the plant gains reproductive success through pollen transfer). Commensalism benefits one party while genuinely leaving the other unaffected (an epiphytic plant growing on a tree's branch gains a growing surface without measurably helping or harming the tree). Parasitism benefits one party at the other's ongoing expense, without necessarily killing it the way predation does (a tapeworm draws nutrients from its host over an extended period). Crucially, these categories are not permanently fixed labels stamped onto a species pair -- the same two species can genuinely shift between categories as resource availability, population density, or environmental context changes, since what actually defines the relationship is the current balance of costs and benefits, not a fixed rule about that species pair.
