Speciation is the process by which one ancestral population splits into two or more populations that can no longer interbreed and produce fertile offspring -- the operational definition biologists use for where one species ends and another begins (the biological species concept, with acknowledged exceptions for organisms that reproduce asexually or hybridize readily). The most common route to speciation is allopatric: some geographic barrier -- a newly uplifted mountain range, a river changing course, a population colonizing an isolated island -- physically prevents two parts of a population from interbreeding. Once separated, each population experiences its own independent history of mutation, genetic drift, and natural selection, accumulating genetic differences that have nothing to do with the barrier itself but everything to do with each population evolving on its own trajectory. Given enough time, these accumulated differences produce reproductive isolation -- the two populations can no longer successfully interbreed even if the original barrier later vanishes and they come back into physical contact, because the genetic and often behavioral divergence has become the actual barrier.

Sympatric speciation, without any geographic separation, is rarer but well documented. In plants especially, polyploidy -- an organism ending up with a doubled or otherwise altered chromosome number, often through an error in cell division -- can instantly create reproductive isolation from the parent population, since offspring with a mismatched chromosome number generally cannot produce viable, fertile hybrids with the original diploid population; a new polyploid lineage can become established as a distinct species within a single generation. Sympatric speciation can also arise through strong disruptive selection, where individuals at either extreme of a trait distribution within one habitat are favored over individuals in the middle, gradually splitting one interbreeding population into two.

Phylogenetics reconstructs the historical pattern of these branching speciation events and represents it as a tree, using independent lines of evidence -- morphological, and increasingly molecular/DNA-sequence data -- to infer which lineages share the most recent common ancestor. The single most important thing to understand about reading a phylogenetic tree correctly is that relatedness is defined entirely by branch-point recency, not by superficial resemblance: two lineages that independently evolved similar-looking traits under similar selective pressures (convergent evolution -- the streamlined body shape shared by sharks, ichthyosaurs, and dolphins despite being fish, extinct reptiles, and mammals respectively) are not closely related just because they look alike, and a tree has no privileged "top" or "most advanced" branch -- every species alive today sits at the tip of a branch exactly as long, in generations since the origin of life, as every other living species's branch.
