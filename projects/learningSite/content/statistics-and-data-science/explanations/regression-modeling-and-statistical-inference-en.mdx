Regression is used for more than describing a dataset already in hand -- it's used to infer how a predictor relates to an outcome and to predict outcomes for new observations that weren't part of the original data. The coefficient of determination, R-squared, is the standard summary of how well a model is doing this: it measures the fraction of the outcome's total variation the model accounts for, from 0 (no better than always guessing the mean) to 1 (the model accounts for all of it).

The trap is treating R-squared as a number to be maximized rather than a diagnostic to be interpreted carefully. Because a model's flexibility can always be increased -- adding more predictors, more interaction terms, higher-order curves -- R-squared on the exact data used to build the model can always be pushed higher, right up to 1 if you add enough flexibility. But climbing R-squared this way often means the model has started fitting the specific noise and quirks of that one dataset rather than a real, underlying pattern that would hold for new data too. This failure mode is called overfitting, and it shows up as an impressively high R-squared on training data paired with much worse performance on data the model hasn't seen -- which is exactly why a model's real value is judged by its performance on new, held-out data, not by how tightly it fits the data it was built from.

A second trap is causal overreach: a regression coefficient describes an observed statistical association, and that association alone cannot distinguish "the predictor causes the outcome" from "some third factor drives both" (confounding) or even reverse causation. Regression is a powerful descriptive and predictive tool, but establishing that a relationship is causal requires a study design -- most reliably, randomization -- built specifically to rule out those alternative explanations, something the regression coefficient by itself can never do.
