A point estimate is honest but incomplete: reporting a sample mean of 52% tells you what the sample actually showed, but says nothing about how much that number might differ from the true population value if a different sample had been drawn. A confidence interval fixes this by reporting a range instead of a single number, constructed with a specific guarantee in mind: if the same sampling procedure were repeated a large number of times, a chosen percentage of the resulting intervals -- conventionally 95% -- would contain the true population value.

That guarantee is about the procedure across many hypothetical repetitions, not about any single interval you've already computed. This is the single most commonly misstated idea in introductory statistics: once a 95% confidence interval has actually been computed from real data, the true population value either falls inside it or it doesn't -- there's no probability left to assign to that already-fixed fact. The 95% describes how often the method succeeds in the long run, across repeated applications with fresh samples, not a probability statement about the specific numbers you're looking at.

The margin of error -- half the width of the interval -- follows a clean relationship: it grows with the population's standard deviation (more inherent variability means less certainty) and shrinks with the square root of the sample size. That square-root dependence has a real practical consequence: because it isn't a linear relationship, doubling your sample size doesn't halve your margin of error -- you'd need to quadruple the sample size to cut the margin of error in half. Precision against sample size is a game of diminishing returns, not one of proportional payoff, which is exactly why very high-precision surveys need dramatically larger samples than moderate-precision ones, not just proportionally larger ones.
