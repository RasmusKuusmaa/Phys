The temptation with more than two groups is to just run a t-test on every possible pair, but that approach has a hidden cost: each individual test carries its own small chance of a false positive, and those chances compound. With four groups there are six possible pairs, and even at a strict 0.05 significance level per test, the true probability that at least one of those six tests falsely flags a difference climbs well above 5% -- exactly the inflated false-positive problem multiple comparisons create.

Analysis of variance sidesteps this by asking one combined question instead of many separate ones: is the variation between the group means larger than the variation you'd expect from ordinary random noise within each group? It does this by directly comparing two sources of variance -- between-group and within-group -- in a single test with one controlled overall error rate, rather than accumulating risk across repeated pairwise comparisons.

But no statistical test, however well-chosen, can rescue a poorly built experiment, which is why experimental design matters just as much as the analysis method. Randomization -- assigning experimental units to treatment groups purely by chance -- is what lets you attribute an observed difference in outcomes to the treatment itself, rather than to whatever pre-existing differences happened to sort themselves unevenly across groups. Replication -- running the comparison across enough independent units -- is what lets random noise actually average out, rather than a handful of unusually high or low values dominating the result. And blocking -- grouping similar experimental units together before randomizing treatment within each group -- removes a known, predictable source of variability (like soil quality varying across a field) from the noise the test has to see through, which is often the difference between detecting a real effect and having it drowned out by variation the design should have controlled for in the first place.
