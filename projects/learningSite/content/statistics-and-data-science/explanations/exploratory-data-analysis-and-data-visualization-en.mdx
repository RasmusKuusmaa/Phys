Summary statistics compress an entire dataset into a handful of numbers, and that compression is exactly where information gets lost. The mean, median, and mode can each answer "what's typical here?" differently, and only the median is robust to outliers -- because it depends on rank order (which value sits in the middle when everything is sorted) rather than the actual magnitude of extreme values, a single wildly large or small data point can drag the mean far from where most of the data actually sits without moving the median at all.

A dataset's shape carries information no single center-measure can hold: whether it's symmetric or skewed toward high or low values, whether it has one dominant peak or several, and whether outliers are present all change what "typical" even means and which further analysis is appropriate. Two datasets can share an identical mean and identical standard deviation while looking completely different once actually plotted -- this is not a rare edge case, it's a routine occurrence, and it's exactly why relying on summary numbers alone is genuinely risky.

This is what makes visualization -- histograms, scatter plots, box plots -- a core analytical tool rather than a final cosmetic step for a report. A histogram immediately reveals skew, multiple modes, or gaps in the data that summary statistics alone would never expose; a scatter plot reveals whether two variables have a linear relationship, a non-linear one, or none at all, information a single correlation coefficient can badly misrepresent (a famous illustration is a nonlinear or clustered relationship that produces the same correlation coefficient as no relationship at all). Exploratory analysis, done properly before any formal modeling, is what catches these situations before they silently produce a wrong or misleading result downstream.
