Water vapor is a gas, and it is entirely invisible -- humid air on a summer day contains plenty of water vapor without any visible cloud at all. A cloud only becomes visible once that vapor actually condenses into liquid water or deposits directly as ice, and two conditions have to be met simultaneously for that to happen: the air has to cool below its dew point (the temperature at which it becomes saturated), and the water needs a microscopic surface to condense onto. That surface -- a condensation nucleus -- is typically a tiny particle of dust, sea salt, pollution, or even bacteria suspended in the air; without one, water vapor resists condensing at all, even well below its nominal dew point, which is part of why cloud seeding (deliberately adding condensation nuclei) can sometimes trigger precipitation from an otherwise stable cloud.

Forming a visible cloud, though, is not the same as producing precipitation. A typical cloud droplet is only a few micrometers to a few tens of micrometers across -- so small and light that air resistance alone is enough to keep it essentially suspended, falling so slowly that it would evaporate in the drier air below the cloud long before covering any real distance. Getting from that suspended droplet to an actual raindrop (roughly a thousand times larger in diameter, and roughly a billion times larger in volume) requires a real growth mechanism, and clouds use one of two, depending on temperature.

In warm clouds -- entirely liquid, with no ice present -- the mechanism is collision-coalescence. Cloud droplets are never perfectly uniform in size; slightly larger droplets fall slightly faster than their smaller neighbors, and as they fall through the cloud they collide with and absorb some of those smaller droplets in their path. This creates a positive feedback: the more a droplet grows, the faster it falls, and the faster it falls, the more smaller droplets it sweeps up, until after enough collisions it's grown large enough to fall as rain rather than remain suspended.

In cold clouds that contain a mix of ice crystals and supercooled liquid droplets (liquid water that remains unfrozen below 0°C, which is common in clouds), a different and often faster mechanism, the Bergeron process, takes over. Ice has a lower saturation vapor pressure than liquid water at the same subfreezing temperature -- meaning the air can be simultaneously slightly under-saturated with respect to the liquid droplets (so they slowly evaporate) while being over-saturated with respect to the ice crystals (so vapor deposits onto them and they grow). The net effect is that water migrates from the liquid droplets to the ice crystals through the vapor phase, growing the ice crystals rapidly at the liquid droplets' expense until they're heavy enough to fall -- reaching the ground as snow if the whole path below stays below freezing, or melting into rain along the way if it doesn't.
