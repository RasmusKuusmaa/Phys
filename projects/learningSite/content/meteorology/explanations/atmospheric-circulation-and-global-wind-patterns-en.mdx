If Earth did not rotate, the simplest possible atmospheric circulation would work exactly as intuition suggests: intense equatorial heating drives air upward, that air flows poleward at altitude where it's coldest, sinks at the poles, and flows back toward the equator along the surface, closing one single, planet-spanning convective loop. Earth's rotation ruins this simple picture. As air moves poleward at altitude, the Coriolis effect deflects its path (to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, left in the Southern), and that deflection grows strong enough that the air can no longer make it all the way to the poles before it's been turned into a nearly west-to-east flow -- the loop simply cannot stay coherent over that full distance.

What forms instead is three narrower, more dynamically stable cells stacked between equator and pole in each hemisphere. The Hadley cell, closest to the equator, is the most direct survivor of the original picture: air rises at the equator, flows poleward until Coriolis deflection and cooling force it to sink, which happens at roughly 30 degrees latitude -- and that descending, already moisture-depleted air is exactly why the world's great subtropical deserts (the Sahara, the Australian outback, the American Southwest) sit at that latitude band on every continent, a direct and predictable consequence of the cell's structure rather than an accident of geography. The polar cell, at the opposite end, is a smaller mirror image driven by the same logic at the cold end of the temperature gradient. Between them, the Ferrel cell is driven less directly by its own convection and more by being dragged along by the Hadley and polar cells on either side of it -- which is why mid-latitude weather (the latitudes most of the world's population actually lives in) is generally more variable and storm-driven than the more steadily patterned tropics or poles.

The jet stream is not a fourth, separate wind system riding on top of this structure -- it is the direct signature of where two of these cells meet. At the boundary between the Ferrel and polar cells especially, the temperature contrast between the cold air on one side and warmer air on the other is sharpest, and that sharp temperature gradient, combined with the same Coriolis deflection driving the cell structure itself, concentrates into a narrow, fast-moving ribbon of west-to-east wind at the tropopause. Because the jet stream's location and strength are governed by where that temperature contrast currently sits, and that contrast shifts with the seasons and with individual weather systems, tracking the jet stream's day-to-day position is one of the most direct ways a forecaster reads where storm systems are likely to travel next.
