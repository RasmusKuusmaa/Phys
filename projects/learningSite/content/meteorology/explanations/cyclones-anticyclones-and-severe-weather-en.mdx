"Cyclone" is a structural term, not a size or intensity label -- it describes any low-pressure system where air spirals inward and upward, and that same basic geometry shows up at wildly different scales driven by wildly different physical mechanisms. The largest and most familiar to most of the world's population is the extratropical cyclone, the everyday mid-latitude storm system that forms directly on a front between two contrasting air masses. Its energy comes from that temperature contrast itself -- the potential energy stored in having cold, dense air right next to warm, light air -- and the cyclone's rotation is what physically organizes the associated cold and warm fronts, dragging them across the map as the whole system moves and evolves.

A tropical cyclone -- called a hurricane in the Atlantic and eastern Pacific, a typhoon in the western Pacific -- looks superficially similar (a spiral of clouds around a low-pressure center) but runs on an entirely different engine. There is no front anywhere near a tropical cyclone; instead, its energy comes from latent heat -- the energy released when water vapor evaporated from warm ocean water condenses into the towering thunderstorms that ring the storm's central eye. Because that engine depends entirely on a continuous supply of warm ocean moisture, tropical cyclones can only form and strengthen over sufficiently warm ocean water, and they weaken rapidly once they move over land or cooler water and that fuel supply is cut off -- exactly the opposite of an extratropical cyclone, which needs a front, not warm water, to sustain itself.

A tornado operates at a completely different scale again -- typically only tens to a few hundred meters across, versus hundreds to thousands of kilometers for the storm systems above. Tornadoes form inside individual supercell thunderstorms, when wind shear (wind speed and direction changing with altitude) sets a portion of the storm's updraft rotating into a mesocyclone; under the right conditions that rotation can stretch, tighten, and intensify -- the same principle as a spinning skater pulling in their arms -- into a narrow, extremely fast vortex that reaches all the way to the ground.

An anticyclone is the mirror-image structure to all of the above: air sinks from aloft and spirals outward from a center of high pressure, rather than rising and spiraling inward. Because descending air compresses and warms as it sinks (the reverse of the cooling that drives cloud formation in a cyclone), an anticyclone actively suppresses cloud formation rather than promoting it, which is why high-pressure systems are characteristically associated with clear skies and calm, settled weather rather than any weaker version of storminess.
