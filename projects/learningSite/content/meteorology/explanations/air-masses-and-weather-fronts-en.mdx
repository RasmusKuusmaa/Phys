An air mass is defined less by where it is now than by where it came from. When a large body of air sits over a relatively uniform surface -- a warm tropical ocean, a frozen polar landmass, a dry continental interior -- for long enough (typically several days to a couple of weeks), it exchanges heat and moisture with that surface until its temperature and humidity roughly equilibrate with it. The resulting air mass is classified by combining its source latitude (arctic, polar, tropical, equatorial) with its source surface (maritime or continental), giving categories like maritime tropical (warm, humid, from a tropical ocean) or continental polar (cold, dry, from a high-latitude landmass). Once it starts moving, an air mass carries these source-region properties largely intact, modifying only gradually at its edges as it passes over new terrain -- which is exactly why knowing an air mass's classification lets a forecaster predict, in broad strokes, the temperature and humidity it will bring to a distant location days before it arrives.

Weather changes happen where two different air masses meet, at a front, and the specific geometry of that meeting determines the weather, not just the temperature contrast between the two masses. A cold front occurs where a mass of cold, dense air actively advances into a region of warmer air; because cold air is denser, it wedges aggressively underneath the warm air, forcing it upward steeply and quickly. That rapid, forced lifting condenses moisture fast, producing a narrow band -- often just tens of kilometers wide -- of intense weather: heavy rain, thunderstorms, sometimes hail, that passes through relatively quickly as the front moves on.

A warm front is the reverse geometry, and it plays out very differently. Here, a warm air mass advances into a region still occupied by retreating cold air, but because warm air is less dense, it cannot wedge underneath -- instead it gradually rides up and over the retreating cold air along a shallow slope that can extend hundreds of kilometers ahead of the surface front position. That slow, gentle ascent produces widespread but comparatively gentle precipitation that can persist steadily for many hours or even a day or more before the front itself arrives.

An occluded front is what happens when a cold front, which typically moves faster than a warm front, catches up to and overtakes one -- the cold air behind lifts the entire warm sector off the ground, squeezing it between two masses of cooler air, and the resulting weather is a complex mix of both parent fronts' characteristics. A stationary front, by contrast, is simply a boundary where neither air mass has enough forward momentum to displace the other, so it stays in roughly the same location -- but because the same lifting mechanisms that drive weather at any front are still active there, a stationary front can produce persistent, sometimes prolonged and heavy, precipitation in one place for days rather than the passing weather a moving front brings.
