A weather forecast is fundamentally a physics calculation, not a search through historical records. Meteorologists first assemble the best available snapshot of the atmosphere's current state -- temperature, pressure, humidity, and wind measured at countless points by satellites, weather balloons, aircraft, and ground stations, interpolated onto a three-dimensional grid covering the globe. That snapshot is then fed as the starting condition into a set of equations describing how fluids move and how heat and moisture transfer, and a supercomputer steps those equations forward in small time increments, projecting the atmosphere's state hour by hour into the future.

Two entirely separate things determine how good the resulting forecast is: how accurately the model's equations and grid resolution capture real atmospheric physics, and how precisely the starting snapshot matched the atmosphere's true state at that instant. The first has improved enormously over decades of meteorological research and now-routine supercomputing power, and continues to improve. The second faces a problem that no amount of additional measurement can ever fully solve, because the atmosphere is what's called a chaotic system.

In a chaotic system, two starting states that differ by an amount too small to ever measure -- literally, a difference below the precision of any real instrument -- will, given enough time, evolve into completely different outcomes; this is the same underlying property popularly known as the butterfly effect. Because measuring the real atmosphere's current state with perfect, zero-error precision is physically impossible (every thermometer, every satellite sensor has some measurement uncertainty, and the atmosphere is never sampled at literally every point in three-dimensional space), the tiny, unavoidable gap between the measured initial state fed into the model and the atmosphere's true initial state will inevitably grow over time, eventually swamping the forecast with error regardless of how good the underlying equations are.

This is why forecast skill degrades gradually and predictably with lead time -- a next-day forecast is reliably accurate, a week-out forecast captures the broad pattern but with real uncertainty, and forecasts genuinely lose most of their skill somewhere around one to two weeks out, a limit meteorologists refer to as the predictability horizon. Crucially, this isn't a temporary technological shortfall waiting to be solved by more satellites or a bigger supercomputer -- chaos theory shows it's a fundamental mathematical property of the atmosphere itself, meaning even a perfect model running on unlimited computing power, fed the most precise measurements achievable, would still see its forecasts diverge from reality past that horizon.
