Climate is what weather does on average, over enough years that day-to-day variation washes out into a stable statistical pattern -- and the standard tool for organizing that pattern into meaningful categories, the Köppen climate classification, makes a deliberate choice that turns out to matter enormously: it classifies by temperature and precipitation seasonality together, not either one alone. A region with a genuine monsoon pattern -- a harsh, near-rainless dry season followed by a torrential wet season -- can have exactly the same annual rainfall total as a region where that same total falls in gentle, evenly distributed amounts all year, but the two places support completely different vegetation, agriculture, and ways of life, because it's the timing of water availability relative to the growing season, not the yearly total, that actually constrains what can survive there. Köppen's categories (and the many refinements built on it since) capture that timing explicitly rather than averaging it away.

Beyond this long-term average pattern, climate also has real, natural variability from one year to the next that has nothing to do with any longer-term trend. The most consequential of these is the El Nino-Southern Oscillation, a coupled ocean-atmosphere system centered on the tropical Pacific that flips, roughly every two to seven years, between two states: an El Nino phase, where surface waters in the central and eastern tropical Pacific warm well above normal, and a La Nina phase, where they cool below normal. Because the tropical Pacific is such a vast expanse of ocean, and because its surface temperature strongly influences where and how atmospheric convection organizes, an ENSO phase shift reliably reorganizes rainfall and temperature patterns across much of the globe -- drought in one region paired with flooding in another, on a semi-predictable multi-year cycle that has been documented for as long as records exist, entirely independent of any human-driven warming trend layered on top of it.

A monsoon is a related idea but a genuinely different mechanism and timescale. It is driven by the simple fact that land heats up and cools down much faster than the ocean does, so during one season the interior of a large landmass (the Indian subcontinent is the textbook case) becomes considerably warmer than the surrounding ocean, drawing in moist ocean air that produces the wet season, while in the opposite season the pattern reverses and dry continental air flows back out over the ocean. Because this reversal is driven by the annual solar heating cycle itself, it repeats every single year on a predictable seasonal schedule, unlike ENSO's irregular multi-year rhythm -- two entirely different phenomena that happen to both fall under the broad heading of "natural climate variability" without being the same thing at all.
