Most classic chemotherapy drugs exploit a simple but blunt targeting principle: cancer cells, almost by definition, divide far more often than most normal cells, and a range of cytotoxic mechanisms can interfere with that division process. Alkylating agents chemically damage DNA directly, cross-linking its strands in ways that block replication; antimetabolites are structurally similar enough to the natural building blocks of DNA and RNA synthesis (nucleotide precursors, folate) that a dividing cell incorporates them by mistake, disrupting synthesis; mitotic inhibitors interfere with the cellular machinery (microtubules) that physically separates chromosomes during division, arresting cells mid-division. All of these mechanisms share the same fundamental limitation: they target the process of rapid division itself, not anything unique to a cancer cell's biology, which is why the body's own normal rapidly-dividing tissues -- bone marrow (producing blood cells), the gastrointestinal lining, and hair follicles -- bear the brunt of the same damage, producing chemotherapy's signature side effects of immunosuppression, nausea/diarrhea, and hair loss.

Targeted therapy represents a conceptually different strategy: rather than attacking proliferation rate broadly, it exploits a specific molecular abnormality known to drive a particular cancer's growth -- a mutated, constitutively active growth-factor receptor, for instance, or an abnormal fusion protein present only in the malignant cells. A drug designed against that specific target can, in principle, leave normal cells (which lack the abnormality) largely untouched while still killing the cancer cells that depend on it, offering better precision than the blunt division-targeting of classic chemotherapy -- though in practice, few targets are perfectly cancer-exclusive, so some off-target effects usually remain.

Both strategies face the same evolutionary problem once treatment begins. A tumor is not a single uniform cell type but a genetically heterogeneous population, with different cells carrying slightly different mutations by the time treatment starts (and acquiring new ones as they continue dividing under treatment). Any therapy that kills the majority of drug-sensitive cells but doesn't achieve complete eradication inevitably applies a selective pressure: whichever subclone happens to carry (or later acquires) a mutation that confers resistance to that specific drug survives, proliferates in the newly-vacated space, and can repopulate the tumor as a now drug-resistant lineage. This is the same natural-selection logic that produces antimicrobial resistance in bacteria, applied within a single patient's own tumor cell population rather than across a microbial species -- which is why cancer treatment protocols often combine multiple drugs with different mechanisms simultaneously, making it far less likely that any single surviving subclone happens to carry resistance to all of them at once.
