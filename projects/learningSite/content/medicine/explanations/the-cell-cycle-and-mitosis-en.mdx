A dividing cell spends most of its time not dividing at all. Interphase, the long stretch between one division and the next, is where the actual preparatory work happens: during G1 the cell grows and carries out its normal metabolic activities; during S-phase it replicates its entire genome, producing two identical copies (sister chromatids) of every chromosome, still joined together at a region called the centromere; and during G2 it continues growing and assembles the proteins mitosis itself will need, including the components of the spindle apparatus. Only after all of that is mitosis, the comparatively brief division phase, ready to begin.

Mitosis proceeds through a fixed, visually recognizable sequence. In prophase, the replicated chromosomes condense from loose chromatin into compact, visible structures, and the nuclear envelope breaks down. In metaphase, the spindle apparatus -- built from microtubules radiating from two opposite poles of the cell -- has captured every chromosome and aligned them all along the cell's equator, each chromosome's two sister chromatids attached to spindle fibers from opposite poles. In anaphase, the connection between sister chromatids breaks, and the spindle fibers pull the now-separated chromatids toward opposite poles of the cell. In telophase, a new nuclear envelope re-forms around each separated set of chromosomes, producing two genetically identical nuclei; cytokinesis then physically pinches the cytoplasm in two, completing the division into two separate daughter cells.

None of this happens on trust. At several points in the cycle -- most critically at the G1/S transition, the G2/M transition, and again during metaphase itself -- dedicated checkpoint proteins verify that the previous step actually completed correctly before allowing the cycle to proceed. The G1 checkpoint checks for DNA damage before committing to replicate it; the G2 checkpoint checks that replication finished completely and accurately before committing to mitosis; and the spindle-assembly checkpoint, active during metaphase, specifically verifies that every single chromosome is correctly attached to spindle fibers from both poles before allowing anaphase to begin, since even one incorrectly attached chromosome would be pulled to the wrong daughter cell, producing an abnormal chromosome number.

This checkpoint system is not a minor bookkeeping detail -- it is what makes mitosis a reliable, faithful copying process rather than a rough approximation. When a checkpoint itself is damaged or bypassed (often through mutation of the genes that encode checkpoint proteins), a cell with unrepaired DNA damage or unevenly distributed chromosomes can proceed through division anyway, and its damaged or abnormal state gets copied and propagated into its descendants rather than caught and stopped. This checkpoint failure, more than the mechanics of division itself, is a central mechanism behind how cancer actually develops at the cellular level, which is exactly why so much cancer biology research targets the checkpoint proteins rather than only the spindle apparatus they regulate.
