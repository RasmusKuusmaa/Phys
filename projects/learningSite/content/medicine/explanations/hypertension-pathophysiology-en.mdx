Blood pressure is not a single physiological quantity with one lever controlling it -- it is the product of two genuinely independent factors, cardiac output (the volume of blood the heart pumps per minute) and total peripheral resistance (how strongly the systemic blood vessels resist that flow). Chronically elevating either factor alone raises blood pressure, which is exactly why hypertension has no single unifying mechanism across all patients: some contributing pathways act primarily on the heart's output, others act primarily on vessel tone, and most real cases involve both to some degree.

The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, already introduced in this subject's adrenal-gland-physiology concept, sits at the intersection of both factors. Angiotensin II, the system's central active hormone, is itself a potent vasoconstrictor, directly narrowing blood vessels and raising peripheral resistance. Aldosterone, released from the adrenal cortex under angiotensin II's influence, acts on the kidneys to retain sodium (and, with it, water), expanding blood volume and thereby raising cardiac output. A single hormonal system, activated together, therefore pushes on both halves of the blood-pressure equation simultaneously, which is part of why RAAS is such a central target in understanding hypertension's mechanisms.

The large majority of hypertension cases fall into the category called primary (or essential) hypertension, and the defining feature of this category is precisely that no single mechanism explains it. Genetic variation affecting kidney sodium handling, vascular reactivity, and RAAS regulation, combined with lifestyle factors accumulating their effects over years, gradually shifts a person's blood-pressure set point upward through some combination of modestly elevated cardiac output and modestly elevated peripheral resistance -- no one lever is broken, but the whole system has settled at a higher equilibrium. This is exactly why primary hypertension cannot typically be cured by removing one specific problem; there usually isn't one specific problem to remove.

A smaller fraction of cases, secondary hypertension, break this pattern by having exactly one identifiable, often mechanistically clean cause: a tumor of the adrenal cortex autonomously overproducing aldosterone regardless of what the rest of the RAAS is signaling, a narrowed renal artery that fools the kidney into releasing excess renin as if blood flow (and, by extension, systemic blood pressure) were too low, or a handful of other similarly discrete mechanisms. Recognizing which category a given case of hypertension falls into is mechanistically important precisely because it determines whether there is a single correctable lesion to look for at all, or whether the condition instead reflects the cumulative, multi-factorial pattern that defines the primary form.
