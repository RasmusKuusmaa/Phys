Sleep researchers distinguish two fundamentally different reasons a person might report "not sleeping at the right time," and mistaking one for the other leads to entirely wrong mechanistic conclusions. The first is primary insomnia: a state now understood as physiological hyperarousal rather than insufficient sleepiness. People with primary insomnia typically show measurably elevated cortisol, an elevated metabolic rate, and heightened cortical (brain) activity specifically during the hours when these should be declining in preparation for sleep -- their nervous system is, in a real physiological sense, more "switched on" at bedtime than a good sleeper's, not merely less tired. This reframes insomnia away from "trying too hard to fall asleep" or "not being sleepy enough" and toward a genuine arousal-system dysregulation operating on top of an otherwise normal circadian clock.

The second is a circadian rhythm disorder, where the arousal system is normal but the internal clock itself runs on a different schedule than desired. Delayed sleep phase disorder is the clearest example: the person's circadian clock is genuinely phase-delayed relative to a typical schedule, not simply a preference or bad habit about bedtime. The clean way to demonstrate this mechanistically is a free-running experiment -- placing someone in an environment stripped of all external time cues (no clocks, no natural light exposure, no scheduled meals) for an extended period. Under these conditions, a person's sleep-wake cycle reveals their body's own intrinsic circadian period, uncontaminated by any external schedule pressure. Someone with delayed sleep phase disorder in this setting would still consistently fall asleep and wake up substantially later, cycle after cycle, than someone with a typical circadian phase -- demonstrating that the shift lives in the clock mechanism itself (likely involving core circadian genes and the timing of the suprachiasmatic nucleus's output), not in willpower or a correctable habit.

These two categories point to entirely different treatment logic, which is exactly why the mechanistic distinction matters beyond being an academic classification exercise: an approach that addresses hyperarousal (calming an overactive stress-response system) does little for a genuinely shifted clock, and an approach that shifts circadian timing (carefully timed light exposure, for instance) does little for a hyperarousal-driven insomnia where the clock was never the problem in the first place. Both produce the same surface complaint -- trouble sleeping at the desired time -- but represent genuinely different dysregulation at the mechanistic level.
