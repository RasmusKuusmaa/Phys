A human genome contains roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes, and with only a handful of specific exceptions, every nucleated cell in the body carries a complete copy of all of them, whether that cell is a neuron, a liver cell, or a skin cell. What makes those cell types look and behave so differently isn't which genes they contain -- it's which genes they actually express, and how much. Gene expression regulation is the layered machinery that controls this: which genes are transcribed, in which cells, at what level, and in response to what signals.

In bacteria, genes are commonly regulated as an operon: several genes involved in one shared task -- say, breaking down a particular sugar -- sit next to each other on the chromosome under the control of one shared regulatory region, so a single regulatory event switches the whole functional cluster on or off together, typically in direct response to whether that sugar is present in the environment. Eukaryotic cells like human cells generally regulate genes individually rather than in operons, mostly through transcription factors: proteins that recognize and bind specific short DNA sequences near a gene's start and either recruit RNA polymerase to begin transcription or physically block it from doing so. Because a given DNA sequence motif can appear near many different genes, one transcription factor can coordinate the expression of a whole functional program of genes at once, and because a single gene's regulatory region often has binding sites for several different transcription factors, whether that gene actually gets transcribed can depend on which combination of factors happens to be present and active in that particular cell.

Beyond this direct transcription-factor control, cells layer on epigenetic regulation: chemical modifications that change how physically accessible a stretch of DNA is to the transcription machinery, without altering the DNA sequence itself at all. Adding a methyl group directly to DNA (DNA methylation) typically compacts that region and suppresses transcription; chemically modifying the histone proteins DNA is spooled around can either loosen chromatin to make a gene more accessible or tighten it to make the gene less accessible. Because these modifications are chemical marks layered on top of the genome rather than changes to the genome's sequence, they can, in principle, be copied to daughter cells during cell division, letting a cell 'remember' its regulatory state as it divides, and in some documented cases even be inherited across generations -- a genuinely different mechanism of inheritance from the DNA sequence itself, and the reason this field is called epigenetics.
