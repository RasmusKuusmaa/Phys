A mutation, in the most general sense, is any change to a DNA sequence -- the term itself carries no built-in judgment about whether that change is harmful, neutral, or beneficial. Mutations are usually grouped by scale. Point mutations affect a single DNA base: a substitution swaps one base for another, while an insertion or deletion adds or removes one (or a small number of) bases. Chromosomal mutations operate at a much larger scale, duplicating, deleting, inverting, or translocating entire stretches of a chromosome that can span many genes at once -- a difference in scope that matters enormously for how many genes, and therefore how much of the organism's development, a single mutational event can affect.

For a point mutation landing inside a protein-coding sequence, its consequence depends entirely on how it changes the genetic code read by the ribosome. The genetic code is redundant -- 61 codons specify only 20 amino acids, so several different codons can code for the same amino acid. A silent mutation changes a codon into a different codon that happens to specify the identical amino acid, leaving the resulting protein completely unchanged. A missense mutation changes the codon to specify a different amino acid, and its severity ranges from negligible (if the new amino acid has similar chemical properties to the original) to protein-destroying (if it disrupts a critical structural or functional region). A nonsense mutation changes a codon into one of the three stop codons, prematurely terminating translation and typically producing a truncated, non-functional protein.

The overwhelming majority of mutations arise not from any external mutagen but from unavoidable, everyday cellular processes: DNA polymerase occasionally mispairs a base during replication, and DNA is constantly exposed to chemical damage from normal cellular metabolism. Cells counter this with extensive proofreading during replication and multiple dedicated DNA repair pathways that together catch the vast majority of these errors before they become permanent. The small fraction that escape repair and get passed on are not simply a source of disease -- they are the fundamental source of all heritable genetic variation in every species, the raw material natural selection has to act on. Without a continuous supply of new mutations, populations would eventually run out of genetic variation entirely, and evolution as a process would have nothing left to select among.
