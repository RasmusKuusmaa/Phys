A neuron's entire shape reflects its one job: receive signals, integrate them, and reliably pass the result on, sometimes over a remarkable distance for a single cell. Dendrites branching off the cell body are the receiving end, gathering input from many other neurons; the soma sums that input; and if the sum crosses a threshold, a single output travels down the axon toward the next cell.

That output is the action potential, and its mechanism depends entirely on ion channels embedded in the membrane. At rest the membrane sits at roughly -70 mV, held there mainly by the membrane's high resting permeability to potassium and the sodium-potassium pump actively maintaining a concentration gradient (more sodium outside, more potassium inside). When depolarization crosses threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels open first, and the resulting sodium influx drives the membrane rapidly positive; voltage-gated potassium channels then open with a slight delay, letting potassium flow out and driving the membrane back down, briefly undershooting rest before channels reset.

The crucial property this mechanism gives an axon is non-decremental conduction: because each stretch of membrane has its own full complement of voltage-gated channels, it doesn't passively relay a weakening signal the way a wire would -- it regenerates the spike from scratch, at full amplitude, using local ion flow as its own trigger. A myelinated axon exploits this by insulating long stretches and only exposing membrane (with its channel machinery) at the nodes of Ranvier, so the spike appears to leap from node to node -- saltatory conduction -- which is both faster and more energy-efficient than continuously regenerating the spike at every point along an unmyelinated axon.
