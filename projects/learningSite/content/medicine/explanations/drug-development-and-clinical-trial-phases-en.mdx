Getting a new drug from a promising laboratory compound to something a doctor can prescribe is a long, deliberately staged filter, and the staging exists for an economic as much as a scientific reason: each stage is dramatically more expensive and time-consuming than the one before it, so the process is designed to eliminate failing candidates as early and cheaply as possible rather than discovering a fatal flaw only after a massive, expensive trial is underway.

The earliest stage, preclinical research, happens entirely outside human subjects. In vitro studies (in cells or isolated biological systems) test whether a compound has the intended biological activity, and in vivo animal studies extend that to a whole living organism, screening for obvious toxicity and getting a first look at how the compound is absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and excreted. The overwhelming majority of candidate compounds never make it past this stage.

Compounds that do proceed enter human clinical trials, which are conventionally organized into four phases with distinct, non-overlapping goals. Phase I is about safety first: a small group, usually tens of healthy volunteers, receives the drug at carefully escalating doses to establish a safe dose range and to see, for the first time in a human body, how the drug behaves pharmacokinetically -- it is not yet testing whether the drug treats anything. Phase II recruits a somewhat larger group of actual patients who have the condition the drug is meant to treat, asking the first real efficacy question and further refining the dose. Phase III is the large-scale trial most people picture when they hear "clinical trial" -- often hundreds to thousands of patients across many sites, comparing the new drug against an existing standard treatment or a placebo, sized specifically to detect a real treatment effect with statistical confidence and to catch side effects too uncommon to show up in the much smaller Phase I or II groups. A regulatory agency's approval decision rests heavily on Phase III results.

Even a successful Phase III trial can't catch everything, though: some adverse effects only occur in one patient in tens of thousands, or only after years of continuous use, frequencies and timescales no pre-approval trial (however large) is realistically powered to detect. That's exactly the gap Phase IV, post-market surveillance, is designed to fill -- ongoing monitoring after approval, across the much larger and more diverse population that only widespread real-world use provides, which is how some rare but serious side effects come to light only after a drug has been on the market for some time.
