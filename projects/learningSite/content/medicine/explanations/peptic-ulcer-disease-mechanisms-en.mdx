The stomach faces a genuine engineering problem: it must produce acid strong enough to begin digesting food and killing ingested pathogens, while keeping that same acid from digesting its own tissue. The solution is a continuously maintained protective barrier -- a layer of mucus combined with bicarbonate secreted by the epithelial cells themselves, which together buffer the immediate surface of the stomach lining to a much less acidic pH than the stomach's interior, keeping the acid and the enzyme pepsin from ever directly contacting living epithelial cells. A peptic ulcer is what happens when this barrier is breached faster than the epithelium can repair itself, letting the stomach's own digestive contents erode into deeper tissue.

Helicobacter pylori is the single most common identifiable cause. This bacterium has evolved specifically to survive within the stomach's harsh acidic environment by colonizing the protective mucus layer itself, where it directly damages the epithelial cells beneath it and triggers a chronic local inflammatory response. That chronic inflammation further degrades the mucosal defense over time, and the combination of direct bacterial damage and self-perpetuating inflammation is what allows a persistent H. pylori infection to eventually produce an ulcer.

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) cause ulcers through an entirely different, purely pharmacological route, with no infection involved at all. NSAIDs work by inhibiting cyclooxygenase enzymes, which is exactly how they reduce pain and inflammation elsewhere in the body -- but those same cyclooxygenase enzymes are also responsible for producing the prostaglandins that normally stimulate the stomach's mucus and bicarbonate secretion. Blocking that pathway systemically removes one of the stomach's key defensive signals, weakening the mucosal barrier even though nothing about the stomach's acid production has changed.

What unites these two very different mechanisms is where they act: both disrupt the defense side of the balance between acid and mucosal protection, not the acid-production side. This is the key reframe peptic ulcer disease requires -- the stomach's acid output in most cases of ulcer disease is entirely unremarkable; what has failed is the system that normally protects the stomach lining from acid it produces every single day without incident.
