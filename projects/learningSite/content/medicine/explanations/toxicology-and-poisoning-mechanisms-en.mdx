Toxicology's central insight is that a poison's danger comes from a specific mechanism, not a generic "toxic" property, and understanding that mechanism is what makes rational treatment possible at all. Carbon monoxide is a clear illustration: it is not chemically corrosive or directly destructive to tissue. Its danger comes entirely from its extraordinarily high affinity for hemoglobin -- far higher than oxygen's own affinity -- so it occupies the exact binding site oxygen needs, and blood that is fully saturated with carbon monoxide simply cannot carry oxygen, even though every other part of the oxygen-delivery system (heart, lungs, blood vessels) may be working normally.

Cyanide produces a superficially similar outcome -- tissue starved of usable oxygen -- through the opposite mechanism. Cyanide does not touch hemoglobin; oxygen is bound, carried, and delivered to tissue entirely normally. Instead, cyanide binds and blocks cytochrome c oxidase, the final enzyme in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, which brings cellular respiration to a halt at the very last step, right where oxygen would otherwise be consumed. The tissue has oxygen sitting right there and simply cannot use it. Because the actual defect is opposite (delivery failure vs. utilization failure), the antidotes are necessarily different: treating carbon monoxide poisoning centers on giving as much supplemental oxygen as possible to outcompete carbon monoxide for hemoglobin's binding sites, while cyanide antidotes work by providing an alternative binding target that pulls cyanide away from cytochrome c oxidase, restoring the cell's ability to actually use the oxygen it already has.

A heavy metal like lead illustrates a third distinct mechanism entirely: rather than disrupting oxygen transport or use, lead disrupts enzymes that require binding a specific metal ion (often zinc) to function, by displacing that ion and occupying the binding site itself, disabling the enzyme regardless of how much oxygen is present or being used. This is the general pattern across environmental toxicology -- a corrosive acid destroys tissue on direct contact, a neurotoxin disrupts a specific receptor or ion channel, a hepatotoxin damages liver cells through a specific metabolic pathway -- and in every case, the antidote or treatment that actually works is the one built around that specific mechanism, not a generic response applicable to "toxins" as an undifferentiated category.
