The monoamine hypothesis of depression -- the idea that low serotonin, norepinephrine, or dopamine directly causes depressive symptoms -- is the most widely known model of mood disorders, largely because it maps neatly onto how antidepressants were first understood to work: selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors block the reabsorption of serotonin at the synapse, leaving more of it available, and this fit the deficiency story perfectly when SSRIs were introduced. But two pieces of evidence have forced a more nuanced picture. First, the timing mismatch: SSRIs raise synaptic serotonin within hours of the first dose, yet clinical improvement in depressive symptoms typically takes two to six weeks to appear -- if depression were simply a matter of insufficient serotonin, correcting that deficiency should produce improvement on a similar timescale, not weeks later. Second, experiments that acutely deplete monoamines (via dietary manipulation, for instance) in healthy volunteers do not reliably produce depression, which is hard to reconcile with a model where the deficiency itself is the disorder.

What fills the gap between the acute neurotransmitter change and the delayed clinical effect appears to be a cascade of slower, downstream adaptations. Chronic antidepressant exposure changes the sensitivity and density of postsynaptic receptors over a period of weeks, and separately promotes neuroplasticity -- new synaptic connections and dendritic growth -- partly through increased expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a protein that supports neuron survival and growth. Depression itself is associated with reduced hippocampal volume and altered function in circuits connecting the prefrontal cortex (involved in emotional regulation), the amygdala (involved in threat/negative-emotion processing), and the hippocampus (involved in memory and stress regulation), and effective treatment appears to at least partially reverse some of these circuit-level changes over the same multi-week timescale that clinical improvement takes to emerge.

Bipolar disorder shares some of this circuitry but is mechanistically its own category, not simply "worse depression." Its defining feature is instability across two opposite mood states -- mania and depression -- rather than a fixed depressive baseline, and the circuits involved in switching between these states, and in mood stabilization more generally, only partially overlap with those implicated in unipolar depression. Mood stabilizer medications used for bipolar disorder work through largely different mechanisms than typical antidepressants, which is itself evidence that the two conditions are not the same disorder at different severities.

It's worth being honest about what remains unresolved: no single circuit or neurotransmitter model currently explains the full clinical picture of either condition, which is exactly why mood disorders remain such an active area of ongoing research rather than a mechanistically closed book.
