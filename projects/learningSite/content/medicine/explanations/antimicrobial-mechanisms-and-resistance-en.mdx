Every antibiotic mechanism ultimately comes down to finding a molecular difference between a bacterial cell and a human cell, and then exploiting it precisely. Beta-lactam antibiotics -- penicillins, cephalosporins, and their relatives -- target penicillin-binding proteins, the enzymes bacteria use to cross-link the peptidoglycan mesh of their cell wall into a rigid structure. Block those enzymes, and a growing or dividing bacterium can no longer properly reinforce its wall; the cell's own internal osmotic pressure, which the wall would normally contain, ruptures it instead. Human cells have no peptidoglycan cell wall and no equivalent structure, so this mechanism simply has nothing to act on in a human cell, which is why beta-lactams are among the safest, most selective antibiotic classes available. Other drug classes exploit a different, more subtle structural gap: bacterial ribosomes (built from different-sized subunits than eukaryotic ribosomes) are similar enough to human ribosomes to share the same basic function, but different enough in molecular detail that a drug like tetracycline or a macrolide can bind tightly to the bacterial version and block its function while barely touching the human version.

Resistance to any of these mechanisms tends to converge on the same short list of strategies, because there are only so many ways to defeat a specific molecular attack. Enzymatic destruction is the most direct: beta-lactamase enzymes, for instance, cleave open the four-membered ring structure at the core of every beta-lactam antibiotic, chemically disabling the drug before it ever reaches its target. Target modification changes the shape of the molecule the drug is supposed to bind, just enough that the drug no longer fits well while the bacterium's own use of that target (for wall-building or protein synthesis) still works fine. Efflux pumps are membrane proteins that actively export the drug back out of the cell as fast as it diffuses in, keeping the internal drug concentration below the level needed to have an effect. Reduced uptake works from the other direction, changing membrane porins or transport proteins so less of the drug ever gets inside the cell in the first place. A single resistant bacterium might combine several of these strategies simultaneously, which is part of why some infections become resistant to multiple antibiotic classes at once.

What makes antibiotic resistance spread so much faster than an ordinary evolved trait is the vehicle it usually travels in. A resistance gene acquired by ordinary chromosomal mutation can only pass to a bacterium's own direct offspring when it divides -- vertical inheritance, the same as any other species. But resistance genes frequently sit on plasmids instead, and plasmids can move sideways: through conjugation, one bacterium extends a pilus to another, forms a direct physical connection, and transfers a copy of the plasmid across -- and this transfer isn't limited to bacteria of the same species. A resistance gene that evolves in one species can, via a single successful conjugation event, jump directly into an entirely unrelated species that has never been exposed to the antibiotic in question, and from there spread through that new population by both vertical inheritance and further horizontal transfer. This horizontal-transfer pathway, layered on top of ordinary mutation and selection, is the central reason public health authorities treat antibiotic resistance as a fast-moving, urgent threat rather than a slow background process.
