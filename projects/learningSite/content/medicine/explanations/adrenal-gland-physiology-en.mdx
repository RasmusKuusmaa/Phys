Despite being called one gland, the adrenal gland is really two organs of completely different embryonic origin fused into one structure. The outer cortex develops from the same tissue that gives rise to the gonads and produces steroid hormones by the classic endocrine playbook: hypothalamic CRH stimulates pituitary ACTH, which stimulates the cortex to release cortisol, and rising cortisol levels feed back to suppress both CRH and ACTH -- the same hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis architecture that governs the thyroid axis, just with a different hormone at the end of the chain.

Cortisol's popular label as "the stress hormone" undersells what it actually does. Beyond its role in the stress response, cortisol runs on a baseline daily rhythm even without any acute stressor, and its core physiological job is to help the body sustain demand over a period of hours to days: it raises blood glucose by promoting glucose production and reducing glucose uptake by non-essential tissues, suppresses immune and inflammatory activity, and mobilizes energy stores. This is a fundamentally different kind of response from a momentary alarm reaction -- it is closer to a sustained resource-reallocation program than a single triggered event.

A separate zone of the adrenal cortex produces aldosterone, and here the control system genuinely diverges from the ACTH-driven pattern governing cortisol: aldosterone is regulated mainly by the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, which senses blood pressure and sodium levels (largely via the kidneys) rather than responding to pituitary signaling. Aldosterone's job is to act on the kidneys to retain sodium and excrete potassium, which in turn affects blood volume and blood pressure -- a completely different physiological thread from cortisol's glucose/immune/energy role, run by a completely different feedback loop, despite both hormones coming from the same cortical tissue.

The adrenal medulla, at the center of the gland, is a different kind of organ entirely: developmentally, it is essentially displaced sympathetic nervous tissue, and functionally it behaves that way too. Rather than waiting for a slow hormonal feedback loop, the medulla releases epinephrine and norepinephrine directly into the bloodstream the instant it receives a signal from the sympathetic nervous system, acting within seconds rather than the minutes-to-hours typical of the cortical hormones. This makes the adrenal medulla less a gland in the classical feedback-regulated sense and more a hormonal amplifier that extends the nervous system's own fight-or-flight signal to every tissue in the body that can respond to circulating epinephrine, not just the ones directly wired to a sympathetic nerve.
