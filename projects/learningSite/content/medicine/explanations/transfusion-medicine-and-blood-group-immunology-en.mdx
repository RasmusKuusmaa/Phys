A blood transfusion is, immunologically, nothing more than introducing a large quantity of another person's cell-surface antigens directly into someone's circulation, and whether that provokes a reaction depends entirely on whether the recipient's immune system already has, or can rapidly develop, antibodies against those specific antigens. The ABO system's defining oddity is that this antibody formation happens without any exposure at all: from early infancy, a person spontaneously develops antibodies against whichever ABO antigens their own red cells lack, likely as a cross-reaction to similar antigenic structures on common gut bacteria. This means a type O person (no A or B antigen) carries both anti-A and anti-B antibodies from a young age, ready to attack incompatible red cells on first contact -- which is exactly what happens if ABO-incompatible blood is transfused: the pre-formed antibodies bind the donor cells' surface antigens immediately, activating complement and causing acute intravascular hemolysis within minutes.

The Rh system, and specifically the clinically dominant D antigen, works by the more conventional immunological rule: antibody formation requires actual prior exposure. A person who is Rh-negative (lacking the D antigen) does not carry anti-D antibodies from birth; they only develop them after their immune system has actually encountered D-positive red cells, whether from a mismatched transfusion or from fetal-maternal blood mixing during pregnancy or delivery. This is why a first Rh-mismatched transfusion or a first pregnancy with an Rh-positive fetus usually passes without an acute reaction -- the antibody response takes time to develop after that first exposure -- but leaves the person sensitized, with memory B cells ready to mount a much faster and stronger antibody response on a second exposure.

This sensitization mechanism is precisely why hemolytic disease of the newborn is a second-pregnancy problem, not a first-pregnancy one: an Rh-negative mother's first Rh-positive pregnancy typically sensitizes her during delivery, when fetal blood cells can enter her circulation, but does not put that first baby at meaningful risk since her anti-D antibody response hasn't developed yet. A subsequent Rh-positive pregnancy is the one at risk, since her already-sensitized immune system can now mount a rapid antibody response that crosses the placenta and attacks the fetus's red cells -- which is exactly why Rh immunoglobulin is given prophylactically to Rh-negative mothers, to neutralize any fetal Rh-positive cells before her immune system has a chance to form its own memory response against them.
