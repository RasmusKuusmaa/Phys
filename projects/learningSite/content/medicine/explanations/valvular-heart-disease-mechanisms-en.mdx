The heart's four valves -- tricuspid, pulmonary, mitral, and aortic -- each exist to do one simple mechanical job: let blood flow forward when the chamber behind them contracts, and prevent it from flowing backward once it stops. Valvular disease is what happens when a valve can no longer do one or the other of these jobs properly, and the two ways a valve can fail produce genuinely opposite mechanical consequences for the heart.

Stenosis is a valve that fails to open fully, creating an obstruction to forward flow. The chamber immediately upstream of a stenotic valve has to generate substantially more pressure than normal to force blood through the narrowed opening, a burden called pressure overload. Cardiac muscle responds to sustained pressure overload the same way skeletal muscle responds to sustained resistance training: it thickens. This hypertrophic response initially helps the chamber generate the higher pressure needed, but a thickened, stiffer ventricular wall also becomes harder to fill properly (contributing to diastolic dysfunction, already covered in this subject's heart-failure-mechanisms concept), and the adaptation eventually reaches a limit beyond which the chamber can no longer compensate.

Regurgitation (also called insufficiency) is the mechanically opposite failure: a valve that fails to close fully, allowing blood to leak backward once the chamber beyond it has already contracted. This creates volume overload rather than pressure overload -- the upstream chamber has to handle both its normal incoming blood and the extra blood leaking back through the incompetent valve on each cycle. A chamber facing volume overload responds differently than one facing pressure overload: rather than thickening its walls, it enlarges its internal chamber size (dilation), stretching to accommodate the larger total volume it now has to manage each cycle.

Because the specific location of a valve determines exactly which chamber bears this burden, the clinical picture of valvular disease varies enormously depending on which of the four valves is affected and which of the two failure modes is present -- aortic stenosis burdens the left ventricle with pressure overload, mitral regurgitation burdens the left atrium (and eventually the left ventricle) with volume overload, and so on for each combination -- but the underlying mechanical logic (pressure overload driving hypertrophy, volume overload driving dilation) is the same generalizable principle across all four valves.
