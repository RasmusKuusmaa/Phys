Innate immunity, covered elsewhere, reacts to broad patterns shared by many pathogens; adaptive immunity solves a different problem -- recognizing one specific pathogen precisely, out of the enormous universe of molecular shapes a pathogen could present. It does this through two families of lymphocytes, B cells and T cells, each of which carries a randomly generated receptor on its surface. During development, before any lymphocyte has ever encountered a real pathogen, the genes encoding these receptors get shuffled and recombined in essentially random combinations, producing a population of lymphocytes so diverse that collectively, across millions of different receptor variants, there's a reasonable chance a handful of cells exist whose receptor happens to fit almost any antigen a real pathogen might eventually present -- a solution arrived at through sheer combinatorial diversity generated in advance, not through any process that anticipates specific threats.

Getting from "a matching lymphocyte exists somewhere in the body" to "an actual immune response is happening" requires activation, and for T cells that starts with antigen presentation. Dendritic cells and other antigen-presenting cells capture fragments of a pathogen at the site of infection, then travel to a nearby lymph node and display those fragments on their surface using MHC molecules, essentially holding up a piece of the pathogen for passing T cells to inspect. A T cell patrolling that lymph node whose receptor happens to bind that particular presented fragment, combined with a second confirmatory signal from the antigen-presenting cell (a safeguard against T cells accidentally activating against harmless fragments), triggers that T cell's activation.

Activation is only the beginning -- what actually produces a meaningful immune response is clonal selection. The activated cell divides rapidly and repeatedly, producing a large clone of genetically identical daughter cells, all carrying that same antigen-specific receptor. Some of these become effector cells that address the current infection: helper T cells release signaling molecules that coordinate and amplify the rest of the response, cytotoxic T cells directly kill host cells already infected by the pathogen, and helper T cells also activate matching B cells to differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells. Other cells from that same clonal expansion become memory cells instead -- they don't participate in fighting the current infection, but persist in the body for years afterward, essentially a standing reserve of cells already primed for that exact antigen.

This explains both halves of adaptive immunity's characteristic timing. The first time a given antigen appears, the whole system has to start from an extremely rare starting population -- perhaps only a handful of naive lymphocytes in the entire body happen to have a matching receptor -- and clonal expansion from that tiny starting point to an effective fighting force takes several days, which is why a first infection can cause real illness before adaptive immunity fully engages. The second time the same antigen appears, that slow search-and-expand process simply isn't necessary: a much larger population of memory cells, already specific to that exact antigen, is standing by and can differentiate into effector cells almost immediately, producing the fast, large secondary response that is the entire mechanistic basis of both natural immunity after recovering from an infection and vaccine-induced protection.
