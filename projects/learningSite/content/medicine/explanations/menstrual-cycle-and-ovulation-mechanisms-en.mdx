It helps to think of the menstrual cycle as two cycles running together rather than one: an ovarian cycle that produces hormones, and a uterine cycle that responds to them. The ovarian cycle starts with the follicular phase, during which follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) drives a cohort of ovarian follicles to begin growing; as they grow, they produce increasing amounts of estrogen. Normally, one follicle in the cohort out-competes the others to become the single dominant follicle that will go on to ovulate, while the rest degenerate.

The key trigger for ovulation is a genuine quirk of feedback biology. For most of the cycle, and for most hormonal axes throughout the body, rising hormone levels suppress the signals that produced them -- classic negative feedback. Estrogen does exactly this for most of the follicular phase. But once the dominant follicle's estrogen output climbs high enough and stays there for roughly two days, the hypothalamus and pituitary respond in the opposite way: that sustained high estrogen briefly flips the feedback positive, producing a sharp spike in luteinizing hormone (LH) -- the LH surge. This surge is the proximate trigger for ovulation, but ovulation itself -- the follicle rupturing and releasing the mature egg -- doesn't happen instantly; it follows the start of the surge by about 36 hours, the time needed for the egg to complete its own final maturation step and for the follicle wall to actually rupture.

After ovulation, the ruptured follicle transforms into a temporary endocrine structure called the corpus luteum, which now secretes progesterone (along with continuing estrogen) to maintain a receptive uterine lining -- this is the luteal phase, and it is far more fixed in length (about 14 days) than the follicular phase, which is why cycle-length variability between people and cycles is mostly explained by variability in how long the follicular phase takes. If no embryo implants, the corpus luteum has a built-in lifespan and degrades regardless, causing progesterone and estrogen to fall; that hormone withdrawal is what causes the endometrium to be shed as menstrual bleeding, which also removes the negative feedback that had been suppressing FSH, letting FSH rise again and start the next cycle's follicular phase.
