Both leukemia and lymphoma begin with the same fundamental event any cancer does: a single cell in the hematopoietic or lymphoid lineage accumulates mutations that let it escape the normal controls on proliferation, survival, and differentiation, and then expands as a clone -- all descendant cells carrying the same founding mutations. What differs is which specific cell type transforms and, largely as a consequence, where that clone predominantly lives.

Leukemia arises from cells that normally develop within the bone marrow itself (myeloid or lymphoid precursors at various stages of maturation), and the malignant clone typically fills the marrow space and releases abnormal cells directly into the peripheral blood, which is why it's diagnosed largely from a blood count and marrow biopsy rather than by finding a solid mass. As the leukemic clone expands, it crowds out the marrow's normal production of red cells, white cells, and platelets, producing anemia, infection susceptibility, and bleeding tendency simultaneously -- not because any one of those cell lines is directly attacked, but because they're all competing for the same limited marrow space and support signals the leukemic clone is now monopolizing.

Lymphoma instead arises from lymphocytes that have already left the marrow and matured within organized lymphoid tissue -- lymph nodes, spleen, tonsils, or gut-associated lymphoid tissue -- and the malignant clone typically proliferates in place there, forming a palpable or imaging-visible solid mass rather than circulating freely, which is why lymphoma is usually diagnosed by biopsying an enlarged lymph node rather than a blood count. The marrow-versus-lymphoid-tissue distinction is a real mechanistic one, tied to which cell type transformed and where that lineage normally resides, but it isn't an absolute boundary: advanced lymphoma can spread into the marrow and blood (a state sometimes described as leukemic phase), and some leukemias produce enlarged lymph nodes, so the distinction describes the predominant, defining pattern rather than a rule with no exceptions.
