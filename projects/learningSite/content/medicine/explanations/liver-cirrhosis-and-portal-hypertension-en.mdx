Cirrhosis is remarkable precisely because it is a convergent endpoint rather than a single disease: alcohol-related liver injury, chronic viral hepatitis, fatty liver disease, autoimmune liver conditions, and several other chronic insults all eventually produce the same underlying structural outcome if the injury continues long enough. The liver responds to repeated hepatocyte death the way most tissues respond to chronic injury -- with fibrosis, the deposition of scar tissue -- but in the liver's case, that fibrosis is organized into distorted regenerative nodules, islands of regenerating liver cells walled off by bands of fibrous scar, which permanently disrupts the liver's normal internal architecture regardless of which specific cause produced it.

That structural disruption is not merely cosmetic; it has a direct mechanical consequence for blood flow. The liver normally receives a large share of its blood supply through the portal vein, which carries blood from the intestines and spleen through a dense internal network of vessels before that blood rejoins the systemic circulation. Cirrhotic nodules and fibrous bands physically compress and distort this internal vascular network, dramatically increasing the resistance blood encounters trying to flow through the liver. Since the amount of blood arriving from the portal circulation does not decrease to match this new resistance, pressure backs up throughout the entire portal venous system -- portal hypertension.

The body's response to this backed-up pressure is to open collateral pathways, alternative veins that allow portal blood to bypass the obstructed liver and reach the systemic circulation by another route. Several such collateral routes exist, but the esophageal and gastric veins are a particularly dangerous one, because these vessels were never built to carry the volume and pressure now being forced through them. Under sustained elevated pressure they progressively dilate into esophageal varices -- thin-walled, fragile, abnormally distended veins sitting just beneath the esophageal lining -- which can rupture and produce sudden, severe gastrointestinal bleeding. This is the clearest illustration of why portal hypertension's danger extends well beyond the liver itself: the structural problem originates entirely within cirrhotic liver tissue, but its most acutely dangerous consequence plays out in vessels that, anatomically, have nothing else wrong with them.
