The peripheral nervous system covers every nerve outside the brain and spinal cord, and it splits along a functional line that matches how much conscious control a person has over each division. The somatic division is the one under direct voluntary command: sensory axons carry touch, pain, temperature, and proprioceptive signals inward from skin, muscles, and joints, while motor axons carry the deliberate commands a person issues outward to skeletal muscle. This is the division responsible for a person choosing to pick up a cup or walk across a room.

The autonomic division handles everything a person doesn't consciously manage -- heart rate, digestive motility, pupil diameter, sweating, blood vessel diameter -- and it's built as two branches with largely opposing effects on shared target organs. The sympathetic branch prepares the body for exertion: it increases heart rate and force of contraction, dilates the pupils and airways, and diverts blood flow away from digestion toward skeletal muscle, the classic fight-or-flight profile. The parasympathetic branch does close to the opposite on most of the same organs: it slows heart rate, constricts pupils, and promotes digestive activity, supporting a rest-and-digest state.

The key physiological detail is that these two branches don't take turns -- most organs are under continuous dual innervation, and their moment-to-moment behavior reflects a running balance between opposing tonic signals rather than a switch flipping from one branch to the other. Resting heart rate is a clear example: the heart's own pacemaker cells would fire faster than typical resting rate on their own, but ongoing parasympathetic (vagal) tone continuously restrains that rate, while sympathetic input continuously pushes the other way -- what's measured as heart rate at any instant is the net result of both inputs pulling simultaneously, not a period where one input is simply absent.
