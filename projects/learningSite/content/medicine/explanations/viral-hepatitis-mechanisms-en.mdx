It is tempting to picture viral hepatitis the way many other viral infections work -- the virus itself invading and killing cells -- but hepatitis viruses are, for the most part, not directly cytopathic to hepatocytes; they replicate within liver cells without necessarily destroying them outright. The liver damage that actually occurs comes overwhelmingly from the immune system's own response: cytotoxic T cells, recognizing viral antigens displayed on the surface of infected hepatocytes, kill those cells in an effort to eliminate the infection. This is a fundamentally immune-mediated injury, not primarily a direct viral one.

This mechanism produces a genuinely counterintuitive consequence. Because the damage is largely a byproduct of the immune system attacking infected cells, the intensity of that immune response, not simply the presence of the virus, determines how much liver injury occurs during an acute infection. A particularly vigorous immune response can destroy infected hepatocytes faster and more completely, clearing the virus more effectively -- but it does so by causing more acute liver damage in the process, an unavoidable tradeoff between clearance and collateral injury rather than a situation where a stronger response is straightforwardly better on every axis.

Whether an infection becomes chronic depends on the outcome of this immune battle: if the immune system successfully clears the virus during the acute phase, the infection resolves; if the virus evades that clearance and persists, the infection becomes chronic, and the same immune-mediated cell destruction recurs at a lower level over years rather than resolving after one acute episode. The different hepatitis viruses differ sharply in how often this happens. Hepatitis A and E, transmitted through the fecal-oral route (contaminated food or water), are essentially always cleared during acute infection and do not become chronic. Hepatitis B and C, transmitted through blood and bodily fluids, can establish long-term persistent infection; hepatitis C in particular has viral characteristics that make it especially effective at evading immune clearance, resulting in chronic infection in a large fraction of untreated cases.

This distinction matters well beyond the acute illness itself. A chronic hepatitis infection means the immune-mediated hepatocyte destruction already described keeps recurring, in smaller repeated episodes, for years or decades rather than occurring once. That sustained, repeated cycle of injury and attempted repair is precisely the kind of chronic insult this subject's liver-cirrhosis-and-portal-hypertension concept already established as the general route to cirrhosis -- which is why chronic viral hepatitis, particularly hepatitis B and C, ranks among the leading causes of cirrhosis worldwide.
