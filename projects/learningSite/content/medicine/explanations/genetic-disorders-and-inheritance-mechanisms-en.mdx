When a genetic disorder runs in a family, the actual pattern it follows -- which relatives are affected, which are unaffected carriers, and what fraction of children are at risk -- is determined entirely by the underlying mechanism causing it, not simply by which gene or chromosome is involved. Single-gene (Mendelian) disorders are caused by a mutation in one specific gene and follow exactly the same inheritance rules as any other single-gene trait. An autosomal dominant disorder, like Huntington's disease, requires only one copy of the disease allele to cause the condition, so an affected parent has roughly a 50% chance of passing it to each child, and the disorder cannot skip a generation -- everyone who inherits the allele will eventually be affected. An autosomal recessive disorder, like cystic fibrosis, requires two copies, so two unaffected carrier parents can have an affected child with roughly 25% probability per pregnancy, even though neither parent shows any sign of the condition themselves. X-linked disorders follow the same male-skewed pattern any X-linked trait does, since males have only one X chromosome to express whatever allele it carries.

Chromosomal disorders are a mechanistically distinct category, caused not by a mutation inside a single gene's sequence but by an abnormal number or structural rearrangement of entire chromosomes. Down syndrome is the best-known example: an error during meiosis (most often nondisjunction, where a chromosome pair fails to separate properly) leaves a gamete with an extra copy of chromosome 21, and fertilization then produces an embryo with three copies instead of the normal two -- trisomy 21. Because this typically arises from a new meiotic error rather than being passed down from an affected carrier parent, most cases of Down syndrome are not inherited from a parent who has the condition, and the risk instead rises with maternal age due to age-related increases in meiotic nondisjunction rates.

A third category, multifactorial disorders, doesn't reduce to any single genetic mechanism at all. Conditions like most forms of coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes, and many common birth defects arise from the combined, additive contribution of many different genes, each conferring only a small increase or decrease in risk, interacting with environmental and lifestyle factors like diet, smoking, and activity level. This combination is exactly why these conditions visibly cluster in families -- relatives share many of the same small-effect risk alleles -- without ever following the clean, predictable dominant or recessive inheritance pattern a true single-gene disorder does.
