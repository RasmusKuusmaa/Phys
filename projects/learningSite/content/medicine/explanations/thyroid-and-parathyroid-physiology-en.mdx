The thyroid gland's core product, thyroid hormone, is unusual among hormones for requiring a specific dietary mineral -- iodine -- as a literal building block; without enough dietary iodine, the thyroid simply cannot make adequate hormone no matter how hard the rest of the feedback loop pushes it to. The gland releases mostly the less active T4 form, which peripheral tissues then convert into the more potent T3 as needed, giving the body a way to fine-tune local hormone activity beyond what the thyroid itself releases.

Thyroid output is set by the same hypothalamic-pituitary architecture running through every major endocrine axis: hypothalamic TRH stimulates pituitary TSH release, TSH drives the thyroid to produce more hormone, and rising thyroid hormone levels feed back to suppress both TRH and TSH. This closed loop has a genuinely useful diagnostic consequence: because TSH and thyroid hormone move in opposite directions when the thyroid itself is malfunctioning, a thyroid that is underperforming shows up as a *high* TSH (the pituitary working overtime trying to compensate for a gland that won't respond), while a thyroid that is overactive shows up as a *low* TSH (the pituitary backing off because there's already too much thyroid hormone in circulation). TSH is often a more sensitive signal of thyroid dysfunction than measuring thyroid hormone itself, precisely because the feedback loop amplifies small changes in the gland's actual output.

What makes thyroid hormone unusual, compared to most other hormones covered elsewhere in this module, is the sheer breadth of its targets: it sets the baseline rate of oxygen consumption and heat production in nearly every tissue in the body, rather than acting on one organ system the way, say, a reproductive hormone does. That is why both an underactive and an overactive thyroid produce diffuse, whole-body symptom patterns -- energy level, body temperature regulation, heart rate, and weight all shift together -- rather than a single localized effect tied to one organ.

The parathyroid glands sit physically embedded in the thyroid's surface, which invites the assumption that they're part of the same system, but they run an entirely separate feedback loop with a different job: maintaining blood calcium within a tight range. When blood calcium falls, the parathyroid glands release parathyroid hormone, which raises calcium back up through three coordinated actions -- pulling calcium out of bone (bone resorption), telling the kidneys to reabsorb more calcium rather than excreting it, and activating vitamin D, which in turn increases calcium absorption from the gut. Once calcium returns to its normal range, parathyroid hormone release shuts back down, the same negative-feedback logic seen throughout the endocrine system, just applied to a mineral rather than another hormone.
