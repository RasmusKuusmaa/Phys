Respiratory failure is not one problem but two, distinguished by which gas the lung has stopped handling adequately, and the distinction matters because oxygen and carbon dioxide behave very differently as they cross the alveolar-capillary membrane. Carbon dioxide is roughly twenty times more soluble in the relevant tissue and diffuses correspondingly more easily; oxygen transfer is far more sensitive to anything that disrupts the matching between airflow and blood flow.

Ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch is the most common mechanism behind hypoxemic respiratory failure. In a perfectly matched lung, every unit of blood flow is paired with a proportionate unit of airflow, so oxygen picked up matches oxygen available. Real lungs never achieve this perfectly, and disease exaggerates the mismatch: a region with airflow but reduced blood flow (say, from a small pulmonary embolus) wastes that ventilation, since there's insufficient blood present to pick up the oxygen; a region with blood flow but reduced airflow (say, a partially consolidated area from pneumonia, or a collapsed airway) wastes that blood flow instead, since the blood passing through picks up little additional oxygen. Either direction of mismatch lowers the overall efficiency of oxygen uptake specifically.

A shunt represents the complete version of this same problem: blood passing through pulmonary circulation while making zero contact with any ventilated alveolus at all, whether because of a structural vascular abnormality or because an entire lung region is completely unventilated (fully collapsed or completely fluid-filled). This has a specific and clinically important consequence: raising the oxygen concentration delivered to the alveoli that are ventilated does nothing for blood that never passed through a ventilated alveolus in the first place, which is why shunt-driven hypoxemia characteristically fails to improve much with supplemental oxygen, unlike ordinary V/Q mismatch, which usually does respond at least partially.

Hypercapnic respiratory failure runs on a different logic entirely, precisely because carbon dioxide's much easier diffusion makes it largely insensitive to the same matching problems that devastate oxygen transfer. A region of lung tissue can be quite poorly matched for oxygen and still clear carbon dioxide reasonably well, as long as some ventilation is reaching it. Hypercapnia instead points toward a failure of ventilation as a whole -- simply not enough total air movement in and out of the lungs, whether from weak respiratory muscles, a depressed drive to breathe, or an airway so obstructed that overall airflow (not just its distribution) is inadequate. This is exactly why a patient can present with significant hypoxemia from V/Q mismatch or shunt while maintaining a completely normal carbon dioxide level, right up until whatever is causing the oxygen problem becomes severe enough to also compromise the patient's overall ability to move air.
