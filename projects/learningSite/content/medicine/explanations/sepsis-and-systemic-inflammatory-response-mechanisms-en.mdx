Every local infection triggers the same basic innate-immune playbook: nearby blood vessels dilate and become more permeable, phagocytes are recruited to the site, and inflammatory signaling molecules (cytokines) coordinate the whole response. This works well precisely because it stays local -- the dilation and leakiness let immune cells into the infected toe or wound while the rest of the circulatory system continues operating normally, maintaining blood pressure and organ perfusion everywhere else.

Sepsis is what happens when that same signaling escapes its local context and floods the entire bloodstream. Cytokines that were meant to coordinate a response in one patch of tissue instead circulate everywhere, and the vascular effects that were helpful when confined to one infected area become catastrophic when they happen throughout the entire vascular system at once: widespread vasodilation and capillary leakiness drop blood pressure and let fluid escape from vessels faster than the heart can compensate for. The result is inadequate blood flow to organs that may be perfectly healthy and nowhere near the original infection -- kidneys, liver, and lungs fail not because the pathogen has invaded them, but because they are being starved of the oxygen and perfusion they need.

This is why sepsis is fundamentally a disorder of the host's own response overshooting its target, not simply an infection that has become "worse" or "spread further." The distinction matters clinically: because the vascular crisis has become somewhat self-sustaining once the systemic cascade is underway, clearing the triggering infection with antibiotics does not automatically reverse the vascular and organ-perfusion problems already in motion -- the circulatory crisis has to be managed in its own right, in parallel with treating the underlying infection, rather than being expected to resolve on its own once the pathogen is gone.
