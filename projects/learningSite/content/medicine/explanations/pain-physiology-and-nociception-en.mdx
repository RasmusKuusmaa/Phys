Pain begins peripherally with nociceptors: specialized sensory nerve endings tuned specifically to stimuli intense enough to threaten tissue -- extreme mechanical pressure, damaging heat or cold, or tissue-damaging chemicals released by injury itself. A nociceptor converts that stimulus into an electrical signal that travels along its own axon into the spinal cord, where it synapses in the dorsal horn -- the first, and one of the most important, relay and modulation points in the entire pathway.

From the dorsal horn, the signal ascends primarily via the spinothalamic tract to the thalamus, and from there to multiple cortical regions that together localize the pain, characterize its quality, and attach emotional and motivational weight to it (the reason a sharp pain demands attention in a way a duller ache does not). Critically, every relay point along this route -- the dorsal horn especially -- is a site where the signal can be amplified or dampened, not just passed along unchanged. Descending pathways from the brainstem and cortex can inhibit incoming nociceptive signals at the dorsal horn itself, which is a real, physiological explanation for why the same injury can hurt more or less depending on attention, expectation, and emotional state, without any change in the tissue damage that triggered it.

This same modulatory machinery is central to understanding chronic pain. Persistent pain long after an injury has healed is not necessarily evidence of ongoing tissue damage -- it frequently reflects sensitization of the nociceptive pathway itself: peripheral nociceptors becoming abnormally excitable (peripheral sensitization), or the dorsal horn amplifying its response to incoming signals so that normally non-painful input is processed as painful (central sensitization). Because this sensitization is a change in how the nervous system processes signals, not a change in the tissue being signaled about, chronic pain can genuinely persist for years after the original injury has fully resolved -- a real physiological state, not evidence that something has been missed or that the person is exaggerating.
