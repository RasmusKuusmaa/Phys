Obstructive lung disease is defined functionally, by increased resistance to airflow that is disproportionately worse on exhalation than inhalation, but that shared functional definition covers at least two mechanistically distinct diseases with very different implications for how much of the obstruction can actually be reversed.

Asthma's obstruction arises acutely, from a combination of airway wall inflammation and bronchial smooth muscle contraction (bronchoconstriction) triggered by an allergen, irritant, cold air, exercise, or another provoking stimulus. Both components narrow the airway's internal diameter, and both are, critically, processes happening to airway tissue that remains structurally intact between episodes -- the smooth muscle can relax again, and the inflammatory swelling can resolve, once the trigger is addressed. This is why appropriately treating an asthma attack (relaxing the constricted muscle, calming the inflammation) can substantially reverse the obstruction, often within minutes to hours.

COPD is a fundamentally different kind of process: cumulative, largely irreversible structural damage, most commonly built up over years of exposure to an inhaled irritant such as tobacco smoke. Two overlapping processes contribute. Chronic bronchitis involves ongoing inflammation and mucus hypersecretion that thickens and narrows the airway walls themselves, a change that persists rather than resolving between exacerbations. Emphysema is a more destructive process still: it breaks down the elastic connective tissue of the alveolar walls, the same elastic tissue that, in a healthy lung, generates outward recoil pressure that helps hold the small airways open during exhalation. Losing that elastic scaffolding means the small airways lose their structural support and collapse prematurely during exhalation, trapping air behind the collapsed segment regardless of how relaxed the surrounding smooth muscle is.

This mechanistic distinction is exactly why the same presenting symptoms -- wheezing, shortness of breath, difficulty exhaling -- can arise from two different diseases with very different trajectories: one built around a reversible combination of inflammation and muscle spasm in structurally normal airways, the other built around an accumulated, largely permanent structural failure of the lung's own supportive architecture.
