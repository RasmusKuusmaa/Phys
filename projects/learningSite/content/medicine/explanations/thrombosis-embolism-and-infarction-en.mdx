Blood's default state inside an intact vessel is liquid, maintained by a genuinely delicate balance: clotting factors stand ready to seal any breach in the vessel wall the moment one occurs, while a separate set of anti-clotting mechanisms actively prevents that same clotting machinery from activating anywhere a breach hasn't actually happened. A thrombus is what results when that balance tips the wrong way -- a clot forming inside a vessel that was never actually breached, or persisting well past the point the original injury needed it to. Rudolf Virchow's classic framework identifies three routes to that tipping point, now known as Virchow's triad: damage to the vessel's inner lining (the endothelium) exposes clot-promoting material that is normally kept hidden; abnormal blood flow, whether stagnant pooling (as in a leg immobilized for a long flight) or turbulent disruption (as at an irregular heart valve), disrupts the smooth flow that normally keeps clotting factors evenly diluted and moving; and a hypercoagulable state -- from a genetic clotting disorder, pregnancy, or certain cancers -- shifts the blood's own chemistry toward clot formation. Most real thromboses involve more than one leg of this triad simultaneously.

A thrombus does its damage in one of two distinct ways, and the distinction matters for both symptoms and treatment. It can simply keep growing at the site where it formed until it occludes the vessel enough to block flow locally -- a slowly worsening problem at one specific location. Or, more unpredictably, a fragment can break free from the main thrombus entirely, becoming a mobile embolus that the bloodstream carries onward, completely unattached, until it reaches a vessel too narrow for it to pass and lodges there -- causing sudden, complete blockage at a site that may be far from where the clot originally formed and had shown no prior symptoms at all. Where an embolus ends up is dictated by ordinary circulatory anatomy, not chance: a thrombus that forms in a leg's deep veins travels with venous blood back toward the heart and is then pumped directly into the pulmonary circulation, so it reliably lodges in the lungs as a pulmonary embolism; a thrombus that forms in the heart itself or in an artery instead travels onward through the arterial system to whatever organ that particular artery supplies -- the brain, a limb, the intestines -- producing a stroke, limb ischemia, or bowel ischemia respectively, depending purely on which vessel it came from.

Whether a vessel is blocked in place or by an arriving embolus, the tissue downstream suffers the same underlying insult: sudden oxygen deprivation. If that deprivation is severe and prolonged enough, the result is an infarction -- localized tissue death, the pathology mechanism underlying a heart attack (myocardial infarction), an ischemic stroke (cerebral infarction), and a pulmonary infarction alike. The severity of any one infarction, however, is not determined solely by which vessel got blocked -- it depends heavily on speed and on collateral circulation, the alternate small vessels many tissues maintain as a backup blood supply. An artery narrowing gradually over years (as in slowly progressive coronary artery disease) often gives nearby collateral vessels time to enlarge and partially take over the threatened territory's blood supply, sometimes allowing severe underlying disease to remain silent for a long time. A sudden, complete blockage gives collateral vessels no such time to compensate, which is why an acute thrombus or embolus occluding a previously healthy-looking vessel can produce a much larger, more devastating infarction than a slowly narrowing vessel of the same final severity ever would.
