Not every pathogen can spread by simple person-to-person contact -- some depend entirely on a non-human intermediary to complete their transmission cycle, and understanding which kind of dependency is at work matters for predicting how a disease will actually spread and how it could be interrupted. A vector-borne pathogen relies on a blood-feeding arthropod -- overwhelmingly mosquitoes and ticks -- not as a passive carrier but as an active biological participant. The malaria parasite, for instance, undergoes part of its life cycle inside the Anopheles mosquito itself, developing through stages that can only occur there before the mosquito becomes capable of transmitting it onward. This means the vector's own biology -- how long it lives, where it ranges geographically, when and how often it feeds -- directly determines where and when the disease can circulate, and it also means that attacking the vector population (insecticides, bed nets, eliminating breeding sites) can interrupt transmission entirely without ever treating a single infected human or targeting the pathogen directly.

A zoonotic disease describes a different, though sometimes overlapping, kind of dependency: a pathogen that normally circulates within an animal reservoir species, with humans as an occasional, incidental host rather than the pathogen's usual home. Rabies circulates in various mammal reservoirs; plague circulates among rodents. Because the animal reservoir keeps the pathogen alive and circulating in nature regardless of what happens to any human who becomes infected, a zoonotic disease's underlying source can never be eliminated by treating human cases alone -- unlike a pathogen (measles, for instance) that exists only in a human-to-human transmission chain, where interrupting that chain in the human population is sufficient to eliminate the disease entirely.

These two categories are not mutually exclusive -- they frequently describe two links of the same chain. Many vector-borne diseases are also zoonotic: a mosquito or tick picks up a pathogen from its normal animal reservoir and only incidentally bites a human while feeding, creating an animal-to-vector-to-human transmission chain rather than a single mechanism. Recognizing which parts of that chain are actually in play for a given disease -- is there a vector, is there an animal reservoir, or both -- determines which intervention point (vector control, reservoir management, or human treatment) can actually interrupt transmission, since attacking the wrong link in the chain leaves the others fully intact.
