Cartilage is a strange tissue in one crucial respect: it has no blood vessels of its own. Every nutrient a chondrocyte needs, and every waste product it produces, has to diffuse through the dense cartilage matrix to and from the synovial fluid bathing the joint, which makes cartilage repair inherently slow compared to almost any vascularized tissue in the body. For most of adult life this slow maintenance is enough to keep pace with ordinary joint use, but it leaves cartilage with very little reserve capacity when the balance shifts toward more breakdown or less repair.

Several things can shift that balance. Obesity and joint malalignment increase the mechanical load a joint's cartilage has to absorb with every step; age reduces chondrocytes' own repair capacity; and a prior injury -- a torn meniscus, a fracture through the joint surface -- can damage cartilage directly and permanently change how load is distributed across the joint afterward. Whatever the trigger, once cartilage volume starts to drop, the process tends to accelerate rather than plateau: the newly exposed subchondral bone remodels in response to its new, unprotected loading pattern, and the stressed chondrocytes themselves release inflammatory cytokines and cartilage-degrading enzymes that damage the surrounding matrix further, a feed-forward loop rather than a single insult that simply resolves.

This is precisely the mechanism that distinguishes osteoarthritis from an autoimmune joint disease like rheumatoid arthritis. There is no misdirected immune attack here -- the driving process is local, mechanical, and cellular, playing out independently in each joint according to that joint's own loading history and repair capacity. That is why osteoarthritis so often shows up asymmetrically, worst in the joints that have carried the most weight or seen the most repetitive stress over a lifetime, rather than the symmetric, systemically inflamed pattern that a true autoimmune arthritis produces.
