The pancreas faces the same basic engineering problem the stomach does, but with an even sharper edge: it manufactures enzymes -- particularly proteases, capable of breaking down protein -- powerful enough to digest the pancreas's own tissue just as readily as they digest food. The safeguard that normally prevents this is timing and location: the pancreas synthesizes and stores these enzymes as inactive precursor molecules called zymogens, which only become catalytically active once they reach the small intestine, where a specific enzyme (enteropeptidase) triggers the activation cascade in an environment safely away from the pancreas itself.

Pancreatitis is what happens when this safeguard fails and zymogens become activated prematurely, while still inside the pancreas. Once even a small amount of active enzyme is present inside pancreatic tissue, it can trigger activation of nearby zymogens as well, producing a self-amplifying cascade of enzymatic activity that begins digesting the pancreas's own cells and surrounding tissue -- autodigestion -- which is what produces the intense local inflammation, tissue necrosis, and pain characteristic of acute pancreatitis.

Gallstones and alcohol are the two most common triggers of this premature activation, and they reach the same endpoint through genuinely different routes. A gallstone can lodge in the shared duct through which both bile and pancreatic secretions normally drain into the small intestine, physically obstructing outflow. This backs up enzyme-rich pancreatic fluid, raising pressure within the pancreatic ducts and pancreatic tissue itself, a mechanical disruption that appears to trigger inappropriate zymogen activation. Alcohol works through a different, less purely mechanical route: it appears to directly disrupt the intracellular machinery -- including the normal packaging and trafficking of zymogens within pancreatic cells -- that keeps these enzymes safely sequestered and inactive, again resulting in activation occurring where and when it should not.

The key mechanistic insight is that despite their different starting points, gallstone-induced and alcohol-induced pancreatitis converge on the identical downstream failure: digestive enzymes becoming catalytically active inside the organ that makes them rather than at their intended destination. Recognizing this shared endpoint, rather than treating the two causes as producing unrelated diseases, is what makes pancreatitis a single mechanistic category rather than two separate conditions that happen to share a name.
