The molecular mechanisms behind antibiotic resistance -- an enzyme that destroys the drug, a modified drug target, an efflux pump that removes it from the cell -- explain how a single bacterium becomes resistant. But they don't, by themselves, explain why resistance is treated as an urgent population-level and global public health crisis rather than a series of isolated, unlucky events. Two additional mechanisms operate above the level of any single bacterium.

The first is selective pressure at the population scale. Any exposure to an antibiotic doesn't just target the organism causing one patient's diagnosed infection -- it exerts pressure on the entire bacterial population present in that setting, in that patient's own microbiome and in the surrounding environment. Susceptible organisms die; any organism that happens to already carry a resistance mechanism survives and, freed from competition with its susceptible neighbors, reproduces disproportionately. This means heavier or less targeted antibiotic use shifts the resistance profile of an entire circulating bacterial population, not just the one organism being treated, and it happens regardless of whether any individual antibiotic course is dosed and completed exactly correctly.

The second mechanism is horizontal gene transfer. Resistance genes are frequently carried on plasmids -- small, circular pieces of DNA separate from a bacterium's main chromosome -- and plasmids can move directly from one bacterial cell to another through a process called conjugation, including between different bacterial species entirely. This means a resistance mechanism that evolves once, in one organism, doesn't need to re-evolve independently in a different species before it can appear there; it can simply be transferred, which spreads a resistance trait far faster across a bacterial community than ordinary vertical inheritance (parent cell to daughter cell) within a single species ever could.

These two mechanisms compound at the scale of a healthcare system or community. Heavy or unnecessary antibiotic use in one patient, farm, or hospital ward doesn't just risk that specific infection failing to respond to treatment -- it shapes the resistance profile of the wider bacterial population that other people, in that same setting or one connected to it through referral, transfer, or ordinary community contact, will subsequently be exposed to. This is exactly why antimicrobial stewardship -- deliberately restricting antibiotic use to situations where it provides genuine benefit, rather than using it whenever it might conceivably help -- is treated as a shared, systemic responsibility distributed across an entire healthcare system, not simply a private treatment decision between one clinician and one patient.
