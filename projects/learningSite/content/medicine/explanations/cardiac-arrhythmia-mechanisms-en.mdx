A normal heartbeat is the product of a precisely sequenced electrical relay. The sinoatrial (SA) node, a small patch of specialized pacemaker cells in the right atrium, spontaneously depolarizes faster than any other cardiac tissue and so sets the pace for the entire heart under normal conditions. Its signal spreads across both atria, triggering their contraction, then converges on the atrioventricular (AV) node, which deliberately delays the signal briefly -- giving the atria time to finish emptying into the ventricles before the ventricles contract -- before releasing it into the His-Purkinje system, a specialized conduction network that distributes the signal rapidly enough that the entire ventricular mass contracts in near-unison rather than as an uncoordinated wave.

Arrhythmias arise from disruption of either half of this system: the generation of the impulse, or its conduction. A generation-side abnormality is the more intuitive category -- some site other than the SA node develops enough spontaneous electrical activity to fire on its own (an ectopic focus), interrupting or overriding the normal rhythm, or the SA node itself fires too quickly, too slowly, or irregularly.

Reentry is the mechanistically distinct, and often more clinically significant, category, and it depends on a structural or functional quirk rather than an abnormal pacemaker. If a region of cardiac tissue contains two possible conduction pathways with different properties -- one that conducts slowly but recovers quickly, another that conducts quickly but recovers slowly -- a single well-timed impulse can travel down the slow pathway just as the fast pathway is finishing its recovery, arrive there in time to re-excite it, and travel back up to re-excite the slow pathway's origin all over again. The result is a self-sustaining loop that requires no ongoing input from the SA node at all: once established, it continues firing on its own, often considerably faster than the SA node's normal rate, and effectively takes over as the heart's dominant pacemaker for as long as the circuit persists.

What actually determines an arrhythmia's danger is not simply whether the resulting rate is fast or slow, but the mechanism and its downstream effect on coordinated pumping. An organized fast rhythm, even an uncomfortably rapid one, can still allow the atria and ventricles to contract in a coordinated sequence and maintain adequate blood flow. A chaotic rhythm -- ventricular fibrillation being the extreme case, where the ventricular muscle depolarizes in an uncoordinated, quivering pattern rather than contracting as a unit -- stops effective pumping almost immediately regardless of the underlying rate, which is why mechanism and coordination, not raw heart rate alone, are what actually determine clinical severity.
