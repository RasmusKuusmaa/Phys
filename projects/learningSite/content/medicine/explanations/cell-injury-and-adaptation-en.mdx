A cell's response to a harmful stimulus is not a simple switch between "fine" and "dead" -- it is a graded continuum, and where a given cell lands on that continuum depends on how severe the stress is and how long it lasts. A brief, mild insult -- a short period of reduced oxygen, a low dose of a toxin -- typically produces reversible injury: the cell's ion pumps fail and water rushes in (cellular swelling), or its metabolism is disrupted enough that fat droplets accumulate inside it, but the underlying structures (mitochondria, nucleus, membranes) remain intact enough that removing the stress lets the cell return fully to normal. Push the same stress further in intensity or duration, and the cell crosses into irreversible injury: its membranes lose integrity beyond repair, calcium floods in uncontrolled, and its own digestive enzymes end up breaking the cell down from within -- a threshold, once crossed, that removing the original stress can no longer undo.

Distinct from injury altogether is a family of adaptive responses, in which a cell or tissue that is not dying instead restructures itself to cope with a new, sustained level of demand. Four patterns cover most of what's seen in practice, and they are defined by exactly what changes: hypertrophy increases the size of individual cells without adding more of them, the only option available to cell types like cardiac and skeletal muscle that essentially cannot divide once mature -- an overworked heart chamber thickens this way. Hyperplasia instead increases the number of cells through active division, available only to cell types capable of proliferating -- the liver's remarkable ability to regrow after partial surgical removal works this way. Atrophy runs the process in reverse, shrinking cell size or number when demand or nutrient supply drops -- a limb immobilized in a cast loses muscle mass through atrophy within weeks. And metaplasia swaps one differentiated cell type for a different one better suited to a changed environment, without any change in cell size or number -- exposed to years of cigarette smoke, the airway's normal ciliated columnar cells are replaced by tougher, more chemically resistant squamous cells.

None of these four adaptations is a disease in itself. Each represents tissue successfully finding a new stable state under changed conditions, and the underlying cells remain healthy, functioning cells of a (possibly different) normal type. The clinical significance of adaptation, when it exists, comes from what it implies about the ongoing stress rather than the adaptation itself: metaplasia specifically is worth monitoring in some tissues because the cell type it produces, while itself benign, sits at increased risk of accumulating further genetic changes over years of continued exposure -- a risk that exists because the underlying irritant is still present and still acting, not because the metaplastic cells themselves are already abnormal in a threatening way.
