The cytochrome P450 (CYP450) family of liver enzymes is the single most important gateway most drugs pass through on their way out of the body, and because a relatively small number of CYP450 enzymes (given names like CYP3A4 or CYP2D6) collectively process the majority of all drugs in clinical use, sharing an enzyme is an extremely common way for two otherwise unrelated drugs to interact.

Enzyme induction and enzyme inhibition are the two dominant mechanisms, and they push in opposite directions. An inducer increases the amount or activity of a specific CYP450 enzyme -- often by upregulating the gene that produces it -- so every drug that enzyme metabolizes gets cleared faster than usual, and its blood concentration, and often its effect, falls. This matters clinically because a drug dose that was carefully calibrated to be effective can quietly stop working once an inducer is introduced, without the drug itself changing at all. An inhibitor does the reverse: by blocking or slowing the enzyme, it causes every drug that enzyme normally handles to accumulate, sometimes to toxic concentrations at a dose that was previously safe. Grapefruit juice is a well-known example of an inhibitor -- it blocks intestinal CYP3A4, and a range of unrelated drugs that rely on that enzyme for their first-pass metabolism can reach unexpectedly high blood levels if taken with it.

A third, subtler mechanism is competitive metabolism: two drugs that are both substrates (rather than an inducer or inhibitor) for the same enzyme simply compete for its limited processing capacity, each slowing the other's clearance somewhat without either one specifically upregulating or blocking the enzyme. The practical upshot of all three mechanisms is the same lesson: knowing what a drug treats tells you nothing about which other drugs it might interact with -- only knowing its metabolic pathway does, which is why checking a new medication against a patient's existing drug list (and even their diet, in the grapefruit case) is a standard part of safe prescribing rather than a formality.
