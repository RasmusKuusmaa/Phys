Shock is a functional diagnosis, not a description of any one broken part -- it names the outcome (blood flow inadequate to meet tissue metabolic demand) without specifying which component of the circulatory system produced it. That is precisely why understanding shock means understanding four genuinely distinct mechanisms that all converge on the same endpoint.

Hypovolemic shock is the most mechanically straightforward: the total volume of blood available to fill the circulation has simply dropped too low, most commonly from hemorrhage, but also from severe fluid losses through burns, vomiting, or diarrhea. The heart and blood vessels themselves are functioning normally; there is just not enough blood for them to work with.

Cardiogenic shock inverts that picture -- blood volume is adequate, but the heart itself has failed as a pump, unable to generate sufficient forward flow despite normal or even elevated filling. This is the same fundamental pumping failure covered in this subject's heart-failure-mechanisms concept, simply severe enough in its acute form to precipitate outright circulatory collapse rather than the more gradual, compensated decline typical of chronic heart failure.

Distributive shock introduces a mechanism neither of the above two involves: the blood vessels themselves lose their normal muscular tone and dilate excessively, expanding the total vascular space far beyond what the existing blood volume can adequately pressurize. Septic shock, triggered when an overwhelming infection causes the immune system to release inflammatory mediators that relax vascular smooth muscle throughout the body, is the most common cause -- the patient has not lost blood volume and the heart has not failed as a pump, but the container the blood is circulating through has become too large for the amount of fluid inside it.

Obstructive shock is the fourth and most purely mechanical category: something physically blocks blood flow despite an otherwise entirely normal heart, normal blood volume, and normal vascular tone. A large pulmonary embolism lodged in the pulmonary artery, or fluid accumulating in the sac around the heart and compressing it from outside, are both examples of a physical barrier interrupting flow rather than a failure of any of the circulatory system's active components.

Because these four categories arise from genuinely different points of failure, the same low-blood-pressure endpoint can mean entirely different things depending on its underlying mechanism, and effectively addressing the cause requires identifying which of the four is actually responsible rather than treating "shock" as a single undifferentiated problem.
