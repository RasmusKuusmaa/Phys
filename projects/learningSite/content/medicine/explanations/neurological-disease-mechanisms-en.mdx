It's tempting to lump every neurological disease into one generic category of "brain damage," but the underlying mechanisms are genuinely different from each other, and that difference is exactly what explains why different neurological conditions look so different clinically.

Neurodegeneration is permanent neuron loss, typically driven by a specific misfolded protein accumulating to toxic levels within a specific, vulnerable population of neurons. Because different neurodegenerative diseases target different neuron populations, their symptoms diverge sharply even though the underlying process -- protein misfolding and accumulation leading to cell death -- is mechanistically similar across them: a disease concentrated in dopamine-producing neurons in one brain circuit produces movement symptoms, while a disease concentrated in neurons supporting memory circuits produces cognitive decline instead. Because the neurons are actually dying, this category of disease is inherently progressive and, with current understanding, not reversible.

Demyelination is a mechanistically separate problem, since the neuron itself is not what's damaged -- it's the myelin sheath insulating the axon that's attacked or destroyed, often through the immune system mistakenly targeting it. Losing that insulation doesn't necessarily silence the axon completely; it slows and degrades the normally fast, saltatory, node-to-node conduction the axon depends on, so signals still get through but less reliably and more slowly. Because the underlying neuron survives, the system retains a genuine capacity to partially recover if remyelination occurs, which is the mechanistic reason demyelinating conditions often show a relapsing-remitting course -- periods of worsening followed by partial improvement -- that a purely neurodegenerative disease typically doesn't show.

Seizures involve neither cell death nor insulation loss but a third, different failure mode: a population of neurons firing excessively and in synchrony rather than each contributing its normal, more independent pattern of activity. Which brain region is swept into that hypersynchronized firing determines what a seizure looks like -- involvement of a motor region produces convulsive movement, involvement of a sensory region produces sensory phenomena. Critically, this is an episodic electrical malfunction rather than structural damage: between seizures, the neurons in the affected region are often structurally intact and can support entirely normal function, even though the underlying tendency toward another hypersynchronized episode remains.
