Before Gregor Mendel's experiments with pea plants in the 1860s, the dominant intuition about heredity was that offspring blended their parents' traits together like mixing two paints, with no way for a trait to reappear once it had been diluted out. Mendel's meticulous breeding experiments overturned this: when he crossed purebred tall and short pea plants, the first generation of offspring were not intermediate-height plants but uniformly tall, and when he bred that generation together, the short trait reappeared in about a quarter of the next generation, in exactly the proportion his particulate model of inheritance predicted -- discrete, unblendable units passing from parent to offspring, later named genes.

Each parent contributes exactly one allele (one version of a gene) to each offspring, so every individual carries two alleles for most genes -- one inherited from each parent. When those two alleles are identical, the situation is straightforward, but when they differ, one determines the phenotype: the dominant allele's effect appears whenever at least one copy is present, while the recessive allele's effect is masked, only appearing in the phenotype when an individual inherits two recessive copies (homozygous recessive). A Punnett square makes this systematic: laying out every possible combination of alleles the two parents' gametes could contribute turns Mendel's discrete-inheritance model into concrete, testable probabilities for each possible offspring genotype and phenotype.

Not every gene follows the simplest autosomal pattern, though. The 22 non-sex chromosome pairs in humans (the autosomes) carry genes that follow straightforward autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive inheritance, affecting males and females identically, since everyone has two copies of each autosome. Genes carried on the sex chromosomes follow a genuinely different pattern: because males have only one X chromosome (paired with a Y chromosome carrying few matching genes), a male expresses whatever allele sits on his single X, dominant or recessive, with no second copy available to mask a recessive one. Females, with two X chromosomes, need two recessive copies to show the same recessive phenotype. This asymmetry is exactly why X-linked recessive conditions like red-green colorblindness and hemophilia show up dramatically more often in men than in women, despite the underlying allele frequency in the population being identical for both sexes -- the difference is purely about how many copies each sex carries, not about which sex the allele 'prefers.'
