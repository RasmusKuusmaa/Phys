An antibody's shape is the key to everything it does. The molecule is built from four protein chains -- two identical "heavy" chains and two identical "light" chains -- arranged into a Y shape held together by disulfide bonds. The two arms of the Y each end in a variable region, so called because its exact amino acid sequence differs enormously from one antibody to another; this is the part of the molecule whose three-dimensional shape actually contacts and binds a specific antigen, and the huge combinatorial diversity generated during B cell development (the same process covered in the adaptive-immunity concept) is what lets the full repertoire of antibodies collectively recognize almost any antigen shape. The stem of the Y, the constant region, is far less variable and doesn't touch the antigen at all -- its job is to determine what happens next once binding occurs, and it's the one structural feature that defines which of the five antibody classes a given antibody belongs to.

Each class trades off structure for a specific functional niche. IgM is built as a pentamer -- five Y-shaped units joined together -- which gives it ten antigen-binding arms total and makes it exceptionally good at triggering the complement cascade once several of those arms have bound antigen; it's also the first class produced in a primary immune response, appearing before the immune system has had time to refine its output. IgG, by contrast, is a single Y-shaped monomer, but it's produced in enormous quantities during a mature immune response and is the only class with a structure recognized by a specific transport receptor on placental cells, letting it be actively pumped from the mother's blood into fetal circulation -- a newborn's early protection against many pathogens comes entirely from maternal IgG delivered this way, since the infant's own adaptive immune system hasn't yet had time to build a repertoire of its own. IgA is produced as a dimer specifically adapted to survive being secreted onto mucosal surfaces -- the gut lining, the respiratory tract, breast milk -- where it intercepts pathogens at the body's actual points of entry, before they ever reach the bloodstream. IgE circulates in very low concentrations but binds tightly to receptors on mast cells; when an IgE-coated mast cell encounters its matching antigen, it triggers rapid degranulation, releasing histamine and other inflammatory mediators, a mechanism that evolved for expelling parasitic worms but that also underlies allergic reactions when misdirected at harmless targets like pollen. IgD, the least understood of the five, functions mainly as a receptor on the surface of naive B cells rather than as a secreted molecule.

Binding an antigen is rarely the end of the story -- what an antibody actually accomplishes depends on what happens after binding. Neutralization is the most direct outcome: an antibody bound to the specific part of a virus or toxin that it needs to interact with a host cell simply blocks that interaction by physically occupying the site, disabling the pathogen or toxin without needing any other immune component involved. Opsonization works differently -- the antibody coats a pathogen's surface, and phagocytes carry receptors that specifically recognize antibody constant regions, so an opsonized pathogen gets engulfed far more efficiently than an uncoated one, turning antibody binding into a "tag" that another cell type acts on. Complement fixation shows how deeply the adaptive and innate systems interlock: certain antibody classes, once bound to a pathogen in sufficient density, present a shape that triggers the same complement cascade described under innate immunity, meaning an antibody's job is often to recruit and direct an innate defense mechanism rather than to do the destructive work itself.
