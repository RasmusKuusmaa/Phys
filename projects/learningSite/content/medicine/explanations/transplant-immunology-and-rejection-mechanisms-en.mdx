A transplanted organ is, immunologically, an entire foreign cell surface -- and the single most important part of that surface, for rejection purposes, is the donor's own set of MHC (in humans, HLA) molecules. These molecules differ from person to person more than almost any other protein, and the recipient's immune system recognizes a mismatched set as unambiguously non-self, which sets up three distinct rejection mechanisms operating on three very different timescales.

Hyperacute rejection is the fastest and most dramatic: it strikes within minutes to hours of the graft being connected to the recipient's blood supply, driven entirely by antibodies the recipient already had before transplant (from a prior transfusion, pregnancy, or earlier transplant) against donor blood-group or HLA antigens. These pre-existing antibodies bind the graft's blood vessel lining immediately, triggering clotting that cuts off the organ's blood supply before any new immune response has time to develop -- which is why pre-transplant antibody screening (crossmatching) exists specifically to catch this risk in advance.

Acute rejection is slower, unfolding over days to weeks, and works through a different mechanism entirely: recipient T cells directly recognizing donor MHC molecules displayed on graft cells. This direct recognition is an unusually strong immune stimulus -- a much larger fraction of a person's T cells can react to a foreign MHC molecule directly than would ever react to an ordinary foreign protein -- which is exactly why acute rejection is the primary target of the T-cell-suppressing drugs (like calcineurin inhibitors) that transplant recipients take for as long as the graft survives.

Chronic rejection is the slowest and, in many ways, the most clinically frustrating: it develops over months to years through gradual antibody- and cell-mediated injury to the graft's blood vessels, progressively narrowing them and starving the organ of blood supply. Because this process is driven by a different combination of mechanisms than acute rejection, the same immunosuppression regimen that reliably controls acute rejection often fails to prevent chronic rejection, making it a leading cause of eventual graft loss.

A related but mechanistically opposite problem is graft-versus-host disease, which only arises when the transplanted tissue itself contains immune cells (as in a bone marrow or hematopoietic stem cell transplant). Here, the donor's own immune cells -- now residing inside the recipient -- recognize the recipient's body as foreign and mount an attack against it, the reverse direction of every rejection mechanism described above.
