A eukaryotic cell solves a hard problem: it needs to run dozens of chemical processes at once, many of which would interfere with or actively destroy each other if they happened in the same open space. Compartmentalization -- wrapping each process inside its own membrane-bound organelle -- is the cell's solution. The nucleus is the most obvious example: it keeps the cell's DNA physically separate from the busy, chemically hostile cytoplasm, and its own inner membrane pores control exactly which molecules can get in to read that DNA or get out once transcribed into RNA. The nucleolus, a dense region inside the nucleus, is where the components of ribosomes are assembled before being exported to do their actual protein-building work in the cytoplasm.

Mitochondria present a similar logic from the opposite direction: they contain the machinery for aerobic respiration, a set of reactions that would be dangerous to run unconfined in the cytoplasm, since it involves highly reactive oxygen intermediates. Enclosing that machinery inside a double membrane keeps its byproducts contained and lets the mitochondrion maintain the steep chemical gradient across its inner membrane that ATP synthase uses to generate most of the cell's usable energy currency. Because mitochondria also sit at a chemical crossroads the cell can exploit for other purposes, they double as a signaling hub: when a cell sustains damage severe enough that continuing would be dangerous to the organism as a whole, mitochondria can release cytochrome c into the cytoplasm, a molecule that (together with other factors) sets off the apoptosis pathway, the cell's own carefully controlled self-destruct sequence.

Protein production and packaging follows its own dedicated pipeline. Ribosomes attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum synthesize proteins destined for secretion or for insertion into membranes, threading the growing protein directly into the ER's interior as it's built; the smooth endoplasmic reticulum, lacking ribosomes, instead specializes in lipid synthesis, calcium storage, and detoxifying certain molecules. Proteins leaving the rough ER travel next to the Golgi apparatus, a stack of flattened membrane sacs that chemically modifies them (commonly by attaching sugar groups) and sorts them into vesicles addressed to their correct destination -- the cell surface, a specific organelle, or outside the cell entirely. Lysosomes sit at the end of a different but related pipeline: they are the cell's digestion and recycling centers, packed with enzymes that break down worn-out organelles, misfolded proteins, and material the cell has imported from outside, all safely contained within a membrane that keeps those same enzymes from digesting the rest of the cell (a lysosomal membrane failure is exactly what leads to a cell digesting itself from the inside, a process called autolysis).

None of these organelles function as an isolated island. A protein's actual journey -- built in the rough ER, modified and sorted in the Golgi, delivered by vesicle to a lysosome or the cell membrane -- only works because each step hands off correctly to the next; a failure anywhere along that chain backs up and disrupts the entire pathway. Understanding organelle function this way, as a set of physically separated but functionally interlocking specialized compartments, is the foundation the rest of cell biology builds on -- how molecules actually get transported and signaled across these membranes, how a cell decides when to divide, and how a cell decides to end its own life when something has gone wrong.
