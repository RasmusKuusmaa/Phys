The pancreas leads a kind of double life. By tissue volume, it is overwhelmingly an exocrine gland, its acinar cells manufacturing the digestive enzymes that flow through a duct system into the small intestine -- the digestive role already covered in this subject's digestive-system concept. Scattered through that exocrine tissue like small islands (hence the name) are the islets of Langerhans, a comparatively tiny population of endocrine cells that nonetheless run one of the body's most tightly regulated feedback loops.

Two cell types within the islets do the actual regulating, and they work in genuine opposition to each other rather than one backing up the other. Beta cells detect rising blood glucose -- most directly after a meal -- and respond by releasing insulin, which lowers blood glucose through two complementary actions: it signals most of the body's cells (particularly muscle and fat tissue) to take glucose up out of the blood, and it signals the liver to convert incoming glucose into glycogen for storage rather than releasing any of its own stored glucose. Alpha cells detect the opposite condition, falling blood glucose between meals or during fasting, and respond by releasing glucagon, which acts specifically on the liver to reverse the storage process -- breaking glycogen back down into glucose and releasing it into the bloodstream to keep glucose available for tissues, especially the brain, that depend on a steady glucose supply.

The key insight this reciprocal arrangement captures is that glucose homeostasis is a two-signal system, not a one-signal system with a backup. In a person with normally functioning glucose regulation, insulin and glucagon are not idle most of the time waiting for an emergency -- they are continuously, actively secreted in inversely related amounts that track the glucose swings of ordinary eating and fasting cycles throughout the day. Insulin rises and glucagon falls after a meal; glucagon rises and insulin falls between meals. Disruption of glucose homeostasis, correspondingly, can come from either side of this push-pull system failing to respond correctly, not only from insulin alone.
