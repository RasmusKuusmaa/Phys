Infective endocarditis is a bacterial or, less often, fungal infection of the endocardium, and in practice this almost always means the heart valves specifically. What makes this infection mechanistically unusual isn't the organisms involved -- often ordinary skin or oral bacteria -- but the site itself. Heart valve tissue is largely avascular: unlike most tissue, which is threaded with its own small blood vessels, valve leaflets have little to no direct blood supply of their own. Immune cells and circulating antibodies reach infected tissue primarily by traveling through the vessels supplying that tissue, so a site with essentially no blood supply of its own is a site the immune system struggles to reach directly, no matter how effectively it's clearing bacteria everywhere else in the body.

Bacteria that colonize a valve don't simply sit exposed on its surface -- they become enmeshed with platelets and fibrin in a structure called a vegetation, a kind of biological shelter that further insulates them from both immune cells and from antibiotics, which likewise depend on blood flow to reach their target in adequate concentration. This combination -- an avascular site plus a physical vegetation -- is exactly why endocarditis typically requires a much longer course of antibiotics than an ordinary soft-tissue infection of similar size, and why source control (sometimes surgical valve repair or replacement) is often necessary in a way it wouldn't be for an infection sitting in well-perfused tissue.

The same low-flow, protected environment that shields the bacteria also creates a distinct mechanical hazard: pieces of the vegetation are physically fragile and can break off, traveling through the bloodstream as septic emboli. Depending on which side of the heart is affected, these emboli can lodge in the brain, spleen, kidneys, or extremities, seeding new infection or causing tissue damage far from the original valve -- which is exactly why endocarditis can present with a scattered, seemingly unrelated collection of symptoms across multiple organ systems rather than a single localized complaint.

Colonization itself doesn't happen just because bacteria reach the bloodstream -- transient bacteremia from routine activities (even vigorous toothbrushing) is common and rarely causes endocarditis on a healthy valve. Establishing infection typically requires two things to coincide: bacteria in the bloodstream, and a valve surface already primed for bacterial adhesion, usually because pre-existing valve damage or abnormal turbulent blood flow has already deposited a platelet-fibrin surface for circulating bacteria to attach to in the first place.
