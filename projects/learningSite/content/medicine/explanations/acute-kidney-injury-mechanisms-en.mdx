Acute kidney injury describes a functional outcome -- a sudden decline in the kidney's filtering ability -- that can result from failure at three genuinely different points along the path blood and urine take through the urinary system, and identifying which of the three is responsible is what determines whether the injury is likely to resolve quickly or has already progressed toward lasting damage.

Prerenal injury occurs before the problem ever reaches the kidney tissue itself: the kidney's filtration process depends on adequate blood flow and pressure reaching the glomerulus, and anything that reduces that flow -- severe dehydration, significant blood loss, or heart failure severe enough to drop cardiac output -- starves the kidney of what it needs to filter effectively, even though the nephrons themselves are, at least initially, completely structurally intact. This is mechanistically the most reversible category: restore adequate blood flow promptly, and kidney function typically recovers fully, precisely because nothing has actually been damaged yet.

Intrinsic (or renal) injury means actual damage has occurred within the kidney's own tissue. The most common route to this category is prolonged, unaddressed prerenal injury: if inadequate blood flow persists long enough, the tubular cells that depend on that blood supply begin to die, a condition called acute tubular necrosis, converting what started as a purely functional, reversible problem into genuine structural damage. Intrinsic injury can also result directly from toxins, certain medications, or inflammatory processes that attack nephron structures without any preceding prerenal phase at all.

Postrenal injury occurs entirely downstream of the kidney: an obstruction anywhere along the urinary tract's outflow path -- a kidney stone lodged in a ureter, an enlarged prostate compressing the urethra, a tumor pressing on the urinary tract from outside -- physically blocks urine from leaving the body. Blocked outflow backs up pressure through the entire system, eventually all the way back to the kidney itself, and that backed-up pressure impairs the kidney's ability to filter even though, at least initially, both the blood supply and the kidney tissue remain completely normal.

The reason this three-category distinction matters practically is that prerenal and postrenal injury are, in their early stages, purely functional problems with an identifiable, addressable external cause -- restore blood flow, or relieve the obstruction, and kidney function can recover completely. Intrinsic injury, by contrast, involves actual cell damage that follows its own recovery timeline regardless of how quickly the original trigger is addressed. Recognizing which category applies, rather than treating "acute kidney injury" as one undifferentiated problem, is what allows the underlying cause to be correctly identified in the first place.
