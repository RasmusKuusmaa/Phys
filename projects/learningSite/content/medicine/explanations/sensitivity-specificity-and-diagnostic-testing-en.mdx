Every diagnostic test makes two distinct kinds of mistake, and sensitivity and specificity are the two numbers that separately quantify them. Sensitivity looks only at people who truly have the disease and asks what fraction of them the test correctly flags as positive -- it says nothing at all about how the test behaves in healthy people. A test with high sensitivity is trustworthy when it comes back negative, because a highly sensitive test rarely lets a real case slip through undetected; this is why sensitive tests are the tool of choice for ruling a disease out.

Specificity looks at the mirror-image group: people who truly do not have the disease, and asks what fraction of them the test correctly identifies as negative. A highly specific test is trustworthy when it comes back positive, because it rarely flags a healthy person by mistake; specific tests are therefore the tool of choice for ruling a disease in, or for confirming a suspicious result from a more sensitive but less specific initial screen.

The genuinely counterintuitive part is that neither sensitivity nor specificity, on their own, tell an individual patient what their own positive result means. That requires Bayes' theorem, and the crucial extra ingredient it needs is prevalence -- how common the disease actually is in the population being tested. Imagine testing 100,000 people for a disease with 1-in-10,000 prevalence (10 true cases) using a test that is 99% sensitive and 99% specific. The test will correctly catch about 9.9 of those 10 true cases (99% sensitivity). But among the 99,990 healthy people, a 1% false-positive rate (from 99% specificity) produces roughly 1,000 false positives. So out of roughly 1,010 total positive results, only about 10 are real -- meaning a positive result on this excellent-sounding test is, in this rare-disease scenario, wrong about 99% of the time. This is not a flaw in the test's design; it's an unavoidable consequence of testing a rare condition, and it's exactly why a positive screening result for a rare disease is typically followed by a second, confirmatory test rather than treated as a diagnosis on its own.
