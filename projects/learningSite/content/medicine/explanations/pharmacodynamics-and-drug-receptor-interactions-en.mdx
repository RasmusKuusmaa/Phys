Pharmacodynamics is the study of what a drug does to the body once it's there, as distinct from pharmacokinetics, which asks how the drug got there and how long it stays. Most drugs work by binding a specific molecular target, most often a receptor protein embedded in a cell membrane, and the central tool for describing that binding's effect is the dose-response curve: plot the size of the biological effect against the dose given (conventionally on a logarithmic scale, since drug potency spans many orders of magnitude across different compounds), and the result is typically a smooth, S-shaped curve rising from no measurable effect at very low doses to a flat plateau at very high ones.

That curve carries two independent pieces of information, and confusing them is a common error. Potency describes how much drug it takes to produce a given effect -- a more potent drug's curve sits further to the left, reaching the same effect at a lower dose. Efficacy describes the maximum effect the drug can produce at all, no matter how high the dose goes -- read from the height of the curve's plateau, not its horizontal position. These two properties can vary completely independently: a highly potent drug (effective at tiny doses) can have low maximum efficacy, while a much less potent drug (needing a large dose) can have very high maximum efficacy. Clinically, efficacy usually matters more than potency for choosing a treatment, since a "weaker" (less potent) drug that reaches a higher maximum effect is often the better choice, and a marketing focus on potency alone can be misleading.

The other central distinction in pharmacodynamics is agonist versus antagonist, both defined relative to a specific receptor rather than to any particular physiological outcome. An agonist binds the receptor and triggers its normal downstream signaling, producing an effect in the same direction the body's own natural ligand would. An antagonist binds the same receptor but produces no signaling response of its own -- instead, by occupying the receptor, it physically blocks the natural ligand (or another drug) from binding there, reducing or eliminating that other molecule's effect. A pure antagonist, given alone with nothing to block, typically produces little or no effect by itself, however high the dose: its entire pharmacological job is interference, not stimulation. This is exactly the relationship between many opioid painkillers (agonists at opioid receptors) and naloxone (an antagonist at those same receptors): naloxone doesn't lower pain thresholds or cause any opioid-like effect of its own, it works purely by displacing the opioid from the receptor.
