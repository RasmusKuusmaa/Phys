Infertility is usually defined clinically as the inability to conceive after a year of regular, unprotected intercourse, but that single definition covers a genuinely wide range of distinct underlying mechanisms, split roughly evenly between the male and female partner (with a meaningful share of cases involving both, or remaining unexplained after full evaluation). Treating infertility as one problem rather than identifying which mechanism is actually responsible is why blanket assumptions about whose issue it is are both unhelpful and frequently wrong.

On the female side, one major mechanistic category is ovulatory dysfunction: something prevents the regular release of a mature egg, whether from polycystic ovary syndrome (a common hormonal/metabolic disorder disrupting normal follicle development), premature ovarian insufficiency, or other causes of irregular or absent ovulation. A mechanistically separate category is structural or tubal disease: the fallopian tubes can become blocked or scarred, most often from prior pelvic infection or endometriosis, physically preventing sperm from reaching the egg or a fertilized embryo from completing its journey to the uterus. These two categories require essentially opposite interventions -- restoring ovulation does nothing to open a blocked tube, and vice versa.

On the male side, the same kind of mechanistic split exists. One category is a problem with spermatogenesis itself: low sperm count, poor sperm motility, or abnormal sperm morphology, which can stem from hormonal imbalances, genetic conditions, prior testicular injury or infection, or heat/toxin exposure affecting the testes directly. The other category is obstructive: a blockage somewhere along the path sperm must travel out of the body -- in the epididymis or vas deferens, for instance, sometimes from prior infection, surgery (including vasectomy), or a congenital absence of part of the duct system -- that can completely prevent otherwise normal, healthy sperm from ever being ejaculated. A semen analysis showing zero sperm (azoospermia) can therefore mean either a production problem or an obstruction problem, and distinguishing between them is essential, because only one of the two is correctable by removing a blockage.

Because these mechanisms are genuinely distinct, effective evaluation has to test for each category specifically rather than assuming a cause based on which partner is being evaluated, and effective treatment has to target the actual mechanism identified rather than a generic 'infertility treatment' that happens to work for a different cause.
