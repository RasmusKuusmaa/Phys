Not every effect a drug produces is the one it was prescribed for, and toxicology's first job is sorting unwanted effects into categories that behave predictably enough to manage. Type A reactions are, in a real sense, too much of a good thing: they follow directly and predictably from the drug's known pharmacological mechanism, just to an unwanted degree or in an unwanted tissue. A blood-pressure medication causing excessive blood pressure drops, or a sedative causing excessive drowsiness, are Type A reactions -- dose-dependent, common, and manageable by adjusting how much drug is given. Type B reactions break that pattern entirely: they aren't a predictable extension of the drug's mechanism, they don't reliably scale with dose, and they're often driven by something specific to the individual -- an unusual genetic variant in a drug-metabolizing enzyme, or an immune system that mistakenly identifies the drug (or one of its metabolites) as a threat and mounts an allergic response. Because Type B reactions are rare and idiosyncratic, they're notoriously hard to catch in clinical trials, which typically enroll too few people to reliably encounter a reaction that only affects, say, one person in ten thousand -- which is why some serious Type B reactions only come to light after a drug reaches wide public use.

The therapeutic index gives toxicology a quantitative handle on drug safety that's separate from either reaction type. It's defined as the ratio between a toxic dose and an effective dose -- classically the dose toxic to 50% of a population divided by the dose effective in 50% of a population, though the exact statistical definition varies by context. A drug with a large therapeutic index has effective and toxic doses that sit far apart on the dose scale, giving clinicians a wide, forgiving margin: even a meaningfully wrong dose is unlikely to cause harm. A drug with a narrow therapeutic index has those two doses sitting close together, so a relatively small dosing error, or an interaction that changes the drug's blood concentration even modestly, can push a patient from an effective dose into a toxic one. This is exactly why drugs like warfarin (an anticoagulant) or many chemotherapy agents require frequent blood monitoring and carefully individualized dosing: their narrow therapeutic index leaves very little room for error, unlike a drug with a wide margin where precise dosing matters much less.
