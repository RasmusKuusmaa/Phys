The human body is not a sterile space that pathogens invade from the outside -- it is, from birth onward, densely populated by microbes that live on the skin, in the gut, in the nasal passages, and elsewhere, the vast majority of them causing no harm at all. Colonization is the word for this stable, ongoing presence: a microbe establishing itself at a body site, often for years, sometimes performing genuinely useful functions like producing vitamins the human body can't synthesize itself or physically crowding out space and nutrients that a more dangerous microbe might otherwise use. Infection is a distinct and further step: the microbe (whether it was already colonizing the body or newly introduced) invades host tissue and begins actively causing damage. The line between the two is not fixed for a given microbe -- it depends on a continuously shifting balance between how virulent the microbe is and how well the host's defenses are currently working, which is exactly why a person with a healthy immune system can carry a potentially dangerous microbe as a harmless colonizer for years, while the same microbe becomes a serious opportunistic infection the moment that person's immune defenses are compromised by illness, medication, or age.

Virulence is best understood as a toolkit rather than a single dial that turns up or down. Adhesion factors -- specialized surface proteins -- let a microbe grip onto host tissue firmly enough to resist being physically swept away by mechanisms like mucus flow or urine flow, which is usually the first requirement for establishing any foothold at all. Invasins are proteins that actively help a microbe breach a barrier it wouldn't otherwise cross, whether that's the wall of a host cell or a tissue layer meant to keep microbes out. Toxins do direct chemical damage, and come in genuinely different flavors: some (like many pore-forming toxins) physically rupture host cell membranes, while others hijack a host cell's own internal signaling machinery to make the cell behave in a way that benefits the pathogen, such as triggering the fluid secretion that produces the severe diarrhea characteristic of cholera. Immune-evasion mechanisms round out the toolkit: a polysaccharide capsule can physically resist being engulfed by an immune cell, and some pathogens survive by hiding inside host cells themselves, a location circulating antibodies simply can't reach.

Because these are separable, independently acquirable traits rather than one fused package, closely related bacteria -- even different strains of the exact same species -- can carry wildly different combinations of them. Escherichia coli is the textbook example: most E. coli strains living in the human gut are harmless commensals, quietly contributing to normal gut function, while other E. coli strains have acquired additional genes (often via the same horizontal gene transfer mechanisms that spread antibiotic resistance) coding for specific adhesion factors and toxins that make them capable of causing severe gastrointestinal or urinary tract disease. Understanding a pathogen's actual disease-causing potential, then, requires looking at the specific virulence factors a given strain carries and the specific state of the host it's interacting with -- neither the microbe's species identity alone nor the host's general health alone tells the whole story.
