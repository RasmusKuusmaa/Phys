Coronary artery disease begins long before any symptom appears, with a subtle injury to the endothelium, the single layer of cells lining the inside of the artery wall. Once that lining is disrupted -- by mechanical stress, chemical irritants, or simply chronic exposure to elevated LDL cholesterol -- LDL particles begin slipping through into the artery wall itself, where they become oxidized and trigger an inflammatory response. Circulating monocytes are recruited into the wall, differentiate into macrophages, and set about consuming the trapped, oxidized LDL; a macrophage that has engulfed enough lipid becomes a foam cell, and the accumulation of foam cells is what forms the soft, lipid-rich core of a developing plaque.

The artery wall does not passively accept this growing lipid deposit -- smooth muscle cells migrate in and lay down a fibrous cap over the lipid core, walling it off from the vessel lumen. This is actually a protective, stabilizing response, and it is the thickness and integrity of that cap, not the size of the lipid core underneath it, that determines whether a given plaque is dangerous. A plaque with a thick, well-formed fibrous cap tends to grow slowly and predictably, narrowing the vessel's lumen over years; once that narrowing becomes severe enough to limit blood flow during increased demand (exercise, for instance), it produces the classic, exertional chest pain of stable angina -- uncomfortable, but mechanically predictable.

The genuinely dangerous scenario is different, and it does not require a large plaque at all. A plaque with a thin, inflamed fibrous cap can rupture suddenly, even if it was only causing mild narrowing beforehand, exposing the highly thrombogenic lipid core directly to circulating blood. This triggers the same coagulation cascade covered in this subject's general thrombosis-and-infarction concept, but now happening inside a coronary artery: a clot forms rapidly at the rupture site and can completely occlude the vessel within minutes, with none of the gradual warning a slowly narrowing stable plaque would give. When that occlusion persists long enough, the heart muscle supplied by that artery is deprived of oxygen and begins to die -- a myocardial infarction, the direct cardiac application of the general infarction mechanism this subject has already established. This is precisely why plaque stability, not simply the degree of arterial narrowing, is the real determinant of acute cardiac risk.
