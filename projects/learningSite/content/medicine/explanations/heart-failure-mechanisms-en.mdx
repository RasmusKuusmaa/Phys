Heart failure is defined by an outcome -- the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's metabolic needs -- rather than by a single underlying mechanism, and the two mechanisms that produce that outcome are genuinely different problems requiring genuinely different mental models. Systolic dysfunction is the more intuitive one: the heart muscle itself has become too weak to contract forcefully, so even a normally filled chamber ejects too small a fraction of its blood with each beat. Diastolic dysfunction inverts the problem: the muscle's contractile strength can be entirely normal, but the chamber wall has become too stiff to relax and expand properly between beats, so it never fills with a normal volume of blood in the first place -- normal contraction of an under-filled chamber still produces too little output.

The Frank-Starling relationship is the heart's built-in short-term compensatory mechanism for reduced pumping ability, and it works by a simple mechanical principle: stretching cardiac muscle fibers by filling the chamber with more blood increases the force of the subsequent contraction, up to a point. This is genuinely useful when pumping ability drops modestly -- a somewhat weakened heart can maintain adequate output by operating at a larger filling volume, extracting more force per beat to compensate. But this relationship has a limit. Push the ventricle's filling volume past the optimal point on this curve, and the relationship reverses: the overstretched muscle fibers actually generate less force, not more, turning what was a compensatory mechanism into a contributor to further decline.

The body's other major compensatory pathway compounds the problem rather than solving it. A failing heart producing reduced output triggers the same physiological alarm system that responds to blood loss: the sympathetic nervous system raises heart rate and constricts blood vessels, and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system retains sodium and water to raise blood volume. Both responses make excellent sense as a short-term answer to acute blood loss, where the underlying problem (not enough blood volume) is exactly what they correct. Applied to a chronically failing heart, though, they backfire: a faster heart rate and constricted vessels increase the workload on a muscle that is already struggling, and retained fluid raises the very venous pressure and congestion (fluid backing up into the lungs or the rest of the body) that produces heart failure's classic symptoms. This is why heart failure characteristically worsens progressively once these compensatory mechanisms become dominant -- the body's own short-term-survival machinery, applied to a chronic problem it was never built to solve, actively drives the disease forward.
