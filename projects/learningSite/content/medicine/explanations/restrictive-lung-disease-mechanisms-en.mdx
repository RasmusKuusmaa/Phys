Lung disease can interfere with breathing in two mechanically opposite ways, and restrictive disease represents one extreme of that spectrum: rather than air struggling to flow through narrowed airways (the obstructive pattern), the lung itself simply cannot expand to accommodate a normal volume of air.

The key physical property at stake is compliance -- how much a lung's volume changes for a given amount of pressure applied to expand it. A healthy lung is quite compliant, expanding substantially with only modest pressure from the respiratory muscles. Pulmonary fibrosis, the prototypical restrictive disease, progressively destroys that compliance by scarring and thickening the interstitium, the thin tissue lying between the alveoli that normally allows the lung to stretch easily. As scar tissue accumulates, the lung becomes stiffer, and the same pressure that once produced a large volume change now produces only a small one.

This has a direct consequence for how someone with restrictive disease actually breathes. Generating enough pressure to force a stiff lung to a normal full breath volume would require substantially more respiratory muscle effort than is sustainable breath after breath, so the more efficient adaptation is to take smaller breaths, more frequently -- rapid, shallow breathing, rather than the labored exhalation characteristic of obstructive disease. Where obstructive disease specifically impairs getting air *out* (since narrowed or collapsing airways resist flow most during exhalation), restrictive disease impairs getting air *in* in the first place, by making the lung tissue itself resistant to expanding.

Because these are genuinely different mechanical failures -- one in the airways' resistance to flow, the other in the lung tissue's resistance to stretching -- they show up as different patterns on lung function testing (obstructive disease characteristically reduces the fraction of air that can be exhaled quickly, while restrictive disease reduces total lung volume itself) and require thinking about a completely different anatomical target: airway walls and smooth muscle for obstructive disease, the interstitial tissue between the alveoli for restrictive disease.
