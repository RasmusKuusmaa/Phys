Population genetics shifts the lens from a single family's Punnett square to an entire population's allele and genotype frequencies, and the first tool anyone learns in that field is the Hardy-Weinberg principle -- not because real populations actually behave this way, but because it defines the baseline they're compared against. For a gene with two alleles, call their frequencies p and q (which must sum to 1, since together they account for every copy of that gene in the population). If mating is random and allele frequencies aren't being pushed around by any other force, simple probability says the three genotypes will occur at frequencies p^2 (two copies of the first allele), 2pq (one of each), and q^2 (two copies of the second allele) -- and, remarkably, those frequencies will stay exactly the same generation after generation, with no drift toward one allele or the other, no matter how rare or common either allele starts out.

That stability is conditional on five assumptions holding simultaneously: no new mutations introducing or removing alleles, no migration bringing in or removing alleles, no natural selection favoring one genotype's survival or reproduction over another, an effectively infinite population size (so random chance in who happens to mate with whom doesn't shift frequencies by luck alone), and mating that is random with respect to the gene in question. Every one of these is a real, biologically meaningful force in actual populations, which means every one of them is also a way equilibrium can be broken.

This is exactly why Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium functions as a null hypothesis rather than a description of biological reality: real populations essentially never satisfy all five conditions at once, so the interesting scientific question is never 'is this population in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium' but rather 'how, and by how much, does it deviate, and what does that deviation reveal.' A population geneticist measures a population's actual genotype frequencies, calculates what Hardy-Weinberg would predict from the same allele frequencies, and treats any statistically significant gap between the two as direct evidence that some evolutionary force -- selection, migration, non-random mating, a mutation pressure, or drift in a finite population -- is actively acting on that gene. Far from being an unrealistic simplification with no practical use, Hardy-Weinberg's very unrealism is what makes it useful: it is the still point against which real, moving populations can be measured.
