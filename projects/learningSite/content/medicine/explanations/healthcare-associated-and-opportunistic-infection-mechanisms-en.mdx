Most microbes living on and in the human body are perfectly capable of causing disease -- they simply don't, because a healthy immune system and intact physical barriers keep them contained or hold them in check. An opportunistic infection is what happens when one of those specific controls goes missing: a fungus that intact neutrophils would ordinarily phagocytose and destroy gets a foothold in the lungs once chemotherapy has crashed the patient's neutrophil count, or a bacterium that lives harmlessly on skin, unable to breach the skin's physical barrier under ordinary circumstances, reaches the bloodstream directly the moment a catheter gives it a path past that barrier entirely. Notice that in both cases the organism itself hasn't changed -- what changed is a specific defense the organism was never actually strong enough to overcome on its own.

Hospitals concentrate the conditions that create this pattern. Medical devices -- central venous catheters, urinary catheters, ventilator tubing, surgical implants -- exist by necessity to do things the body's own anatomy doesn't normally allow, which means they inherently breach the skin or mucosal barriers that would otherwise keep colonizing microbes confined to a body surface. Every one of those devices is a standing bypass of a barrier evolution built specifically to keep microbes out of sterile tissue and the bloodstream.

The second half of the hospital-specific picture is antibiotic use. Any environment with concentrated antibiotic use is also a concentrated selective pressure: susceptible bacteria die, and any organism that already happens to carry -- or quickly acquires -- a resistance mechanism survives and reproduces disproportionately. Over time this shifts the resistance profile of the bacterial population circulating in that specific ward or unit, independent of any single patient's own treatment history.

These two mechanisms don't just coexist -- they compound in the same patients. The immunocompromised, device-carrying patient who is most vulnerable to an opportunistic infection in the first place is very often being cared for in the same environment where the circulating bacterial population skews resistant, so the organism that finds its opportunity through a breached barrier or a missing immune defense is also disproportionately likely to be one antibiotics handle poorly. Neither risk factor causes the other, but a hospital environment concentrates both onto the same vulnerable population.
