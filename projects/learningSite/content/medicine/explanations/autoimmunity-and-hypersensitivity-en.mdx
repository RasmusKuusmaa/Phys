Every lymphocyte's antigen receptor is generated by a randomized gene-shuffling process, and that randomness is a double-edged sword: it's precisely what generates enough receptor diversity to recognize almost any foreign antigen, but it also guarantees that some fraction of newly generated lymphocytes will, purely by chance, end up with a receptor that binds one of the body's own proteins. The immune system has genuine safeguards against this. During development in the thymus (for T cells) and bone marrow (for B cells), lymphocytes whose receptors bind too strongly to self-antigens are eliminated before they ever leave those organs -- a process called central tolerance. Some self-reactive cells slip through anyway, so a second layer, peripheral tolerance, actively suppresses or inactivates self-reactive lymphocytes that do make it into general circulation. Autoimmune disease is what happens when both layers fail for a particular self-antigen: self-reactive lymphocytes that should have been eliminated or suppressed instead become active, and they attack the corresponding tissue using the exact same mechanisms (cytotoxic T cell killing, antibody production, inflammation) that would normally be aimed at an actual pathogen. Which tissue gets attacked depends entirely on which self-antigen the escaped lymphocytes happen to target -- pancreatic beta cells in type 1 diabetes, joint tissue in rheumatoid arthritis, myelin in multiple sclerosis -- which is why autoimmune diseases are typically organ- or tissue-specific rather than a diffuse, whole-body immune failure.

Hypersensitivity starts from a completely different place: nothing has gone wrong with self-recognition at all. The immune system correctly identifies its target as foreign, but the response to that foreign target is disproportionate, misdirected in its consequences, or triggered against something that posed no real danger in the first place. Because the underlying trigger and effector mechanism differ so much between different hypersensitivity reactions, immunologists split it into four numbered types rather than treating it as one phenomenon. Type I is what most people mean by "allergy": an antigen (pollen, a food protein, venom) binds IgE antibodies already stuck to the surface of mast cells, and that binding triggers immediate degranulation -- the release of histamine and other mediators stored in the mast cell -- producing symptoms within minutes, ranging from mild itching to life-threatening anaphylaxis. Type II involves antibodies binding directly to antigens on the surface of the body's own cells (or, in a mismatched transfusion, cells from another person), marking them for destruction the same way antibodies would mark a pathogen for destruction -- which is exactly why blood typing matters. Type III involves antibodies binding soluble antigen to form immune complexes that circulate and then deposit in small blood vessels or tissue, where they trigger local inflammation and complement activation, sometimes days after exposure. Type IV is fundamentally different from the other three because it's driven by T cells rather than antibodies at all; instead of a pre-existing antibody triggering an immediate reaction, T cells have to be recruited and activated at the exposure site, which is a genuinely slower process, taking one to three days to fully develop -- exactly the timeline seen in contact dermatitis from poison ivy or in a positive tuberculin skin test.

Keeping autoimmunity and hypersensitivity conceptually separate, and correctly identifying which of the four hypersensitivity types is at play in a given reaction, isn't pedantry -- each involves a genuinely distinct immune pathway, and understanding which pathway is active is what actually explains why symptoms appear on the timescale they do, why one reaction is IgE-driven and life-threatening within minutes while another is T-cell-driven and takes days to develop.
