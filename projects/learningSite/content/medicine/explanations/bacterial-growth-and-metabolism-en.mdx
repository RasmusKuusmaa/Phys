A bacterial growth curve is one of the cleanest, most reproducible patterns in all of biology, and it emerges from a genuinely simple cause: a fixed volume of medium contains a finite amount of nutrients. In the lag phase immediately after inoculation, individual bacteria are metabolically active -- synthesizing enzymes and adjusting their internal chemistry to the new medium -- but the population isn't yet increasing in number, because that adjustment takes real time. Once cells are ready, the population enters the log (exponential) phase: with nutrients still abundant and no meaningful resource competition yet, each bacterium divides into two at a roughly constant time interval (the doubling time), so the population size doubles again and again at that same fixed interval -- 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 -- which is exactly the mathematical definition of exponential growth. This phase cannot continue indefinitely for a simple reason: doubling growth in a fixed volume with finite nutrients will always eventually collide with that limit, typically within a matter of generations rather than an extended period.

As nutrients become scarce and metabolic waste products build up, the population enters the stationary phase: new cells are still being produced, but at a rate now matched almost exactly by the rate other cells are dying, so the total population size levels off rather than declining immediately. Eventually the death phase takes over as nutrient depletion and toxic waste accumulation become severe enough that the death rate exceeds whatever division is still occurring, and the population shrinks. This whole four-phase pattern is a direct, almost mechanical consequence of growing in a closed, finite system -- it is not a special property of any one bacterial species, and the same shape appears whether the organism is a fast-growing species like E. coli (which can double in as little as twenty minutes under ideal lab conditions) or a much slower-growing one, just compressed or stretched along the time axis.

Running alongside this population-level story is a cell-level distinction in how bacteria actually extract energy from nutrients. Aerobic respiration uses oxygen as the final destination for electrons stripped from glucose during a multi-step process, and this arrangement lets a cell extract a large amount of usable energy (as ATP) per glucose molecule. Anaerobic metabolism instead either ferments glucose directly (accepting a much lower energy yield in exchange for not needing oxygen at all) or uses a molecule other than oxygen, such as nitrate or sulfate, as the final electron acceptor -- still extracting real, usable energy, just less of it per glucose molecule than the aerobic pathway manages. Bacterial species differ in how rigidly they're tied to one strategy: obligate aerobes cannot survive without oxygen at all, obligate anaerobes are actively poisoned by it (oxygen's reactive byproducts damage cellular machinery they have no defense against), and facultative anaerobes carry both metabolic pathways and simply use whichever one the current environment supports, defaulting to the more energy-efficient aerobic pathway whenever oxygen happens to be available.
