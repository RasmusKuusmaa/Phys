Skin color ultimately comes down to melanin, a pigment produced inside melanocytes and then transferred into the surrounding keratinocytes that make up most of the epidermis. Because pigmentation depends on several distinct steps -- having melanocytes present, those melanocytes successfully synthesizing melanin, and that melanin being produced in a normal, regulated amount -- pigmentation disorders divide cleanly according to which step actually fails, even when the visible result (a change in skin color) looks superficially similar across conditions.

Vitiligo is a loss-of-pigment condition caused by an autoimmune process: the immune system mistakenly targets and destroys melanocytes themselves within specific patches of skin. Because the pigment-producing cells are gone entirely from those patches, no melanin can be produced there regardless of what else is happening in the skin, which is why vitiligo patches are typically well-demarcated and can slowly expand as the autoimmune process affects adjacent melanocytes over time.

Albinism is also a loss-of-pigment condition, but through an entirely different mechanism: an inherited defect, most commonly in the enzyme tyrosinase, which melanocytes need to actually synthesize melanin from its precursor molecules. In albinism, melanocytes are present and structurally normal -- the cellular machinery for producing pigment is intact except for this specific enzymatic step, which is why albinism is generally present from birth and affects pigmentation uniformly (skin, hair, eyes) rather than in patches, unlike vitiligo's patchy, progressive pattern.

Hyperpigmentation conditions sit at the opposite end mechanistically: rather than a deficiency, melanocytes that are entirely normal and healthy produce excess melanin, usually in response to a trigger like inflammation (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation), hormonal changes, or UV exposure. This is not a disease of the melanocyte itself but a normal cell responding, sometimes excessively, to an external signal -- a mechanistically opposite process from either vitiligo or albinism, even though all three ultimately show up as the same kind of visible change: an alteration in skin color.
