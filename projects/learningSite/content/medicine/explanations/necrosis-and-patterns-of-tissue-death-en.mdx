Apoptosis and necrosis both end in a cell's death, but everything about how they get there is different, and that difference matters enormously for the surrounding tissue. Apoptosis is an orderly, energy-consuming process the cell actively carries out on itself: its own machinery breaks down the DNA and cellular structures inside an intact membrane, and the resulting fragments are neatly packaged and cleared away by neighboring cells without spilling anything or provoking an inflammatory response. Necrosis is the opposite in every respect: it is what happens when an insult -- oxygen deprivation, a toxin, physical trauma -- overwhelms the cell's ability to maintain its own membrane integrity and energy supply. The cell swells uncontrollably as ion pumps fail, its membrane eventually ruptures, and its entire contents, including enzymes normally kept safely contained, spill directly into the surrounding tissue -- which is exactly what triggers the local inflammatory response necrosis is known for.

Because necrosis is a passive process driven by the specific way a cell's environment failed it, the resulting dead tissue looks different depending on what caused the failure, and pathologists use those visual differences diagnostically. Coagulative necrosis is the default pattern for sudden ischemia (blood supply loss) almost everywhere in the body: even as the cells die, a structural scaffold protein resists the digestive enzymes released during cell death long enough that the tissue's basic architecture -- the ghostly outline of its original structure -- remains recognizable for several days. Liquefactive necrosis breaks that pattern in two very different situations that happen to produce the same visual result: bacterial infections, where neutrophils flood in and release enzymes that actively digest the tissue into a liquid mass, and, unusually, ischemic death specifically in the brain, which lacks the structural protein that holds coagulative necrosis together elsewhere and has a very high fat content, so oxygen-starved brain tissue liquefies rather than retaining its shape.

Two further patterns round out the classic set. Caseous necrosis produces a soft, friable, cheese-like dead tissue with no preserved architecture at all -- neither the ghostly outline of coagulative necrosis nor the fully liquid state of liquefactive necrosis -- classically at the center of a tuberculosis granuloma, where the combination of dying macrophages and the bacterium's resistant cell wall produces this distinctive appearance. Fat necrosis is mechanistically unrelated to the other three: it results from lipase enzymes (normally involved in digesting dietary fat) being released into adipose tissue, most classically around an inflamed or injured pancreas, where the released lipases digest nearby fat cells and the resulting free fatty acids react with calcium to form visible, chalky white deposits -- a distinctive enough appearance that finding it on imaging or at autopsy points specifically toward pancreatic disease.
