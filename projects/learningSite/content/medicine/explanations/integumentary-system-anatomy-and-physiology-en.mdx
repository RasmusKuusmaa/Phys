Skin earns its classification as the body's largest organ through the same logic used for any other organ: it's built from multiple distinct tissue layers working together toward shared functions, not a single uniform material. The epidermis, the outermost layer, is a stack of keratinocytes generated continuously at its deepest layer and pushed steadily upward; as they rise they progressively fill with the tough structural protein keratin, flatten, and die, so that the very surface that actually contacts the outside world is a layer of dead, keratin-packed cells rather than living tissue -- a deliberate design that trades cellular life for mechanical toughness and waterproofing exactly where it's needed most. Beneath the epidermis, the dermis is a dense connective-tissue layer carrying blood vessels, the sensory nerve endings that make skin the body's largest sensory surface, and the hair follicles and sweat/sebaceous glands classified as skin's appendages. The hypodermis beneath that anchors skin to underlying muscle and stores energy as subcutaneous fat.

Skin's barrier function depends on the epidermis specifically: its outermost dead-cell layer is embedded in a lipid matrix that keeps water from evaporating out and blocks most microorganisms and chemicals from entering, the same reason a compromised barrier (from a burn, a wound, or a disease like eczema) creates both a fluid-loss and an infection risk simultaneously, not two unrelated problems.

Thermoregulation is one of skin's most actively controlled functions, not a passive side effect of having a surface exposed to the environment. Blood vessels in the dermis can dilate to bring more warm blood near the surface for heat loss, or constrict to conserve heat, both under direct nervous-system control; eccrine sweat glands are triggered the same way, releasing sweat whose evaporation actively carries heat away. Together these two mechanisms let skin serve as the body's primary heat-dissipation organ, working in the opposite direction (vasoconstriction, reduced sweating) when the goal is heat conservation instead.
