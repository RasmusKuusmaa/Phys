Hemostasis depends on two systems working in sequence: platelets, which adhere to an injured vessel wall and aggregate into a soft initial plug within seconds, and the coagulation cascade, a series of enzymatic reactions among clotting factors that converges on thrombin converting fibrinogen into a fibrin mesh, reinforcing that initial platelet plug into a stable clot over minutes. Because these two systems handle different jobs -- immediate plugging versus long-term reinforcement -- a defect in one produces a recognizably different bleeding pattern than a defect in the other.

Clotting-factor deficiencies, of which hemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency) and hemophilia B (factor IX deficiency) are the classic inherited examples, leave platelets fully capable of forming that initial plug, so a minor cut may seem to stop bleeding normally at first. What fails is the fibrin reinforcement: without it, the fragile platelet plug can rebleed hours later, producing bleeding into joints (hemarthrosis) and deep muscle tissue that is delayed relative to the injury and disproportionately deep for how minor the trigger was. Platelet disorders -- thrombocytopenia (too few platelets, from marrow failure, immune destruction, or consumption) or a functional platelet defect -- instead compromise the initial plug itself, producing immediate, superficial bleeding: petechiae (pinpoint skin hemorrhages), easy bruising, and mucosal bleeding from the gums or nose.

Disseminated intravascular coagulation sits apart from both as a single process that produces both problems simultaneously. A severe systemic trigger -- sepsis, major trauma, certain malignancies, obstetric complications -- causes the coagulation cascade to activate throughout the entire microcirculation at once rather than at one localized injury site, forming small clots that obstruct capillaries in multiple organs. This uncontrolled, body-wide clotting consumes platelets and clotting factors faster than the body can replace them, so the same patient who is forming pathological microclots is simultaneously left with too few remaining platelets and clotting factors to control bleeding anywhere else -- a single mechanism producing the clinical picture of both thrombosis and hemorrhage at once, which is what makes DIC diagnostically and therapeutically distinct from either a pure factor deficiency or a pure platelet problem.
