A birth defect is not a single kind of event -- it can arise through any of three mechanistically distinct routes, and telling them apart is central to understanding both cause and prevention. The first route is purely genetic: a chromosomal abnormality (an extra or missing chromosome, a structural rearrangement) or a mutation in a single gene disrupts development because the underlying instructions for building a structure are themselves incorrect, independent of anything in the intrauterine environment -- these are the same categories of genetic disruption covered by this subject's existing genetic-disorders concept.

The second route is purely environmental: a teratogen -- a drug, an infectious agent, ionizing radiation, or an environmental toxin -- reaches the developing embryo or fetus and disrupts a structure's formation from the outside, even when every relevant gene is completely normal. What makes teratogens mechanistically distinctive is their absolute dependence on timing: a given teratogen can typically only disrupt a structure during that structure's own critical window of active formation, so identical exposure at a different point in pregnancy can produce a much milder effect, an entirely different defect, or none at all. This is exactly why the embryonic period -- roughly weeks three through eight of human development, when essentially every major organ system is being newly built -- is the highest-risk window for structural malformations, while the fetal period that follows is comparatively lower-risk for new structural defects, since it is mostly a period of growth and maturation of structures already formed.

The third route is genuinely multifactorial: a genetic susceptibility that, alone, would never produce a defect only manifests as one when combined with a specific environmental trigger, and neither factor is independently sufficient. This is precisely why a population exposed to the identical teratogen shows a range of outcomes -- some pregnancies affected, some entirely unaffected -- rather than a single, uniform result: the genetic background each fetus brings to that exposure genuinely changes the outcome.
