A vaccine's entire mechanism rests on one fact already established elsewhere: the adaptive immune system responds far faster and more effectively to an antigen it has seen before, because the first exposure leaves behind a standing population of memory cells. A vaccine's job is simply to trigger that same memory-forming process safely, without requiring an actual, potentially dangerous infection to be the trigger. Different vaccine platforms achieve this in different ways -- an inactivated vaccine presents a killed pathogen, a live-attenuated vaccine presents a deliberately weakened pathogen still capable of provoking a full immune response but engineered to be incapable of causing serious disease, a subunit vaccine presents an isolated protein fragment with no live pathogen involved at all, and an mRNA vaccine delivers genetic instructions that let the recipient's own cells manufacture one specific pathogen protein temporarily, after which normal cellular machinery degrades the mRNA. Whichever platform is used, the antigen it presents gets captured by antigen-presenting cells and triggers the same clonal-selection process already covered for natural infection, ending in the same outcome: a population of memory B and T cells specific to that antigen, ready to mount a fast secondary-style response.

That mechanism, repeated across a large fraction of a population, is the entirety of what herd immunity is -- there's no separate immunological pathway operating "at the population level." A pathogen's ability to spread through a population depends on finding a chain of susceptible hosts to move through; if immune individuals are common enough, a chance encounter between an infected person and another person is statistically far more likely to hit someone with existing immune memory who clears the infection before it can spread further, breaking the chain. This is a purely statistical/network consequence of many individual immune systems doing what they already do -- it emerges automatically once the fraction of immune individuals crosses a threshold specific to how contagious a given pathogen is, the same way a forest fire needs a minimum density of unburned fuel to keep spreading and will sputter out below that density regardless of how the fuel got sparse.

Immunological memory, whether from natural infection or vaccination, isn't a permanent, all-or-nothing state. Memory cell populations can shrink over time, and antibody levels circulating in the blood (titers) can decline, at rates that differ enormously depending on the specific pathogen and the type of immune response it provokes. Some infections and vaccines leave memory that persists for decades with essentially no measurable decline; others produce a memory response that wanes meaningfully within a few years. This is the actual, mechanistic reason some vaccines include scheduled booster doses and others don't -- a booster isn't a sign the original vaccination failed, it's a scheduled second exposure timed to arrive before memory has waned enough to meaningfully weaken protection, reflecting a known property of that specific pathogen's immune response rather than any flaw in the vaccine platform itself.
