Calling the pituitary the body's "master gland" captures something real -- it does directly control hormone output from the thyroid, adrenal cortex, and gonads -- but it invites the wrong picture if taken to mean the pituitary sits at the top of the chain with nothing above it. The hypothalamus, a small region of the brain sitting immediately above the pituitary, is what actually issues the top-level instructions. It synthesizes small releasing hormones (like TRH, CRH, and GnRH) and sends them not through the general bloodstream but through a dedicated, local vascular shortcut called the hypophyseal portal system, which carries them the short distance directly to the anterior pituitary at a much higher concentration than general circulation would allow. This gives the brain a direct, fast-acting lever over pituitary hormone release, tying endocrine output to whatever the rest of the nervous system is sensing.

The anterior and posterior lobes of the pituitary are really two different organs sharing one gland, with completely different relationships to the hypothalamus above them. The anterior pituitary is a true hormone-synthesizing gland: it manufactures its own tropic hormones -- TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH, growth hormone, and prolactin -- and releases them into the general circulation in response to the hypothalamic releasing hormones it receives, each tropic hormone then acting on one specific downstream target (TSH on the thyroid, ACTH on the adrenal cortex, LH/FSH on the gonads). The posterior pituitary does not synthesize anything at all; oxytocin and antidiuretic hormone are actually made by neurons whose cell bodies sit in the hypothalamus, with their long axons running down into the posterior pituitary, which functions as nothing more than a storage-and-release site at the end of those axons.

Nearly every axis running through this hypothalamic-pituitary system closes with negative feedback: as the downstream gland's hormone (thyroid hormone, cortisol, sex steroids) rises in the blood, it acts back on both the pituitary and the hypothalamus to suppress further release of the hormones that stimulated it, the same self-correcting logic that keeps a thermostat from letting a room's temperature run away in either direction. This is why measuring a pituitary tropic hormone (like TSH) is often a more sensitive way to detect a problem in the downstream gland than measuring the downstream hormone directly -- a struggling thyroid produces less thyroid hormone, which removes the negative feedback and drives TSH up, so an elevated TSH is actually a signal of an underperforming thyroid, not an overactive pituitary.
