Immunodeficiency describes any state in which the immune system can't mount an adequate response, but lumping every case into one category obscures a genuinely important mechanistic split. Primary immunodeficiencies trace back to a specific genetic defect -- a mutation disrupting one particular gene needed somewhere in immune development or function, such as a gene required for T cells or B cells to mature properly, or one needed to assemble a functional antibody, or one needed for phagocytes to actually kill what they've engulfed. Because the defect is genetic, it's present from conception onward, and it typically first becomes apparent in infancy or childhood through a telltale pattern: infections that are unusually frequent, unusually severe, caused by organisms that wouldn't normally cause serious disease in a healthy person, or that keep recurring despite treatment. Secondary immunodeficiencies, despite producing similarly severe susceptibility to infection, arise completely differently: the immune system was built normally, but something external is actively damaging or suppressing it later in life. A virus that specifically infects and destroys immune cells is one well-known cause; deliberately administered immunosuppressive medication (necessary to prevent a transplanted organ from being rejected, or to calm an overactive immune response in autoimmune disease) is another, entirely intentional one; severe malnutrition and certain cancers, particularly those affecting bone marrow or lymphoid tissue, are others. The practical reason this distinction matters is that primary and secondary immunodeficiency call for entirely different approaches -- one is a fixed genetic condition to manage around, the other often has a specific, sometimes reversible external cause to identify and address.

A separate and easily confused phenomenon is pathogen-side immune evasion, where the problem isn't a weakened host immune system at all -- it's a pathogen that has evolved a specific countermeasure against one particular immune mechanism. Antigenic variation is one such countermeasure: some pathogens can alter the specific surface proteins that antibodies and T cell receptors recognize, fast enough that a memory response built against last month's version of the pathogen no longer matches this month's, forcing the adaptive immune system to essentially start over as though encountering a new pathogen entirely. Latency is a different strategy entirely: rather than constantly fighting active immune surveillance, some pathogens shut down almost all of their own gene expression once inside a host cell, entering a dormant state in which they present essentially nothing for the immune system to detect, sometimes for years or decades, before reactivating and resuming active replication under the right conditions. A third category of evasion works even more directly: some pathogens produce molecules that specifically interfere with antigen presentation itself, or block the signaling pathways immune cells use to coordinate a response, disabling the very detection machinery that would otherwise flag them.

What ties these evasion strategies together, and what separates them cleanly from immunodeficiency, is that in every one of these cases the host's immune system is functioning exactly as designed. A recurring infection caused by a pathogen using antigenic variation or latency isn't evidence that something is wrong with the person's immune system -- it's evidence that a particular pathogen has, over evolutionary time, found and exploited a specific gap in how that immune system operates, which is a completely different problem requiring a completely different kind of explanation and, where treatment exists, a completely different kind of intervention.
