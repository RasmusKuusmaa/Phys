Bone looks static once it's finished growing, but it never actually stops changing. Throughout adult life, small teams of osteoclasts continuously resorb tiny volumes of existing bone -- clearing out microdamage before it accumulates into a real crack -- and osteoblasts follow behind, laying down new bone matrix to replace exactly what was removed. In healthy adult bone this cycle is tightly coupled: resorption and formation stay roughly matched, so total bone mass holds steady even though the specific bone tissue present at any moment is constantly being replaced.

Osteoporosis is what happens when that coupling breaks, tipping the balance toward net resorption. The single most common cause is the drop in estrogen at menopause: estrogen normally restrains osteoclast activity, and its withdrawal lets resorption outpace formation for years afterward, which is why postmenopausal women bear the largest share of osteoporosis cases. But estrogen loss is not the only route to the same imbalance. Chronically elevated parathyroid hormone (from a parathyroid gland disorder) directly stimulates osteoclasts to resorb bone in order to raise blood calcium, and if that stimulus persists, the skeleton pays the price for maintaining calcium homeostasis elsewhere in the body. Long-term glucocorticoid medication suppresses osteoblast activity directly. And bone that isn't regularly mechanically loaded -- during prolonged bed rest, spinal cord injury, or even extended spaceflight -- loses density because the remodeling cycle actually responds to mechanical stress as a formation signal, so removing that stress removes part of what normally keeps formation matched to resorption.

Whatever the specific trigger, the mechanistic outcome is the same: less bone, with a degraded internal microarchitecture (thinner trabeculae, more perforations in the internal scaffolding), even though the bone tissue that remains is chemically and structurally normal at the cellular level -- it's simply present in a reduced, weaker-architecture quantity. This is exactly why osteoporosis produces no direct symptom of its own before a fracture: there's no pain signal for "bone density is declining," only for the eventual consequence, a fracture from a fall or load that a denser, better-architected skeleton would have absorbed without incident.
