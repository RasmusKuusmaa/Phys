Anatomically, the nervous system splits cleanly into two parts, and the split matters because the two parts do genuinely different jobs. The central nervous system -- brain and spinal cord, both encased in bone (skull and vertebral column) and bathed in protective cerebrospinal fluid -- is where information actually gets processed and decisions actually get made. The peripheral nervous system is everything else: every nerve running from the spinal cord and brainstem out to the skin, muscles, and organs. Sensory (afferent) peripheral nerves carry information inward, from receptors in the skin, muscles, and organs toward the central nervous system; motor (efferent) peripheral nerves carry commands outward, from the central nervous system to the muscles and glands that actually execute a response. Without the peripheral half of this system, the central nervous system would be an isolated processor with no input and no way to act on its output -- which is exactly the physiological problem in a peripheral nerve injury: the spinal cord and brain remain intact and functional, but the specific channel connecting them to a limb can be cut off.

Both halves of the system do their work using the same basic cell, the neuron. A neuron has a cell body that contains its nucleus and does its metabolic housekeeping, and a long thin projection called an axon that carries an electrical impulse -- a rapid, self-propagating change in the cell's membrane voltage -- away from the cell body, sometimes over a distance of a meter or more (the longest axons in the human body, running from the spinal cord to the toes, are among the longest single cells in nature).

That electrical impulse cannot simply leap across to the next neuron, because neurons are not physically fused together. Where one neuron's axon terminal approaches the next neuron, a tiny gap called the synaptic cleft separates them, and crossing it requires switching signal types entirely. The electrical impulse arriving at the axon terminal triggers the release of neurotransmitter molecules into that gap; those molecules diffuse across and bind specific receptor proteins on the receiving neuron's membrane. What happens next depends entirely on which neurotransmitter bound which receptor -- some combinations make the receiving neuron more likely to fire its own impulse (excitatory), and others make it less likely (inhibitory). A single neuron in the brain routinely receives thousands of such synaptic inputs simultaneously, some excitatory and some inhibitory, and effectively sums them to determine whether it fires -- which is the basic computational operation underlying everything the nervous system does, from a simple reflex arc to, in aggregate across billions of neurons, thought itself. This overview deliberately stops here: how specific circuits of neurons implement vision, memory, movement planning, or mood, and what goes wrong in specific neurological and psychiatric conditions, is the proper subject of a dedicated neuroscience module rather than an anatomy-and-physiology overview.
