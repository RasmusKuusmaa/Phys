An epidemic curve -- new cases plotted against time -- is one of the first diagnostic tools an outbreak investigator reaches for, because its shape alone carries information about how the outbreak is spreading. A single, sharp peak that rises and falls within one incubation period strongly suggests a common-source outbreak: everyone was exposed to the same contaminated source (a shared meal, a single water supply) at roughly the same time, and the cases cluster tightly around one incubation-period-later peak before dropping off once the source is removed or exhausted. A series of successive waves, each larger than the last, points to something structurally different: ongoing transmission from person to person, where each generation of cases seeds the next, producing a curve that climbs in a step-like or exponential pattern rather than a single bump.

The basic reproduction number, R0, is the single quantity that most determines how that second kind of outbreak behaves. It is defined as the average number of secondary infections one infectious person produces in a population with no prior immunity and no control measures -- a purely hypothetical baseline scenario. If R0 is above 1, each generation of infection is larger than the last, and the outbreak grows exponentially until something (immunity, behavior change, running out of susceptible people) slows it down. If R0 is below 1, each generation is smaller than the last, and the outbreak sputters out on its own even without intervention.

The crucial insight that separates a sophisticated understanding of R0 from a naive one is that R0 is not purely a property of the pathogen -- it is a property of the pathogen interacting with a specific population and context. The same virus can have a very different R0 in a densely packed city with poor sanitation than in a sparsely populated region with good hygiene practices, because R0 depends on how often infectious and susceptible people actually come into contact, and what fraction of those contacts result in transmission. This is precisely why non-pharmaceutical interventions -- reducing contact rates through distancing, improving hygiene to lower the transmission probability per contact -- can drive the effective reproduction number below 1 without altering the pathogen itself in any way.

The herd immunity threshold formalizes this relationship. If a certain fraction of the population is already immune (through prior infection or vaccination), an infectious person effectively "wastes" some of their contacts on people who can't be infected, lowering the effective reproduction number below R0. The fraction of the population that needs to be immune to push transmission reliably below the growth threshold is given by 1 minus the reciprocal of R0: a highly transmissible pathogen with a large R0 requires a correspondingly larger immune fraction to stop its spread than a less transmissible one, which is why different diseases are quoted with very different herd immunity targets.
