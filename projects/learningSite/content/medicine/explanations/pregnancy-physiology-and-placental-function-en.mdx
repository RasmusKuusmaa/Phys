Implantation, when the early embryo burrows into the uterine lining about a week after fertilization, sets off the formation of the placenta -- and it's worth being precise about what the placenta actually is: not simply a patch of maternal tissue the embryo attaches to, but a genuinely new organ built from both fetal tissue (the trophoblast layer surrounding the early embryo) and maternal tissue (the endometrium) working together, existing only for the duration of the pregnancy and then being delivered afterward.

One of the placenta's first jobs is hormonal rescue. Left alone, the corpus luteum that formed after ovulation has a fixed lifespan of about two weeks before it degrades, which would end the pregnancy by cutting off progesterone. The early placenta prevents this by producing human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), a hormone that signals the corpus luteum to keep producing progesterone well past its normal lifespan -- hCG is, not coincidentally, exactly what a home pregnancy test detects. This arrangement is temporary: by roughly the end of the first trimester, the placenta itself has matured enough to take over as the dominant source of both progesterone and estrogen directly, a genuine handoff from one hormone-producing structure to another partway through pregnancy.

The placenta's other central job is physical exchange, and here the key fact is what does NOT happen: maternal and fetal blood never mix directly. Instead, maternal blood pools in intervillous spaces surrounding tiny fetal blood vessels contained within placental villi, and oxygen, nutrients, and waste products cross a thin membrane separating the two circulations by diffusion and active transport, not by the two blood supplies merging. This separation is what allows the fetus to have a completely different blood type from the mother without a problem in most pregnancies, though it isn't a perfect barrier -- some substances, including certain infections, drugs, and (in Rh-incompatible pregnancies) small amounts of fetal blood cells, can cross it.

Pregnancy's physiologic demands extend well past the uterus and placenta. Maternal blood volume expands by roughly 40-50% to support placental blood flow, the heart's cardiac output rises to move that extra volume, and both breathing rate and metabolic rate increase to meet the combined oxygen and energy demands of mother and fetus together. These aren't side effects to be corrected -- they're necessary physiological adaptations, and they're also the reason several standard lab reference ranges (for things like hemoglobin concentration, since the same red cell mass is now diluted in more plasma) are genuinely different, and should be interpreted differently, during pregnancy than outside of it.
