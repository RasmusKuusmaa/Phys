Not every muscle in the body works the same way, because not every muscle has the same job. Skeletal muscle -- the kind attached to bone by tendons, striated in appearance under a microscope, and under conscious voluntary control -- exists to move the skeleton deliberately: walking, gripping, chewing, blinking. Cardiac muscle exists in exactly one place, the heart, and is built to contract rhythmically and involuntarily for an entire lifetime without ever truly fatiguing the way skeletal muscle does after sustained effort. Smooth muscle lines the walls of hollow organs and blood vessels -- the gut, the bladder, the arteries -- and contracts slowly and involuntarily, moving material along the digestive tract or narrowing and widening a vessel's diameter to redirect blood flow. All three share the same underlying molecular contraction machinery, but their control (voluntary vs. involuntary), speed, and fatigue resistance are tuned to entirely different jobs.

A muscle's mechanical limitation shapes how the whole musculoskeletal system is organized: a muscle can only actively shorten and pull on whatever it's attached to, never actively lengthen and push. This is why skeletal muscles almost always come in antagonistic pairs spanning a joint. At the elbow, the biceps brachii contracts to flex the joint (bringing the forearm toward the shoulder), and the triceps brachii contracts to extend it back out -- each muscle only ever pulls, and the joint's two-way motion comes from alternating which muscle in the pair is doing the pulling, not from either muscle reversing its own action.

That pulling happens, at the level of a single muscle fiber, through the sliding filament mechanism. Each fiber is packed with repeating contractile units called sarcomeres, built from two kinds of protein filament: thin filaments made of actin, and thick filaments made of myosin. When a nerve signal triggers contraction, myosin heads projecting from the thick filaments repeatedly bind to the thin filaments, pull them a short distance, release, and rebind further along -- a ratcheting cycle powered by ATP hydrolysis, much like a rope being hauled in hand over hand. Crucially, neither the actin nor the myosin filament itself gets shorter during this process; what changes is how far the two sets of filaments overlap. As myosin heads ratchet the actin filaments inward, the sarcomere's overall length shrinks even though its component filaments stay the same length -- and because a whole muscle fiber is simply many sarcomeres in series, and a whole muscle is simply many fibers in parallel, this microscopic sliding scales up directly into the macroscopic shortening you feel when a muscle contracts.
