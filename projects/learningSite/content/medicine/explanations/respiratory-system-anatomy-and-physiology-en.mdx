Air entering through the nose or mouth travels a single path before it can do anything useful: pharynx, larynx (home of the vocal cords), then the trachea, which splits into a left and right main bronchus, one per lung, and from there branches again and again -- lobar bronchi, segmental bronchi, progressively smaller bronchioles -- roughly twenty-three times before terminating in clusters of alveoli. This is not incidental plumbing; it is the entire point of the airway's structure. Gas exchange can only happen across a thin membrane, and the rate of exchange scales with surface area, so the branching tree exists specifically to pack an enormous exchange surface -- on the order of 70 square meters, roughly the area of a tennis court -- into a chest cavity that could never hold a single sac of equivalent area.

At each alveolus, gas exchange itself needs no pump or active machinery at all: it is pure diffusion. Fresh air arriving in the alveolus has a higher concentration of oxygen than the blood arriving in the surrounding capillaries, so oxygen diffuses across the thin alveolar-capillary membrane into the blood; that same blood arrives with a higher concentration of carbon dioxide than the alveolar air, so carbon dioxide diffuses the other way, out into the alveolus to be exhaled. Both movements happen simultaneously, driven by nothing more than each gas's own concentration difference across an extremely thin (about one cell layer on each side) membrane -- moving air in and out, and blood past the alveoli, is the entire mechanical job of breathing; the exchange itself just happens on its own once the gradients exist.

Maintaining those gradients efficiently requires something less obvious: airflow and blood flow have to be matched at the level of each individual alveolus, a relationship called ventilation-perfusion matching. An alveolus that receives generous airflow but only a trickle of blood can't transfer much oxygen overall, no matter how fresh its air is, because there's little blood available to carry that oxygen away; an alveolus that receives generous blood flow but little air is in the mirror-image situation. The lung continuously adjusts for local mismatches -- for instance, constricting blood vessels serving poorly ventilated alveoli, redirecting blood toward alveoli that are actually getting air -- to keep the whole organ's gas exchange as efficient as the sum of its many small units allows.

Finally, what actually triggers each breath is a common point of confusion. It would seem natural to assume the body monitors oxygen and breathes when oxygen runs low, the way a fuel gauge triggers a warning light. In fact the primary driver is the opposite gas: chemoreceptors (in the brainstem and in the carotid arteries) are highly sensitive to rising carbon dioxide levels in the blood, and it's CO2 buildup, not falling oxygen, that produces the urgent, uncomfortable drive to breathe. This is exactly why breath-holding becomes unbearable within roughly a minute, well before oxygen has dropped anywhere near a dangerous level -- and it's also why deliberately hyperventilating before holding your breath (which flushes out CO2 without meaningfully raising oxygen) is dangerous: it delays the CO2-driven urge to breathe without addressing the oxygen level that's actually falling underneath it, which is how experienced swimmers have drowned attempting exactly this trick.
