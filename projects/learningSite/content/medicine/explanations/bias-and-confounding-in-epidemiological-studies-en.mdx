Bias and confounding both make a study's result untrustworthy, but they are fundamentally different problems with fundamentally different fixes, and conflating them leads to trying to solve one with a tool meant for the other. Bias lives entirely inside the study itself -- it is a flaw in who was selected, how data was collected, or how outcomes were measured. Selection bias means the study's participants (or the split between its comparison groups) don't represent the population the researchers actually want to draw conclusions about -- for instance, a study recruiting participants from a hospital's cardiology ward will systematically overrepresent people with heart-related risk factors compared to the general population. Recall bias specifically affects studies that ask people to remember past exposures: someone who has a disease has often turned that history over in their mind repeatedly, searching for a cause, while a healthy control has no comparable reason to have thought hard about, say, what pesticides they used twenty years ago. Both kinds of bias share a defining feature: running the exact same flawed study design again, even with a much larger sample, would reproduce the same systematic distortion, because the problem is in the method, not the sample size.

Confounding is a completely different kind of problem, because it isn't a flaw in the study at all -- it's a true feature of how variables are related in the real world that the study failed to account for. A confounder is a third variable that is genuinely associated with both the exposure being studied and the outcome, without being a step on the causal pathway between them. The lighter-and-lung-cancer example makes this vivid: nobody seriously believes carrying a lighter causes cancer, but smokers are much more likely to carry lighters (to light cigarettes) and are, entirely separately, much more likely to develop lung cancer, so in any dataset that doesn't account for smoking status, lighter-carrying will show up statistically associated with lung cancer even though there is no direct causal arrow between them at all. Unlike bias, confounding can be addressed even after data collection -- through statistical adjustment (measuring the confounder and controlling for it mathematically) or, better, through study design choices like randomization, which (in an RCT) balances confounders, known and unknown, between groups automatically.

Effect modification is worth distinguishing from both, because it is neither an error to fix nor a distortion to adjust away -- it is a real, correctly measured phenomenon in which an exposure's true effect genuinely differs across subgroups. A blood pressure medication, for instance, might genuinely lower blood pressure more effectively in younger patients than in elderly ones due to real physiological differences between the groups. A good study identifies and reports this kind of effect modification as a substantive finding in its own right, since it tells clinicians something true and useful about who the treatment helps most -- treating it as a bias or confounding problem to be corrected away would throw out a real signal by mistaking it for noise.
