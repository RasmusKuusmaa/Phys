Every neoplasm begins the same way: a group of cells escapes the normal signals that regulate how often and how much a cell should divide. What happens after that escape is what pathology's most consequential classification is built around. A benign neoplasm still respects most of the rules a normal tissue lives by -- its cells closely resemble the tissue they came from, it grows slowly, and critically, it stays contained behind a defined boundary (often a fibrous capsule) rather than invading neighboring structures. A malignant neoplasm -- cancer -- breaks those remaining rules: its cells often look less like their tissue of origin (a property called differentiation, with poorly differentiated tumors generally behaving more aggressively), it grows faster and less predictably, and it invades directly into surrounding tissue rather than staying confined. The single capability that matters most clinically is metastasis: cells breaking away from the primary tumor, entering the bloodstream or lymphatic system, and establishing new, independently growing tumors at distant sites -- the reason a cancer's location of origin often matters less for outcome than whether it has already spread.

No single genetic change produces this whole picture at once. The now-standard framework for thinking about malignant transformation describes it as the sequential accumulation of several independent capabilities, each of which a normal cell's own safeguards would otherwise block. A cell has to learn to generate its own growth signals rather than waiting for external cues; it has to become deaf to the anti-growth signals that would otherwise halt its cycle; it has to disable the apoptosis machinery that would otherwise trigger its own destruction in response to the DNA damage a nascent cancer cell typically carries; it has to bypass the normal limit on how many times a cell can divide (most normal cells senesce after a set number of divisions; cancer cells reactivate telomerase or another mechanism to keep dividing indefinitely); and, later in a tumor's evolution, some of its cells acquire the additional, distinct capability to invade local tissue and metastasize. Because each of these is a separate hurdle, a cell typically needs several independent mutations, acquired over years, to clear all of them -- which is exactly why cancer risk rises steeply with age and why most cancers develop slowly rather than appearing from a single mutagenic event.

The genes whose mutation drives this process fall into two classes defined by their opposite normal roles. Proto-oncogenes are normal genes that promote cell growth and division under appropriate conditions; a mutation that locks one into permanent activation turns it into an oncogene, a growth signal stuck in the "on" position regardless of what the cell's actual circumstances call for -- and because this is a gain-of-function change, a mutation in just one of the cell's two copies of the gene is often enough to drive abnormal signaling. Tumor suppressor genes run in the opposite direction: they normally restrain the cell cycle or trigger apoptosis when they detect DNA damage, acting as a brake rather than an accelerator. Disabling a brake generally takes more than damaging one copy, since the other copy can usually still do the job -- which is why cancer typically requires both copies of a given tumor suppressor gene to be lost or inactivated before its restraining function disappears entirely, a pattern (sometimes called the "two-hit" requirement) that is one of the clearest genetic signatures distinguishing tumor suppressor loss from oncogene activation.
