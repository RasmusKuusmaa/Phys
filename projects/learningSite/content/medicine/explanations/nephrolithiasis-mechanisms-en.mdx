Ordinary chemistry would predict that urine, given how much dissolved calcium, oxalate, and uric acid it routinely carries, should crystallize constantly -- these minerals are frequently present at concentrations that exceed their solubility in pure water. That crystallization does not happen routinely because urine is not pure water; it contains a range of other constituents (citrate is a well-studied example) that act as inhibitors, physically and chemically interfering with the nucleation and growth of mineral crystals even when the raw concentration of a given mineral would otherwise favor crystallization. Kidney stone formation is fundamentally a story about this balance tipping, not simply about a mineral being present.

The balance can tip from either direction. Rising mineral concentration is the more intuitive route: dehydration concentrates urine generally, a diet especially high in a particular mineral increases its urinary load, and certain metabolic conditions cause the kidney to excrete more of a given substance than normal, any of which can push a mineral's concentration past what the available inhibitors can manage. Equally important, though less obvious, is reduced inhibitor activity itself -- if the substances that normally suppress crystallization are present in lower amounts or working less effectively, a stone can form even without any unusual rise in mineral concentration.

Once a small crystal nucleus does form, it grows through an entirely ordinary physical process: supersaturated urine flowing past the nucleus continues depositing more of the same mineral onto it, the same nucleation-and-growth process that governs crystallization in any supersaturated solution, biological or otherwise. This is why stones, once started, tend to keep growing rather than simply staying at their initial microscopic size.

Different stone types reflect genuinely different underlying chemistry rather than one universal mechanism playing out identically each time. Calcium oxalate stones, the most common type by far, form under the calcium/oxalate supersaturation dynamics just described. Uric acid stones form under a chemically distinct condition -- most importantly, a persistently low (acidic) urine pH, since uric acid's solubility depends heavily on pH -- and are driven by different dietary and metabolic contributors than calcium oxalate stones. Recognizing which specific chemistry underlies a given stone matters precisely because the contributing factors, and therefore what actually shifts the underlying balance back toward solubility, genuinely differ between stone types.
