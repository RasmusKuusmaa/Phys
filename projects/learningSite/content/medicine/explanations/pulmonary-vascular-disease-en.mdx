The pulmonary vasculature sits in a mechanically unusual position: it is the only place in the body where the entire output of one side of the heart (the right ventricle) must pass through before reaching its destination, which means a problem obstructing that vasculature affects far more than the lung tissue immediately downstream of the blockage.

A pulmonary embolism begins, in the overwhelming majority of cases, as a blood clot forming somewhere else entirely -- typically a deep vein of the leg or pelvis, the deep vein thrombosis already implied by this subject's general thrombosis-embolism-and-infarction concept. When a piece of that clot breaks free, it travels with venous blood flow back to the right side of the heart and out into the pulmonary arteries, where its size determines which vessel it eventually lodges in and, in turn, how much lung tissue and how much of the right ventricle's outflow tract is affected.

The regional consequence is a specific kind of ventilation-perfusion mismatch: the alveoli downstream of the blockage are still being ventilated with air (nothing has happened to the airways), but they are no longer receiving blood flow, so that ventilation is wasted -- a dead-space pattern, mechanistically the mirror image of the shunt-like mismatch produced when pneumonia fills alveoli with fluid but leaves their blood supply intact.

The more dangerous consequence, when the embolism is large, is entirely separate from gas exchange. Because all of the right ventricle's output has to pass through the pulmonary circulation, a large enough clot creates a sudden, severe increase in the resistance the right ventricle must pump against -- essentially an acute pressure overload imposed all at once, rather than developing gradually. A right ventricle that has never had to generate this much pressure can fail outright under that sudden demand, producing a form of circulatory collapse that is mechanistically much closer to the obstructive shock covered in this subject's shock-and-circulatory-failure concept (a physical blockage to flow) than to an ordinary respiratory problem.

Pulmonary hypertension represents the same underlying category of problem -- resistance in the pulmonary vasculature that the right ventricle must overcome -- playing out on a chronic rather than acute timescale. Rather than one sudden blockage, the pulmonary arteries themselves narrow and stiffen progressively, whether from repeated small emboli, chronic lung disease damaging the vascular bed, or other causes. The right ventricle responds to this chronic pressure overload exactly the way any heart chamber responds to sustained pressure overload -- by thickening its wall (hypertrophy), the same adaptation already covered for the left ventricle's response to conditions like aortic stenosis, just occurring on the right side of the heart in response to a vascular problem rather than a valve problem.
