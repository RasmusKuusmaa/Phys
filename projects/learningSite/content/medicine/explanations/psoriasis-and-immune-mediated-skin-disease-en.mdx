Psoriasis plaques look, to an untrained eye, similar to many other inflamed, scaly skin conditions, but the mechanism producing them is specific and different from something like eczema. The trigger is immunological: T cells become inappropriately activated against the skin, releasing a cascade of inflammatory cytokines centered on the IL-23/IL-17 signaling axis. Those cytokines instruct keratinocytes to proliferate dramatically faster than normal -- turnover that would normally take roughly a month compresses to a matter of days. New cells arrive at the surface faster than the normal shedding process can remove them, and they pile up as the thick, silvery-scaled, sharply demarcated plaques that are psoriasis's hallmark.

This immune-driven mechanism is why psoriasis is not simply a local skin problem. The same overactive inflammatory pathway that drives skin plaques can also target joints, producing psoriatic arthritis in a meaningful fraction of patients, and psoriasis is associated with broader systemic inflammatory risk beyond the skin and joints. That systemic connection is the single biggest mechanistic difference from a barrier-dysfunction condition like eczema, where the primary lesion begins with a structural gap in the skin itself rather than a circulating immune signal.

Because the driving problem is immune signaling rather than a structural skin defect, psoriasis treatments that specifically target the IL-23/IL-17 pathway are far more directly aimed at the disease's actual mechanism than a barrier-repair strategy would be -- the reverse of what works best for eczema, which is exactly why distinguishing the two mechanistically, not just by appearance, matters.
