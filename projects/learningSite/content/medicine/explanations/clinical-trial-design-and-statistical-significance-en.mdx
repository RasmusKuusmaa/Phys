A well-designed clinical trial defends against bias with two separable tools that are easy to conflate but address different vulnerabilities. Randomization happens at the moment participants enter the trial: assigning them to treatment or control purely by chance means that, on average, the two groups start out balanced on every characteristic that might affect the outcome, whether or not the researchers thought to measure it. This is what allows a later difference in outcome to be attributed to the treatment rather than to some pre-existing difference between the groups.

Blinding addresses a completely different vulnerability that persists after randomization: the psychology of knowing who got what. If participants know they received the active drug rather than a placebo, they may report their symptoms differently simply from that expectation (or the reverse, if they resent being in the "boring" control group). If the researchers assessing outcomes know which participants received treatment, their judgment of ambiguous outcomes -- did this X-ray improve, is this pain reduced -- can shift accordingly, even unconsciously and with the best of intentions. A single-blind trial hides the assignment from participants only; a double-blind trial hides it from both participants and the assessors evaluating results, and is considered stronger evidence precisely because it closes both of these channels for distortion at once.

Once a trial is run, its result is usually summarized by a p-value: the probability of observing a difference at least this large between groups, if the treatment genuinely had zero effect. A p-value below the conventional (but ultimately arbitrary) 0.05 threshold is called "statistically significant," meaning the observed difference is unlikely to be a fluke of random sampling. A confidence interval adds information a bare p-value doesn't provide: a range of plausible values for the true effect size, whose width reflects how precisely the trial measured that effect -- a narrow interval reflects a precise measurement, a wide one reflects genuine uncertainty even if the result happens to be statistically significant.

The single most important thing to hold onto is that statistical significance answers only "is this difference probably real, or could it be chance?" -- it says nothing about whether the difference is big enough to matter to a patient. A trial with tens of thousands of participants can detect and declare significant an effect so small it would never change a treatment decision, while a promising drug tested in only fifty patients can fail to reach significance for a real, clinically meaningful effect simply because the trial lacked the statistical power -- driven overwhelmingly by sample size -- to reliably distinguish a true effect of that size from random noise.
