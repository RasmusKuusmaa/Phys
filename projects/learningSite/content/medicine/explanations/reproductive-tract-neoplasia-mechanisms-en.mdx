It's tempting to treat 'reproductive-tract cancer' as one category with one general cancer-causing story, but the mechanisms driving cancer in different reproductive organs are genuinely different from each other, and knowing which mechanism applies is what makes a specific prevention strategy make sense or not.

Cervical cancer stands out because its primary cause is unusually well-established and specific: persistent infection with one of a handful of high-risk strains of human papillomavirus (HPV). HPV's E6 and E7 viral proteins directly interfere with the cell's own tumor-suppressor proteins (p53 and Rb), disabling the normal cell-cycle checkpoints that would otherwise stop an abnormally dividing cell -- the same checkpoint machinery this subject's cell-cycle content covers, here being actively sabotaged by a virus rather than failing on its own. Because the cause is a specific, vaccine-preventable infection, HPV vaccination before likely exposure is an unusually direct and effective cervical-cancer-prevention strategy, a genuine rarity among cancer types.

Endometrial cancer works through a completely different mechanism: unopposed estrogen exposure. Estrogen normally stimulates the endometrium to grow during the first half of the menstrual cycle, and progesterone (rising in the luteal phase) counterbalances that growth signal and prepares the tissue to be shed if pregnancy doesn't occur. When something disrupts that balance -- anovulatory cycles that never produce a progesterone-secreting corpus luteum, obesity (since fat tissue itself is a meaningful estrogen-producing tissue), or long-term estrogen-only hormone therapy without a progestin -- the endometrium keeps receiving a growth signal with nothing to oppose it, and that sustained unopposed proliferation is what drives increased cancer risk over time. This is not simply "too much estrogen" in an absolute sense; a normal menstrual cycle involves plenty of estrogen exposure that isn't linked to elevated risk, precisely because progesterone is there to balance it during the second half of each cycle.

Ovarian cancer's most common form (epithelial ovarian cancer) has a more heterogeneous mechanism, but a notable piece of it is linked to lifetime ovulation number: each ovulation event ruptures the ovary's surface and requires a small tissue-repair process, and the cumulative mutation risk from repeated rounds of this repair process over many ovulatory cycles appears to meaningfully contribute to risk over a lifetime -- part of why factors that reduce total lifetime ovulations (like pregnancy or hormonal contraception) are associated with reduced risk. A meaningful minority of ovarian cancers instead trace to inherited BRCA1/2 mutations, a genetic mechanism distinct from the cumulative-ovulation story.

Male reproductive-tract cancers follow yet other mechanisms. Testicular cancer arises from the germ cells themselves (the cells that would otherwise become sperm), and unusually for a solid tumor, it tends to affect younger men rather than being predominantly a disease of older age. Prostate cancer's growth, by contrast, typically (though not universally) remains dependent on androgen signaling, which is the mechanistic basis for androgen-deprivation as a treatment approach in advanced disease. Recognizing which of these genuinely different mechanisms -- infectious, hormonal, cumulative-mutation/hereditary, or androgen-dependent -- actually applies to a given reproductive-tract cancer is what determines whether vaccination, hormone modulation, genetic screening, or androgen blockade is the intervention that actually targets the underlying cause.
