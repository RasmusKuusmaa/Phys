A common intuition treats the brain's wiring as something fixed once development finishes, but the cellular evidence points the other way: synapses are constantly adjusting their own strength based on how they're actually used, throughout life. The best-characterized mechanism for this is long-term potentiation. When a synapse experiences strong, repeated activation in a short window, the postsynaptic cell responds by trafficking additional neurotransmitter receptors into that specific synapse and, with enough repetition, can trigger genuinely structural changes -- new dendritic spines, enlarged synaptic contacts. The result is a synapse that produces a measurably bigger response to the same presynaptic signal than it did before, and this strengthening is durable, lasting from hours in an isolated brain slice to a lifetime in an intact circuit reinforced by ongoing use.

This process is often summarized with the phrase 'cells that fire together, wire together' (Hebb's rule): a synapse that's reliably active just before its postsynaptic neuron fires gets strengthened, essentially encoding the fact that this input reliably predicts that outcome. Synapses that fire independently of the postsynaptic cell's activity, by contrast, tend to weaken over time (long-term depression) -- the two processes together sculpt a circuit's connection-strength pattern to reflect which inputs have actually been informative, rather than leaving every connection at some fixed default strength. Because millions of synapses can each be independently strengthened or weakened, a specific memory or skill is thought to be encoded as a specific, distributed pattern of connection strengths across a circuit, not stored in any single neuron.

Plasticity isn't uniform across the lifespan. Development includes critical periods where certain circuits (classically, the visual system) are unusually sensitive to experience and can be shaped dramatically by it, and that heightened sensitivity narrows with age. But the adult brain doesn't lose plasticity entirely -- it retains a real, if reduced, capacity for synaptic strengthening and weakening for life, which is the mechanism underlying adult learning, skill acquisition, and the partial functional recovery sometimes seen after brain injury. What's largely absent in the adult human brain is neurogenesis on a scale that could simply regrow lost tissue -- meaningful ongoing neuron generation in adults is limited to a small number of specific regions, not a general-purpose repair mechanism, so recovery after injury relies overwhelmingly on existing surviving circuits rewiring around the damage rather than new neurons replacing what was lost.
