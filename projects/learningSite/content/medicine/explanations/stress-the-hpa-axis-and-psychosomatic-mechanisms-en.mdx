The HPA axis's job is to mount a coordinated hormonal response to a threat, and it does this identically regardless of whether the threat is physical (an injury, an infection) or purely psychological (a work deadline, a social conflict) -- the hypothalamus doesn't distinguish the source, only that a threat has been perceived. It releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), which travels to the anterior pituitary and triggers release of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) into the bloodstream, which in turn travels to the adrenal cortex and stimulates cortisol release. This entire cascade, from perceived threat to circulating cortisol, typically takes minutes -- a genuinely fast, coordinated hormonal response.

Critically, the system is designed with a built-in shutoff: circulating cortisol acts back on both the hypothalamus and pituitary (and on a brain region called the hippocampus, which is rich in cortisol receptors) to suppress further CRH and ACTH release. This negative feedback loop is what makes an acute stress response self-limiting -- once the stressor passes, cortisol falls, the suppressive signal fades, and the system returns to its normal baseline rhythm (cortisol itself has a normal daily rhythm even at rest, peaking in the morning and falling through the day). An acute stress response, functioning as designed, is not harmful; it's an adaptive, temporary reallocation of the body's resources toward dealing with a threat.

Chronic psychological stress breaks this self-limiting property. Sustained, repeated activation of the axis appears to blunt the sensitivity of the very glucocorticoid receptors responsible for the negative feedback -- essentially, the "off switch" becomes less responsive the more it's used under chronic stress, so cortisol stays elevated for far longer than the brief, adaptive version would. This prolonged elevation is where the genuine physical harm comes from, through several traceable downstream pathways: cortisol at chronically high levels suppresses specific populations of immune cells, measurably impairing certain aspects of immune function; it promotes the redistribution of fat toward the abdomen and interferes with normal insulin signaling, contributing to insulin resistance over time; and through effects on blood vessel function and blood pressure regulation, it contributes to elevated cardiovascular risk.

This entire chain -- from a psychological stressor, through a specific hormonal cascade, to specific, measurable effects in the immune, metabolic, and cardiovascular systems -- is the actual mechanism behind what's meant by "psychosomatic" illness in a clinical, mechanistic sense. It is not a claim that the symptoms are imagined; it's the opposite claim, that a real biochemical pathway genuinely connects a psychological state to genuine physical disease risk in specific organ systems, each step traceable and each effect measurable.
