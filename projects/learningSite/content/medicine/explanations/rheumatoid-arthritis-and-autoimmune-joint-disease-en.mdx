Every joint's interior is lined by a thin membrane called the synovium, whose normal job is producing the lubricating synovial fluid a joint needs to move smoothly. In rheumatoid arthritis, the same breakdown of self-tolerance that underlies autoimmune disease generally targets this membrane specifically: self-reactive T cells and B cells infiltrate the synovium, and the resulting chronic inflammation causes the once-thin membrane to thicken dramatically into an abnormal, locally invasive tissue called pannus.

Pannus is not just inflamed tissue sitting passively in the joint -- it actively grows into and erodes the adjacent cartilage and the underlying bone, releasing enzymes that break down the surrounding matrix as it advances. This is a fundamentally different destructive mechanism from osteoarthritis's slow mechanical wear: here, an immune-driven tissue is actively invading and destroying structures it shouldn't be touching at all, rather than a repair system simply falling behind cumulative mechanical demand.

Because the trigger is a systemic immune process rather than any one joint's individual loading history, rheumatoid arthritis's footprint looks different from osteoarthritis's in a very characteristic way: it tends to strike paired joints symmetrically (both wrists, both sets of finger joints), since there's no reason a systemic immune process would treat the left and right sides of the body differently, and it frequently produces effects entirely outside the joints -- fatigue, low-grade fever, and in more severe disease, involvement of other organs -- because the underlying inflammatory process was never confined to joint mechanics in the first place.

This mechanism is also why early, effective disease control matters more than simply managing pain. Pannus-driven erosion of cartilage and bone does not spontaneously reverse once it has occurred; suppressing the immune process that drives pannus formation can halt further destruction, but it cannot regrow joint structure that has already been eroded away, which is why the field's emphasis on controlling rheumatoid arthritis early and aggressively is a direct consequence of this destructive mechanism, not just a general preference for early treatment.
