The brain's anatomy reflects a division of labor built up over evolutionary time, with newer structures layered on top of older ones rather than a single uniform processing mass. The cerebral cortex, the brain's outermost and most recently evolved layer, does the most sophisticated cognitive work, and it's organized into four lobes per hemisphere with reasonably well-characterized dominant functions: frontal (planning, decision-making, voluntary motor output via the primary motor cortex), parietal (integrating touch, proprioception, and spatial information), temporal (auditory processing, and language comprehension in the dominant hemisphere), and occipital (visual processing).

Beneath the cortex sit older structures that handle more fundamental jobs. The limbic system, including the hippocampus and amygdala, handles memory consolidation and emotional salience respectively -- the hippocampus is essential for converting short-term experience into long-term memory, while the amygdala tags experiences (especially threatening or emotionally significant ones) for stronger encoding and faster future response. The brainstem, connecting the brain to the spinal cord, is evolutionarily the oldest region discussed here, and it runs the functions a person doesn't consciously manage at all -- breathing rhythm, heart rate, the sleep-wake cycle -- which is exactly why brainstem damage carries such a disproportionately high risk of death compared to cortical damage of similar size: a person can lose substantial cortical tissue and survive with a specific cognitive deficit, but even small brainstem lesions can disrupt the automatic functions life depends on.

The cerebellum sits beneath the occipital lobe and plays a fundamentally different role from all of the above: it doesn't initiate movement, it refines it, comparing the motor cortex's intended movement against real-time sensory feedback and making continuous corrections. This is why cerebellar damage produces ataxia -- clumsy, poorly-timed, poorly-coordinated movement and balance disturbance -- rather than paralysis: the underlying motor commands from the cortex are still generated and still reach the muscles, they're simply no longer fine-tuned along the way.
