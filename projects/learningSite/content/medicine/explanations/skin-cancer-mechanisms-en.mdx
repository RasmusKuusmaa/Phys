Skin cancer's most important mechanistic split is not by how severe or advanced a case is, but by which skin cell type the cancer arose from, because that origin determines almost everything about how the cancer behaves. Ultraviolet radiation is the shared trigger across skin cancer types: UV photons are absorbed directly by DNA, and the specific chemistry of that absorption tends to fuse adjacent pyrimidine bases into characteristic mutation-prone lesions. Cells have repair machinery that fixes most of this damage and an immune system that eliminates many of the cells that escape repair, but damage accumulated over years -- both from cumulative everyday exposure and from intense intermittent burning exposure -- can eventually overwhelm both defenses in a particular cell lineage.

Basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma both arise from keratinocytes, the same structural cells that make up most of the epidermis. Mutations accumulating in these cells' growth-control genes let them proliferate abnormally, but keratinocytes are not naturally a highly migratory cell type, so these cancers typically grow slowly, stay localized for a long time, and rarely metastasize to distant sites -- which is why they're collectively called non-melanoma skin cancer and are generally considered less dangerous, though still requiring treatment.

Melanoma arises from an entirely different cell type: melanocytes, the pigment-producing cells that supply keratinocytes with the melanin that gives skin its color and provides some UV protection. Melanocytes are developmentally derived from cells with an inherent capacity for migration, and when a melanocyte accumulates cancer-driving mutations, that latent migratory capacity gets switched back on, letting melanoma cells invade surrounding tissue and spread to distant organs far more readily and far earlier than a keratinocyte-derived cancer typically would. This cell-of-origin difference, not merely 'more UV damage' or 'later diagnosis,' is why melanoma is disproportionately responsible for skin-cancer deaths despite being less common than non-melanoma skin cancer overall -- and why melanin, while it reduces UV-driven mutation risk, doesn't make anyone with functioning melanocytes immune to the disease.
