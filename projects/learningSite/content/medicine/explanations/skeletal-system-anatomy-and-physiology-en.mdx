The skeleton looks, at first glance, like a fixed piece of architecture -- 206 bones bolted together to hold the body's shape. That picture misses what bone actually is: a living tissue with its own blood supply, nerve supply, and an ongoing metabolic life. Two cell populations drive this: osteoclasts, which dissolve old or damaged bone matrix, and osteoblasts, which lay down new matrix and mineralize it. In a healthy adult these two processes stay roughly balanced, continuously replacing the entire skeleton's mineral content over a period of years -- which is exactly what lets a bone that develops a stress fracture, or simply experiences a change in habitual load (a new exercise routine, a cast that shifts weight-bearing patterns), remodel itself to better resist that specific pattern of stress rather than staying frozen in its original form.

That remodeling cycle does double duty as the body's mineral reserve. Bone stores the vast majority of the body's calcium and phosphate, and specific hormones (parathyroid hormone chief among them) can accelerate osteoclast activity to release calcium into the blood on demand, or promote its return to bone when blood calcium runs high. Because so many of the body's cells -- muscle and nerve cells especially -- depend on blood calcium staying within a narrow physiological range to function at all, this reservoir role is not a side effect of having bones; it is one of the skeleton's primary jobs, on equal footing with mechanical support.

Where two bones meet, the joint connecting them is built for whatever specific balance of mobility and stability that location needs. Fibrous joints, like the sutures between skull plates, allow essentially no movement and exist purely to hold bones rigidly together. Cartilaginous joints, like those between vertebral bodies, allow slight, cushioned movement. Synovial joints -- the knee, hip, shoulder, and most of the joints people think of when they picture a joint at all -- are enclosed in a fluid-filled capsule that lets bones glide against nearly frictionless cartilage-capped surfaces, trading some structural rigidity for a wide range of motion. This spread of joint types is not an inconsistency in the skeleton's design; it reflects that a skull needs rigidity a shoulder never would, and a shoulder needs mobility a skull never should have.

Finally, the skeleton contributes to two functions that have nothing directly to do with movement. Certain bones -- the skull, rib cage, and vertebral column especially -- form rigid enclosures that physically shield the brain, heart, lungs, and spinal cord from mechanical injury. And inside the marrow cavities of specific bones (the pelvis and sternum, prominently, in an adult), stem cells continuously divide and differentiate into red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets in a process called hematopoiesis -- meaning the skeletal system is, quite literally, also part of the circulatory and immune systems' supply chain.
