Every drug administered to the body faces the same four hurdles, conventionally summarized as ADME: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. Absorption matters most for anything not injected directly into the bloodstream. A tablet swallowed whole must dissolve, survive the stomach's acidic environment, cross the intestinal lining into the portal blood supply, and then pass through the liver before reaching general circulation -- that liver pass, called first-pass metabolism, can inactivate a substantial fraction of some drugs before they ever get a chance to act anywhere else in the body. Bioavailability is the precise term for what fraction of the administered dose survives this journey intact: by definition it is 100% for an intravenous dose, since that route bypasses absorption and first-pass metabolism entirely, and it is often considerably lower for an oral dose of the identical drug.

Once a drug reaches the bloodstream, distribution determines where it goes next. A drug doesn't spread through the body like a dye through water; its movement into any particular tissue depends on how much blood that tissue receives, how readily the drug crosses cell membranes (fat-soluble drugs cross more easily than water-soluble ones), how much of it is bound to plasma proteins like albumin (only the unbound fraction is typically free to leave the bloodstream and act), and whether specialized barriers restrict its entry -- the blood-brain barrier being the most famous example, deliberately keeping many drugs and other substances out of the central nervous system.

Metabolism and excretion are how the body eventually clears a drug. Metabolism, carried out mostly by liver enzymes, chemically alters the drug's structure -- most commonly making it more water-soluble and less pharmacologically active, though prodrugs are a notable exception where metabolism converts an inactive administered form into the actually active drug. Excretion, predominantly via the kidneys into urine (though also via bile, breath, or other routes depending on the drug), removes the drug and its metabolic products from the body permanently. Because these four processes vary independently across different drugs -- one drug might be rapidly absorbed but slowly metabolized, another the reverse -- knowing a drug's pharmacodynamic mechanism alone says nothing about how often or in what amount it needs to be given; that's a separate question this concept's four processes answer.
