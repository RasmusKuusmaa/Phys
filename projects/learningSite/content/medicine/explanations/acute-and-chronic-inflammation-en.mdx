Inflammation's textbook signs -- redness, heat, swelling, pain -- are all direct consequences of one underlying vascular event: local blood vessels dilate and their walls become more permeable, letting more blood (hence the redness and warmth) and protein-rich fluid (hence the swelling) reach the site within minutes of an injury or infection being detected. Neutrophils, the first responders among white blood cells, follow that fluid out of the bloodstream and into the tissue, where they attack pathogens and clear debris. This entire sequence -- vascular change, fluid leakage, neutrophil arrival -- is acute inflammation, and it is built to be self-limiting: once the triggering stimulus is cleared, the vascular changes reverse, the neutrophils are cleared away (many by their own apoptosis), and the tissue either regenerates fully or, if the damage was significant, is patched with a scar. The entire cycle typically completes within days.

Chronic inflammation is not simply acute inflammation that has gone on too long -- it is a qualitatively different process that develops when the original trigger cannot be cleared: a foreign body the body cannot break down, an infection the immune system cannot eliminate, or an ongoing autoimmune reaction attacking the body's own tissue. The cell population shifts accordingly, from short-lived neutrophils to longer-lived macrophages and lymphocytes, cells built for a sustained response rather than a rapid burst. And unlike acute inflammation's clean sequence of damage-then-repair, chronic inflammation runs both processes at once, continuously, for weeks, months, or years: tissue destruction from the ongoing immune activity and attempted repair happening side by side, in the same location, without either ever fully winning. That simultaneous destruction-and-repair is precisely why chronic inflammation so often ends in fibrosis rather than restored normal tissue -- fibroblasts keep laying down collagen in response to the ongoing damage, and the result is functional scar tissue rather than the original specialized tissue.

Granulomatous inflammation is a specific, recognizable pattern within chronic inflammation, reserved for situations where macrophages encounter something they can neither destroy nor safely ignore -- certain bacteria with cell walls resistant to normal digestion (the bacterium causing tuberculosis is the classic example), or foreign material like a splinter or suture that cannot be broken down. Rather than continuing an open-ended, unsuccessful attempt at elimination, the macrophages organize into a tight, walled-off cluster (a granuloma) that physically contains the irritant, sometimes for a lifetime. This is best understood as a stalemate rather than a resolution: in latent tuberculosis, for instance, the causative bacteria can remain alive and walled off inside granulomas for decades with no active disease, until something (like a weakened immune system) disrupts the containment and allows the infection to reactivate.
