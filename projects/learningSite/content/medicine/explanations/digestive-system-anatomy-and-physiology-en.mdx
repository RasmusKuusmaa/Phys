The gastrointestinal tract is, anatomically, a single continuous tube roughly nine meters long from mouth to anus, and it makes more sense to think of digestion as a relay running along that tube than as one organ's job. In the mouth, chewing mechanically breaks food into smaller pieces while salivary enzymes begin breaking down starches, and swallowing sends the resulting mass down the esophagus, whose only job is transport -- rhythmic muscular contractions (peristalsis) push food toward the stomach regardless of body position, which is why you can swallow while lying down or even upside down.

The stomach's popular reputation as the seat of digestion is only partly earned. It does real chemical work -- hydrochloric acid denatures proteins and activates the enzyme pepsin, which begins breaking proteins into shorter chains -- and it does real mechanical work, churning food into a semi-liquid mixture called chyme. But the stomach's lining is not built to absorb the products of digestion; it releases chyme in controlled amounts into the small intestine, where the far larger share of the actual work happens.

The small intestine is where digestion and absorption both reach their peak, and it accomplishes this with substantial outside help. The pancreas, an organ sitting behind the stomach, secretes a cocktail of enzymes directly into the small intestine's first segment (the duodenum) -- proteases to continue breaking down proteins, lipases to break down fats, amylase to break down remaining carbohydrates -- doing the bulk of the tract's actual enzymatic digestion. Fat digestion needs an extra step first, because fat doesn't mix with the tract's watery contents: the liver continuously produces bile, which the gallbladder stores and releases on cue, and bile's job is purely mechanical -- emulsifying large fat globules into much smaller droplets so pancreatic lipase has enough surface area to actually act on them. Once broken down into small enough molecules, nutrients cross the small intestine's lining -- itself folded and covered in finger-like villi and microscopic microvilli specifically to maximize absorptive surface area -- directly into the bloodstream (or, for some fats, into the lymphatic system).

By the time undigested material reaches the large intestine, essentially all available nutrients have already been extracted; the large intestine's job is different, not a continuation of the same one. It reabsorbs much of the remaining water and electrolytes, concentrating waste into a manageable, semi-solid form, and it hosts an enormous resident population of gut bacteria that ferment otherwise-undigested fiber, in the process producing some vitamins and short-chain fatty acids the body can still use. What eventually leaves the body as waste has already gone through this entire sequential division of labor -- mechanical and initial enzymatic breakdown in the mouth and stomach, the great majority of chemical digestion and essentially all absorption in the small intestine (with the pancreas and liver as essential outside contributors), and water reclamation plus bacterial fermentation in the large intestine.
