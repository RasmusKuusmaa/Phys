Getting a nutrient from a meal into the bloodstream requires a working chain of several distinct steps: mechanical breakdown, enzymatic digestion into simpler absorbable molecules, and finally transport of those molecules across the intestinal epithelium into the blood. "Malabsorption" describes the downstream symptom that results whenever any link in this chain fails, which is exactly why it functions as a shared clinical picture rather than a single disease -- genuinely different conditions can fail at completely different steps and still present with an overlapping set of consequences (weight loss, diarrhea, nutrient deficiencies).

Celiac disease is a failure at the very last step of this chain, absorption itself, driven by an autoimmune mechanism. In someone with celiac disease, gluten exposure triggers an immune response that attacks and progressively flattens the small intestine's villi, the finger-like projections whose entire purpose is multiplying the intestine's absorptive surface area many times over. With that surface area destroyed, even nutrients that have already been completely and correctly broken down by normal digestive enzymes have nowhere near enough absorptive surface remaining to cross into the bloodstream in adequate quantity. The digestive machinery upstream of absorption is entirely intact in celiac disease; what has failed is the final transport step.

Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency fails at an earlier link in the same chain: digestion itself, before absorption ever becomes relevant. The pancreas's exocrine function -- producing the enzymes (lipases, proteases, amylases) that break dietary fat, protein, and carbohydrate down into their absorbable forms -- can fail for various reasons (chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, and other causes), and without those enzymes reaching the small intestine in adequate quantity, food is never properly broken down into anything the intestinal lining could absorb even if that lining were in perfect health. Someone with pancreatic exocrine insufficiency can have a completely undamaged, fully functional intestinal absorptive surface and still develop severe malabsorption, because there is simply nothing correctly digested for that surface to absorb.

This is the central mechanistic point malabsorption syndromes illustrate: a shared symptom picture does not imply a shared cause, and correctly understanding any specific case requires identifying which particular step of the digestion-and-absorption pathway has actually failed, not simply recognizing that "malabsorption" is occurring.
