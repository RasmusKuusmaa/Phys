The cell membrane's structure is what makes its selective behavior possible in the first place: it is a phospholipid bilayer, two layers of lipid molecules with water-loving heads facing outward toward the watery environments on both sides and water-fearing tails facing inward toward each other, studded throughout with proteins that float within this fluid layer (the "fluid mosaic model" of membrane structure). That lipid core is what makes the membrane a barrier to anything charged or large in the first place, and it's also why small, nonpolar molecules like oxygen, carbon dioxide, and lipid-soluble hormones can slip straight through it by simple diffusion, moving down their own concentration gradient with no help and no energy cost.

Everything else needs a protein escort. Facilitated diffusion still moves a substance down its concentration gradient and still costs no cellular energy, but it requires a channel or carrier protein embedded in the membrane, since the substance (glucose, ions) cannot cross the hydrophobic lipid core unaided. Active transport is the more expensive alternative: it moves a substance against its concentration gradient, which takes energy, almost always supplied by ATP. The sodium-potassium pump is the textbook example, continuously exporting sodium ions and importing potassium ions against their respective gradients; the steep ion gradients this pump maintains are not a side effect but the entire point, since neurons and muscle cells depend on suddenly, briefly reversing those gradients (a nerve impulse or muscle contraction) as their basic mechanism of electrical signaling.

Chemical communication between cells reuses the same underlying logic of the membrane as gatekeeper, but for information rather than matter. Most signaling molecules -- peptide hormones, neurotransmitters -- are themselves too large or too polar to cross the membrane, so they don't try to: they bind a specific receptor protein sitting in the membrane, and that binding event, occurring entirely at the cell surface, is what actually triggers the response. The receptor changes shape when the signaling molecule binds, and that shape change sets off a signal transduction cascade -- a chain of intracellular proteins that each activate the next, often using small molecules called second messengers (cyclic AMP is a common one) to relay and amplify the signal deep into the cell.

That amplification step matters enormously: because each activated protein in the cascade can go on to activate many copies of the next protein in the chain, a single hormone molecule binding a single receptor can ultimately switch on thousands of downstream molecules, turning one small binding event at the cell surface into a large, fast, coordinated cellular response -- a gene switching on, a muscle fiber contracting, a vesicle releasing its contents. This transport-and-signaling machinery is what lets a cell act as a controlled, responsive system rather than simply reacting passively to whatever concentration gradients happen to exist around it, and it's the same machinery every subsequent stage of a cell's life -- deciding when to divide, deciding when to die -- ultimately has to route its own internal decisions through.
