Everything this subject has already established about host-pathogen interactions -- immune cells recruited to a site of infection, inflammatory mediators coordinating the response, the tissue-level consequences of that response -- applies to pneumonia too, but with one crucial difference in location: the battle is fought inside the alveoli, the thin-walled air sacs that exist for exactly one purpose, letting oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse between air and blood across the shortest possible distance. That anatomical fact is what makes pneumonia's physiological consequences distinct from an infection anywhere else in the body.

When a pathogen -- typically a bacterium, though viruses and other organisms cause pneumonia too -- reaches the alveoli, the same innate and adaptive immune mechanisms already established in this subject's immunology module respond: phagocytes and other inflammatory cells are recruited into the infected airspace, along with protein-rich fluid, in an effort to contain and clear the infection. This process, consolidation, is visible on imaging as the affected region of lung fills in solid rather than showing the normal air-filled pattern, and it is a genuinely appropriate, functioning immune response doing exactly what it is supposed to do.

The problem is that the alveolus doing this immune work cannot simultaneously do its normal job. Efficient gas exchange depends on oxygen and carbon dioxide diffusing across an extremely thin air-blood barrier; fill that same airspace with inflammatory cells and fluid instead of air, and diffusion across it slows dramatically or stops entirely. A consolidated alveolus, in other words, is functionally removed from the lung's gas-exchange capacity for as long as consolidation persists -- not because anything is wrong with the alveolus's structure, but because the space that should carry air is occupied by the very process fighting off the infection.

This is why the severity of pneumonia's respiratory symptoms tracks the extent of consolidated lung tissue rather than simply the presence or absence of infection. A small area of consolidation in an otherwise healthy lung with ample reserve capacity may produce only mild symptoms, since the unaffected lung can compensate; a larger consolidated area, or the same degree of consolidation in someone whose lung reserve is already reduced by another condition, can produce substantial shortness of breath and measurably low blood oxygen, even though the infection itself may be entirely confined to one lobe. The core physiological insight is that pneumonia's danger is a side effect of an appropriately functioning immune response occurring in an organ that has essentially no spare room for that response without some cost to its primary function.
