Intact skin is remarkably good at keeping microorganisms out, which is why most skin infections begin at a point where that barrier has already failed -- a cut, an insect bite, an area of eczema or psoriasis with a compromised barrier, or simply skin kept persistently moist and softened. What happens after entry depends heavily on which category of organism is responsible, because bacteria, fungi, and viruses exploit skin in mechanistically different ways.

Bacterial skin infections, like impetigo or cellulitis, generally work by breaking down tissue directly: the responsible bacteria secrete toxins and enzymes that degrade the barriers between skin cells and tissue layers, letting the infection spread through superficial layers (impetigo) or deeper into the dermis and subcutaneous tissue (cellulitis). This is an active invasion of living tissue.

Fungal skin infections, most commonly caused by dermatophyte fungi (the cause of conditions colloquially called ringworm, athlete's foot, or jock itch depending on location), work completely differently. These fungi specifically metabolize keratin, the structural protein that makes up the skin's outer, already-dead cell layer -- meaning they can establish and spread an infection entirely within non-living tissue, without ever needing to breach into living cells the way bacteria do. This is also why dermatophyte infections tend to stay superficial and spread outward in a ring pattern as the fungus consumes keratin at the infection's advancing edge.

Viral skin infections, like those caused by herpes simplex virus or human papillomavirus (warts), work differently still: these viruses actually infect living keratinocytes, hijacking the cell's own internal machinery to replicate viral copies of themselves. This is why viral skin lesions can persist, recur, and sometimes remain latent within cells for long periods -- the virus has established itself inside living cells, not simply sitting on the skin's surface -- a fundamentally different relationship with the host tissue than either the bacterial or fungal mechanism.
