The endocrine system's defining trait is timescale: where the nervous system fires signals in milliseconds along dedicated point-to-point connections, hormones are released into the general circulation and must travel by diffusion and blood flow to reach whatever cells happen to have the right receptor, a process that inherently takes minutes to hours rather than an instant. This is also what separates true endocrine signaling from its close relatives -- paracrine signaling, where a cell releases a signal that only reaches its immediate neighbors, and autocrine signaling, where a cell responds to its own released signal -- the same basic chemical-messenger idea operating at progressively larger distances.

What a given hormone can do, and how fast, is largely set by which of three structural classes it belongs to. Peptide and protein hormones like insulin and growth hormone are built from amino acid chains, which makes them water-soluble but unable to cross the fatty cell membrane; they instead bind a receptor sitting on the cell's surface, and that binding event triggers a fast intracellular relay (a "second messenger" cascade) that can alter cell behavior within seconds to minutes without the hormone itself ever entering the cell. Steroid hormones like cortisol, estrogen, and testosterone are built from cholesterol, which makes them lipid-soluble and able to diffuse straight through the cell membrane; once inside, they typically bind a receptor that then travels to the nucleus and directly changes which genes are being read, a mechanism that takes longer to produce an effect (hours) but tends to produce longer-lasting changes once it does.

Amine hormones -- thyroid hormone and the adrenal medulla's epinephrine and norepinephrine -- are structurally the odd ones out: each is built from a single modified amino acid, small enough that different amine hormones end up behaving like either of the other two classes depending on their specific chemistry. Thyroid hormone is lipid-soluble enough to act more like a steroid hormone, entering cells and influencing gene transcription over hours, while epinephrine acts through cell-surface receptors like a peptide hormone, producing its effects within seconds.

None of this happens without the last, essential piece: a target cell must actually carry the matching receptor for a hormone to have any effect on it at all. A hormone circulates past every cell in the body indiscriminately, but only cells expressing its specific receptor can respond, which is why the same hormone can produce completely different effects in different tissues (or none) depending purely on which cells happen to be listening.
