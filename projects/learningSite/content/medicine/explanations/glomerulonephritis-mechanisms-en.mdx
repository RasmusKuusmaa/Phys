The glomerulus's job -- filtering blood under high pressure through an extremely fine barrier that lets water and small solutes through while holding back blood cells and large proteins -- makes it uniquely vulnerable to a category of immune injury that has nothing to do with any pathogen actually infecting the kidney itself.

The more common mechanistic pattern is immune complex deposition. When an infection occurs anywhere in the body -- a streptococcal throat or skin infection is a classic example -- the immune system produces antibodies against the pathogen's antigens, and antigen-antibody complexes form in the general circulation as part of an entirely normal immune response. These complexes are usually cleared without incident, but the glomerulus's structure makes it a site where circulating complexes are especially prone to becoming physically trapped: blood is forced through the glomerular filter under substantial pressure, and complexes of a certain size can lodge within the filtration barrier itself rather than passing through or being cleared elsewhere. Once trapped there, these complexes activate the complement system and recruit inflammatory cells locally, producing glomerular inflammation and damage even though the kidney was never the actual target of the original immune response -- it is, in a real sense, an innocent bystander caught in the crossfire of an immune reaction aimed at something else entirely.

A mechanistically distinct pattern involves antibodies that form directly against components of the glomerular basement membrane or other structural elements of the filtration barrier itself. In this pattern, the kidney is not an incidental casualty of an immune response directed elsewhere -- it is the specific, direct target of an autoimmune attack, the same fundamental process of self-tolerance breakdown covered in this subject's autoimmunity concept, here targeting glomerular structures specifically.

Regardless of which of these two mechanisms is responsible, both converge on the same functional consequence: damage to the filtration barrier compromises its normal size-selectivity. A healthy, intact glomerular barrier reliably excludes red blood cells and large proteins from the filtrate; an inflamed, damaged barrier lets both leak through into the forming urine. This is exactly why blood and excess protein in the urine are the characteristic findings of glomerulonephritis -- not because the kidney is producing anything abnormal, but because its normally reliable filtration barrier has been breached, letting through substances that a healthy barrier would have excluded in the first place.
