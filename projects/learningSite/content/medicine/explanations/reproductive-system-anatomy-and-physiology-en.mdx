The male and female reproductive tracts are built around genuinely different production timelines for their respective gametes, and that difference in timeline explains a lot of what follows in reproductive medicine. In the testes, the seminiferous tubules house spermatogenesis: diploid spermatogonia divide mitotically to maintain a stem-cell population, then a subset commits to meiosis, producing haploid spermatids that mature into motile sperm. This process runs continuously from puberty through most of adult life, taking roughly two to three months from start to finish for any given sperm cell, and produces enormous numbers of sperm on an ongoing, renewable basis.

Oogenesis runs on the opposite kind of schedule. By roughly mid-gestation, a female fetus has already formed essentially the full lifetime supply of oocytes, each one having begun meiosis and then arrested partway through it -- specifically in prophase of meiosis I -- where it will sit, unchanged, for years to decades. Each menstrual cycle, a cohort of arrested oocytes begins to grow, one is selected to fully mature, and only around the time of ovulation does that single oocyte resume and complete meiosis I (the second meiotic division isn't completed at all unless fertilization actually occurs). No new oocytes are ever added to this supply; it can only decline with age, which is a large part of why female fertility has a much sharper age-related decline than male fertility does.

Sperm leaving the testes are not yet functionally complete: they lack the motility and fertilizing capacity a sperm cell needs to reach and penetrate an egg. The epididymis, a long coiled duct sitting against each testis, is where this final maturation happens during the days sperm spend in transit and storage there, before being propelled through the vas deferens during ejaculation, picking up seminal fluid contributed by the seminal vesicles and prostate gland along the way.

On the female side, the fallopian tubes are where an ovulated egg is actually swept up (not automatically drawn in by simple proximity -- the tube's fimbriae actively sweep over the ovary's surface) and where fertilization, if it happens, actually takes place, before the resulting embryo travels on to the uterus. The uterine lining, the endometrium, is not a passive structural surface: under the influence of the ovarian hormones estrogen and progesterone, it thickens and becomes receptive to implantation during the second half of each cycle, and is shed as menstruation if no implantation occurs -- an actively managed tissue cycling on the same monthly rhythm as the ovary itself.
