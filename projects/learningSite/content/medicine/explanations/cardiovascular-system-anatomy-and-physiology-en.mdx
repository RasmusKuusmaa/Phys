Ask someone to point to their heart and most people place a hand to the left side of their chest -- and then conclude the heart itself must live there. What they're actually locating is the point of maximal impulse, where the heart's lower-left chamber presses most firmly against the chest wall during contraction. The heart itself sits behind the sternum, roughly centered in the chest, tilted so that its apex points down and to the left; that tilt, not an off-center location, is why the beat feels strongest on the left.

Structurally the heart is really two pumps sharing one muscular wall, not one pump. Each side has two chambers: a thin-walled atrium that receives incoming blood, and a thicker-walled ventricle that does the actual pumping out. The right atrium receives oxygen-poor blood returning from the entire body, passes it to the right ventricle, which pumps it to the lungs -- the pulmonary circulation. The left atrium receives the now oxygenated blood returning from the lungs, passes it to the left ventricle, which pumps it out through the aorta to the entire rest of the body -- the systemic circulation. These two circuits form a closed figure-eight, not two independent loops: blood leaving the right side always returns to the left side via the lungs, and blood leaving the left side always returns to the right side via the body, but the right and left chambers themselves never directly mix blood in a normally formed heart.

Four one-way valves keep this figure-eight moving in a single consistent direction: the tricuspid and mitral valves sit between each atrium and its ventricle, and the pulmonary and aortic valves sit between each ventricle and the artery it empties into. Each valve opens to let blood pass forward and slams shut the instant pressure would otherwise push blood backward -- and it's specifically those four valves closing, not the muscle contracting, that produces the two heart sounds a stethoscope picks up ('lub' as the atrioventricular valves close at the start of ventricular contraction, 'dub' as the semilunar valves close at its end).

The whole sequence -- both atria filling and contracting, then both ventricles filling and contracting -- repeats as the cardiac cycle, alternating between diastole (chambers relaxed, filling with blood) and systole (ventricles contracting, ejecting blood). What makes this rhythm remarkable is where it comes from: a small patch of specialized cells in the right atrium, the sinoatrial node, spontaneously and rhythmically depolarizes on its own, generating the electrical signal that triggers each heartbeat without any input required from the brain at all. This is why a heart removed for transplant keeps beating in a dish, and why a transplanted heart -- with all its original nerve connections severed -- beats normally in its new body; the nervous system's role is real but secondary, speeding the heart up or slowing it down, strengthening or weakening each beat, never originating the beat itself.
