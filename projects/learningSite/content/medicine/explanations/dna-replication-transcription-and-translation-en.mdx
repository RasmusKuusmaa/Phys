Before a cell can divide, it has to copy its entire genetic instruction set with extremely high fidelity, and that job belongs to DNA replication. The double helix is unwound at a specific site called the origin of replication by the enzyme helicase, exposing two single strands that each serve as a template. DNA polymerase then builds a new, complementary strand along each template -- but polymerase can only add new nucleotides in one chemical direction, which creates a genuine asymmetry: on one template, the new strand (the leading strand) can be synthesized in one continuous run following the replication fork as it opens; on the other template, the new strand (the lagging strand) has to be built backward, in short separate pieces (Okazaki fragments) that a separate enzyme, DNA ligase, later stitches together. The end result is still two complete, essentially identical double helices, one going to each daughter cell -- but the mechanism that produces that symmetric-looking outcome is not itself symmetric.

Transcription is a fundamentally different operation aimed at a fundamentally different goal: rather than copying the whole genome, it copies one gene at a time into a disposable, working RNA copy. RNA polymerase recognizes a promoter sequence marking where a specific gene begins, then reads along the DNA template strand, building a complementary messenger RNA (mRNA) strand instead of a DNA strand. Because only the gene actually needed at that moment gets transcribed, a cell can express wildly different sets of genes depending on its type and current needs, all while sharing the exact same underlying DNA sequence. In eukaryotic cells, this newly made mRNA is processed further before it leaves the nucleus (a cap and tail are added, and non-coding regions called introns are cut out), then exported to the cytoplasm to be read.

Translation is where the genetic message actually becomes a physical protein. A ribosome, itself built from both protein and RNA, binds the mRNA and reads it in non-overlapping groups of three bases at a time, called codons. Each codon specifies exactly one amino acid (or a stop signal), and the correspondence is delivered by transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules: each tRNA carries one specific amino acid on one end and a three-base anticodon on the other end that base-pairs with the matching mRNA codon, ensuring the right amino acid gets added at each step. The ribosome links each newly delivered amino acid onto the growing protein chain in the order the codons specify, continuing until it reaches a stop codon, at which point the finished protein chain is released.

Laid end to end, these three processes form what's often called the central dogma of molecular biology: DNA is replicated to preserve the genetic instructions across cell divisions, DNA is transcribed into a portable RNA working copy of one gene at a time, and that RNA copy is translated, three bases at a time, into the specific sequence of amino acids that folds into a functioning protein. Treating these as one blurred process obscures exactly what makes the system work: three distinct enzyme machines, each solving a different sub-problem (permanent archival copying, selective on-demand transcription, and precise codon-to-amino-acid translation), operating in a fixed sequence rather than as a single continuous event.
