Bacteria are prokaryotic, but fungi and parasites belong to the same broad domain of life as humans do -- Eukarya -- which sounds like a minor taxonomic detail until you're trying to design a drug. A fungal or parasitic cell has a nucleus, mitochondria, and ribosomes built on the same eukaryotic template as a human cell's, which means the list of molecular targets that exist in the pathogen but not in the patient is much shorter than it is for bacteria; this single fact explains most of what's distinctive about treating fungal and parasitic disease compared to bacterial disease.

Pathogenic fungi split structurally into yeasts and molds. Yeasts are single-celled and reproduce by budding, growing a small outgrowth that pinches off into a new, genetically identical cell -- Candida species, part of the normal human microbiome in small numbers, are the classic example, causing disease (candidiasis) specifically when their population grows unchecked, often because a competing bacterial population has been suppressed or the host's immune defenses are compromised. Molds instead grow as hyphae, branching multicellular filaments that extend and interweave into a mycelium; dermatophyte molds causing ringworm and athlete's foot exploit keratin in skin, hair, and nails as a nutrient source without needing to invade deeper, living tissue at all. The more dangerous end of fungal disease is invasive infection, where a fungus normally kept in check by a healthy immune system spreads into deeper tissue or the bloodstream -- almost exclusively a disease of the immunocompromised, since a fully functional immune system handles these fungi without difficulty in the vast majority of healthy people.

Parasites span an even wider range of complexity. Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic parasites -- the Plasmodium species that cause malaria are transmitted by Anopheles mosquito bite, injected as a form that first infects the liver before entering red blood cells, where cyclical rounds of replication and cell rupture produce malaria's classic periodic fever pattern, timed to the parasite's own reproductive cycle rather than appearing immediately on infection. Helminths are multicellular parasitic worms -- tapeworms, roundworms, and flukes -- large enough to be visible to the naked eye as adults, and capable of persisting inside a human host for years, sometimes with minimal symptoms, through strategies like living in a location relatively shielded from immune surveillance (the intestinal lumen) or actively suppressing the local immune response around themselves. Across both fungi and parasites, the shared eukaryotic-cell problem keeps reappearing: an antifungal drug that damages a fungus's ergosterol-containing membrane, for instance, has to reckon with the fact that a related but distinct sterol, cholesterol, sits in every human cell membrane too, which is exactly why several major antifungal drug classes carry meaningfully more side-effect risk than a typical course of antibacterial antibiotics.
