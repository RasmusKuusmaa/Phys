Eczema's itch is often mistaken for the disease itself, but the itching and inflammation are downstream consequences of a structural problem: a leaky epidermal barrier. Filaggrin and related structural proteins normally help keratinocytes flatten, align, and pack tightly together as they die and rise toward the skin's surface, sealed by a lipid matrix that keeps the barrier watertight. When filaggrin function is reduced (through genetic variants or acquired dysfunction), that sealing process is incomplete, leaving microscopic gaps in the outer barrier. Water escapes outward through those gaps faster than normal, which is why eczema-prone skin is characteristically dry; more importantly, allergens, irritants, and microbes penetrate inward far more easily than they would through an intact barrier.

That inward penetration is what triggers the immune system's response -- the redness, swelling, and intense itch of an eczema flare are the inflammatory reaction to substances that shouldn't have gotten past the barrier in the first place, not a spontaneous immune malfunction unrelated to the skin's physical structure. This is why eczema treatment strategies that focus purely on suppressing inflammation without also restoring the barrier (moisturizing, barrier-repair creams) tend to be less durable than approaches addressing both.

The itch-scratch cycle then compounds the original problem. Scratching provides brief relief but mechanically disrupts the skin further, widening the very barrier gaps that caused the itch, and can introduce new irritants or bacteria directly into broken skin. This is why the cycle is self-sustaining rather than self-limiting: each scratch worsens the barrier defect that produced the itch in the first place, and a barrier this compromised is also more vulnerable to genuine secondary infection, a real but distinct complication layered on top of the primary condition.
