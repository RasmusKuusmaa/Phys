Most drugs leave the body by first-order elimination: at any given moment, the rate of removal is proportional to how much drug is currently present, not a fixed amount per unit time. That single mathematical fact has a very intuitive consequence -- the concentration falls by the same fraction (half) over the same fixed time interval, no matter what the starting concentration was, exactly the same pattern radioactive decay follows. That fixed interval is called the elimination half-life, and it's one of the most quoted numbers in clinical pharmacology precisely because so much practical dosing behavior follows directly from it.

A single dose's concentration-time curve is fully described by the same halving relationship used for radioactive decay: after one half-life, half the drug remains; after two, a quarter; after three, an eighth; and so on, with the concentration approaching (but mathematically never quite reaching) zero. This is why a drug with a short half-life (a few hours) needs to be dosed several times a day to maintain a therapeutic concentration, while a drug with a long half-life (days) can be dosed once daily or even less often.

Repeated dosing changes the picture only slightly, but in a way that's easy to get wrong. If a drug is given again before the previous dose has fully cleared, the new dose adds to whatever concentration remains, and the peak concentration climbs with each subsequent dose -- but not forever. Because elimination rate scales with concentration, a higher concentration eliminates faster in absolute terms, and the system settles into a steady state where the amount eliminated between doses exactly balances the amount added by each new dose. As a practical rule, that steady state is reached, to a good approximation, after about four to five half-lives of regular dosing -- a drug with a 6-hour half-life reaches steady state in roughly one to one-and-a-quarter days, while a drug with a 5-day half-life can take three to four weeks. The same four-to-five-half-life rule governs the reverse process too: how long it takes a drug (or its effects) to essentially clear the body once dosing stops.
