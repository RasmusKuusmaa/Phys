A virus solves the problem of reproduction by not solving it at all -- it outsources the entire job to a host cell. Stripped to its essentials, a virus is genetic material (either DNA or RNA, single- or double-stranded depending on the virus family, but never a mix of both types at once) enclosed in a capsid, a shell built from repeating protein subunits that self-assemble into a stable geometric structure. Some viruses add a further layer: an envelope, a lipid membrane the virus acquires by budding through a host cell's own membrane on its way out, studded with viral proteins that let it recognize and attach to the next host cell it encounters. None of this comes with the machinery a cell would need to do anything on its own -- no ribosomes to build proteins, no mitochondria or fermentation pathway to generate energy, nothing resembling independent metabolism. A virus outside a host cell is, functionally, an inert particle; it only becomes biologically active once it injects or delivers its genetic material into a susceptible cell and conscripts that cell's own ribosomes, enzymes, and energy supply to build new virus particles.

Once inside, a virus follows one of two broad strategies, and the difference between them is really a difference in timing and risk tolerance. The lytic cycle is fast and destructive: the viral genome immediately redirects the host cell toward transcribing and translating viral genes, assembling new capsids, and packaging fresh copies of the viral genome inside them, until the cell is so full of new virus particles that it lyses (bursts), releasing everything at once to go infect neighboring cells. The lysogenic cycle trades speed for stealth: certain viruses (temperate bacteriophages are the classic example, though the general strategy shows up elsewhere too) instead insert their genetic material directly into the host cell's own chromosome, where it becomes a prophage -- a stretch of viral DNA that looks, to the host cell's replication machinery, just like any other piece of the host's own genome. Every time that host cell divides, the integrated viral genome gets copied right along with it, for free, without producing a single new viral particle or doing any visible damage. This dormant state can persist across many host-cell generations until some trigger -- often a stress signal indicating the host cell's future is in jeopardy anyway, such as DNA damage -- flips the prophage into the lytic cycle, at which point it excises itself from the host chromosome and starts producing new virus particles in earnest.

This two-strategy structure also explains why viruses evolve so quickly, which matters enormously for both natural outbreaks and drug development. RNA viruses in particular use an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase to copy their genome, an enzyme that (unlike the DNA polymerases cells use to copy their own chromosomes) has little or no proofreading ability, so it introduces new mutations at a comparatively high rate every time the virus replicates. Combined with the sheer number of new virus particles a single lytic infection can produce, this generates enormous genetic diversity within a viral population extremely quickly -- which is exactly the raw material natural selection acts on when a new drug-resistant strain emerges, or when a virus's surface proteins change enough to partially evade a host's existing antibody response.
