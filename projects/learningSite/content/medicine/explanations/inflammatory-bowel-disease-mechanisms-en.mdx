Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis are grouped together as "inflammatory bowel disease" because both are chronic conditions driven by an inappropriate, self-sustaining immune response against the gastrointestinal tract, but the two diseases differ in exactly the ways that determine their clinical behavior and their response to surgery: where the inflammation occurs, and how deep it goes.

Crohn's disease can involve any segment of the digestive tract, from the mouth all the way to the anus, though it most commonly affects the terminal ileum and colon. Its hallmark distribution pattern is patchy and discontinuous -- inflamed segments ("skip lesions") interspersed with entirely normal-appearing bowel, rather than one continuous affected region. Just as distinctively, Crohn's inflammation is transmural, extending through the full thickness of the bowel wall rather than staying confined to its inner lining. This depth is mechanistically what produces Crohn's disease's characteristic complications: inflammation that has burrowed all the way through the wall can tunnel into adjacent structures, forming fistulas (abnormal connections between the bowel and other organs, or the skin), and repeated cycles of inflammation and healing through the full wall thickness can produce fibrotic strictures that narrow the bowel lumen.

Ulcerative colitis is far more geographically constrained: it is confined to the colon and rectum, and unlike Crohn's, it spreads in a continuous pattern beginning at the rectum and extending proximally, without the patchy skip lesions Crohn's produces. Its inflammation is also mechanistically shallower, limited to the mucosa, the innermost lining layer, rather than penetrating the full wall thickness. This shallower, mucosa-limited inflammation is why ulcerative colitis does not typically produce the fistulas and strictures characteristic of Crohn's -- there simply isn't deep transmural damage to tunnel through or scar into a stricture.

This location-and-depth distinction has a direct, practically important consequence: because ulcerative colitis is entirely confined to a removable, defined anatomical region (the colon and rectum), surgically removing that tissue removes the disease along with it -- a genuinely curative option unique to ulcerative colitis. Crohn's disease offers no equivalent option, precisely because its disease process is not tied to one removable segment; even after removing an actively diseased section of bowel, the same underlying immune process can produce new inflammation anywhere else along the digestive tract that was never touched by surgery.
