Breathing runs automatically without conscious thought because respiratory centers in the brainstem generate its basic rhythm continuously, but that rhythm is not fixed -- it is constantly fine-tuned by chemoreceptor feedback reporting on the blood's actual gas content, and the relative importance of the two feedback pathways is the opposite of what popular intuition usually assumes.

Central chemoreceptors sit in the brainstem itself, positioned to sense the composition of the cerebrospinal fluid bathing the brain rather than blood directly. Carbon dioxide crosses the blood-brain barrier readily, where it shifts cerebrospinal fluid pH in a way these receptors detect with high sensitivity; a modest rise in blood carbon dioxide produces a correspondingly prompt increase in ventilation. This makes central chemoreceptors, responding to carbon dioxide, the dominant driver of breath-to-breath ventilation under essentially all normal conditions.

Peripheral chemoreceptors, located in the carotid bodies (at the carotid artery bifurcations in the neck) and the aortic bodies, respond primarily to oxygen -- but only when oxygen falls substantially, not to small fluctuations within the normal range. This is a genuinely different sensitivity profile from the central system's fine-grained response to carbon dioxide, and it means oxygen functions more as a backup alarm for genuinely low blood oxygen than as the primary, continuous regulator of everyday breathing. The popular framing of oxygen as the main trigger for breathing gets this backward: under ordinary conditions, it is carbon dioxide, sensed centrally, that is doing almost all of the moment-to-moment work.

Sleep apnea illustrates how this control system, or the airway it operates, can fail in genuinely distinct ways that happen to look superficially similar. In obstructive sleep apnea, the respiratory control system itself is functioning entirely normally -- the problem is mechanical, the upper airway's muscle tone relaxing during sleep to the point that the airway physically collapses and blocks airflow. The resulting rise in carbon dioxide is detected exactly as it should be by central chemoreceptors, which trigger a brief arousal from sleep sufficient to restore airway muscle tone and resume breathing, a cycle that repeats many times a night. In central sleep apnea, by contrast, the airway itself remains open, but the brainstem's respiratory drive intermittently fails to generate a breathing signal at all, so no chemoreceptor feedback loop is being ignored -- the signal to breathe simply isn't being sent. Distinguishing these two mechanisms matters because "interrupted breathing during sleep" is the shared symptom of two genuinely different failure points in the same overall control system.
