Chronic kidney disease is set in motion by a wide range of long-term causes -- diabetes and hypertension are by far the most common, but chronic glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, and repeated episodes of acute kidney injury can all initiate it as well -- but understanding why the disease so often continues progressing regardless of which cause started it requires looking past the original trigger and toward how the kidney's remaining tissue responds to nephron loss.

Nephrons are lost gradually as the underlying cause does its damage, but the kidney does not simply function at a reduced capacity proportional to how many nephrons remain. The surviving nephrons compensate actively: they undergo hyperfiltration, individually filtering at a higher rate than normal to help make up for the lost filtering capacity elsewhere in the kidney. This compensation is remarkably effective in the short term -- overall kidney function, as measured by standard clinical tests, can remain close to normal even after a substantial fraction of total nephrons has already been lost, which is precisely why chronic kidney disease is so often diagnosed only after it has already progressed considerably; the compensatory mechanism masks the underlying loss for years.

The mechanism that makes the disease progressive, however, is that hyperfiltration is not a cost-free adaptation. The elevated pressure and flow each surviving nephron experiences while compensating is itself a form of chronic mechanical stress, and over time it damages the very nephrons performing the compensation. As hyperfiltrating nephrons themselves begin to fail, an even smaller number of nephrons must compensate for a now-larger deficit, forcing them into even more aggressive hyperfiltration, which damages them faster in turn. This is a genuinely self-perpetuating cycle: nephron loss drives compensatory hyperfiltration, which drives further nephron loss, independent of whatever originally started the process.

This is exactly why chronic kidney disease frequently continues to progress even after its original cause has been brought under excellent control -- controlling blood sugar or blood pressure addresses the initial trigger, but it does not, by itself, interrupt a hyperfiltration cycle that has already become self-sustaining among the surviving nephrons. Understanding this distinction between the disease's original cause and its self-perpetuating progression mechanism is central to understanding why chronic kidney disease behaves the way it does, rather than simply mirroring the severity of whatever condition initiated it.
