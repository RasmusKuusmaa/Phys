Most communication between neurons switches medium partway through the journey: an electrical impulse travels down the axon, but at the synapse it becomes a chemical signal before becoming electrical again in the next cell. The switch happens because an arriving action potential opens voltage-gated calcium channels at the axon terminal; the resulting calcium influx is the trigger that causes neurotransmitter-filled vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release their contents into the synaptic cleft, the narrow gap separating the two cells.

What that released neurotransmitter does next is entirely a matter of which receptor it finds on the other side, not some inherent property of the molecule. Glutamate is the nervous system's dominant excitatory neurotransmitter, generally depolarizing the postsynaptic membrane and pushing it toward its own firing threshold; GABA plays the opposite dominant role, generally hyperpolarizing the membrane and pushing it further from threshold. But this excitatory/inhibitory outcome is a property of the receptor-neurotransmitter pairing, not the neurotransmitter alone -- acetylcholine, for example, excites skeletal muscle at nicotinic receptors but inhibits heart muscle at a different receptor type entirely, which is exactly why the same molecule can have opposite effects depending on where it acts.

A postsynaptic neuron typically receives thousands of synaptic inputs simultaneously, some excitatory and some inhibitory, and whether it fires its own action potential depends on the net sum of all of that input reaching threshold at the axon hillock, not any single synapse's activity in isolation -- this summation is what lets a neuron integrate information from many sources rather than simply relaying one input.

A small minority of synapses skip the chemical step entirely: electrical synapses connect two cells' membranes directly through gap junctions, channels large enough to let ions flow straight from one cell's cytoplasm to the next. This sacrifices the flexibility that chemical modulation provides (a chemical synapse's strength can be adjusted; an electrical synapse's essentially can't) in exchange for speed -- useful in circuits where a group of neurons needs to fire in near-perfect synchrony, since there's no synaptic delay to introduce timing variability.
