Ordinary cell division (mitosis) exists to produce two genetically identical daughter cells for growth and tissue repair, but producing sperm and egg cells demands something different: each gamete must carry only half the normal chromosome number, so that fertilization restores the full complement rather than doubling it every generation. Meiosis accomplishes this through two successive divisions. In meiosis I, homologous chromosome pairs -- one copy inherited from each parent -- first pair up and then separate into different daughter cells, halving the chromosome number; meiosis II then resembles an ordinary mitotic division, splitting each of those cells' sister chromatids apart, for a final yield of four haploid cells from one starting diploid cell.

The genetically interesting part happens early, during meiosis I, before the chromosome number is even halved. While homologous chromosomes are paired up, they don't just sit side by side -- they physically exchange corresponding segments of DNA in a process called crossing over, so each chromosome that eventually gets passed to a gamete is often a genuine patchwork of both grandparents' original DNA, not an intact copy of either one. Separately, when homologous pairs are then pulled apart into different daughter cells, which member of each pair (maternal or paternal in origin) ends up in which daughter cell is independent of how every other chromosome pair was distributed -- independent assortment -- which alone already generates millions of possible combinations across the 23 human chromosome pairs before crossing over adds even more variation on top.

The combined effect of crossing over and independent assortment is that every gamete a person produces is, apart from statistically negligible exceptions, genetically unique -- no two sperm cells from the same man, and no two egg cells from the same woman, carry an identical set of chromosomes. This is the direct mechanistic reason full siblings, despite sharing the same two parents, inherit different combinations of grandparental genetic material and end up sharing only about half of their variable DNA on average, rather than being genetic copies of one another the way mitotic daughter cells are copies of their parent cell.
