The dopamine hypothesis is one of psychiatry's oldest and best-evidenced mechanistic models, built on a genuinely striking convergence of evidence. Drugs that increase dopaminergic activity -- amphetamines and other stimulants -- can, at sufficiently high doses or with chronic use, induce psychotic symptoms (paranoia, hallucinations) in people with no prior psychiatric history, which is hard to explain unless excess dopamine signaling itself is capable of producing these symptoms. On the treatment side, essentially every antipsychotic medication shares a common mechanism -- blocking dopamine D2 receptors -- and a decades-old finding (the correlation between a drug's clinical potency and its D2-receptor-blocking affinity) remains one of the cleaner dose-response relationships in psychiatric pharmacology. Both lines of evidence point toward excess dopamine signaling, specifically in the mesolimbic pathway (running from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens and related limbic structures), as a genuine driver of positive symptoms -- hallucinations and delusions.

Where the model runs into trouble is with everything else a psychotic disorder typically includes. Negative symptoms (flattened emotional expression, reduced motivation, social withdrawal) and cognitive symptoms (impaired working memory, attention, executive function) don't respond well to dopamine-blocking treatment and, mechanistically, appear to involve the opposite problem in a different location: reduced dopamine and glutamate signaling specifically within the prefrontal cortex, rather than an excess anywhere. This creates a genuinely awkward clinical reality -- the same broad D2-receptor blockade that quiets an overactive mesolimbic pathway does nothing to correct an underactive prefrontal one, and in some cases can worsen prefrontal function further, since dopamine signaling there is already too low rather than too high.

This regional split -- too much dopamine in one circuit, too little in another -- is a genuinely useful way to understand why psychotic disorders present such a mixed treatment picture: a medication regimen can meaningfully help one symptom domain while leaving another largely untouched, not because the medication has failed, but because it's addressing only one half of a two-part, opposite-direction problem. It's also why the dopamine hypothesis, despite its real predictive successes for positive symptoms, is understood within the field as a genuinely partial account rather than a complete explanation of what causes psychotic disorders in the first place.
