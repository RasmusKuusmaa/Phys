The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis follows the same basic wiring diagram as the thyroid and adrenal axes covered elsewhere in this module: a hypothalamic releasing hormone (GnRH, gonadotropin-releasing hormone) stimulates the anterior pituitary to release two tropic hormones, LH and FSH, which travel to the gonads and stimulate them to do two things at once -- produce sex steroid hormones (testosterone in the testes; estrogen and progesterone in the ovaries) and support the maturation of gametes (sperm or eggs). This is the same releasing-hormone-to-tropic-hormone-to-target-gland pattern already seen with TRH/TSH/thyroid and CRH/ACTH/adrenal cortex, just applied to a different downstream target.

What differs sharply between the sexes is not the architecture but the pattern of output it produces. In the male HPG axis, LH and FSH are released in a relatively steady, continuous pattern, driving ongoing testosterone production and continuous sperm development without a strong cyclical rhythm. In the female HPG axis, the same hormones drive a genuinely cyclical pattern spanning roughly a month: estrogen and progesterone levels rise and fall in a coordinated sequence tied to the maturation and release of an egg.

The mechanism behind that cyclical pattern is the single most distinctive feature of the female HPG axis, and it breaks the usual endocrine rule that feedback is always negative. For most of the menstrual cycle, rising estrogen does exactly what negative feedback predicts -- it suppresses GnRH, LH, and FSH release, the same pattern seen in every other axis in this module. But when estrogen rises high enough and stays there long enough (late in the follicular phase, as a dominant ovarian follicle matures), the feedback relationship briefly reverses: instead of suppressing the pituitary, sustained high estrogen triggers a sudden, large surge in LH release. That LH surge is what triggers ovulation, the release of the mature egg from the ovary. Once ovulation occurs and the cycle moves into its second half, feedback reverts to the standard negative pattern, with rising progesterone (and estrogen) from the resulting corpus luteum once again suppressing GnRH/LH/FSH.

This positive-feedback exception is worth understanding specifically because it is genuinely rare in physiology -- most homeostatic hormone systems in the body, including every other axis in this module, rely entirely on negative feedback to stay within a stable range; a system that briefly amplifies its own signal, as the female HPG axis does around ovulation, is a deliberate, time-limited departure from that default, not evidence that reproductive hormones follow some entirely separate set of rules.
