Every time a muscle fiber contracts, its internal contractile machinery generates force that has to be transmitted, safely, to the cell membrane and beyond it to the surrounding connective tissue. Dystrophin is one of the key proteins that makes this safe transmission possible: it forms part of a structural complex anchoring the internal actin cytoskeleton to the cell membrane, spreading contractile force across the membrane rather than letting it concentrate at vulnerable points. In Duchenne muscular dystrophy, a genetic mutation means dystrophin is missing or produced in a non-functional form from birth, so every contraction transmits force in a way the membrane isn't built to handle -- repeated over thousands of contractions, this tears the membrane at a microscopic level, and a muscle fiber that has sustained enough of this damage eventually dies and is replaced by fibrous and fatty tissue rather than functional muscle.

This is a fundamentally structural problem: the muscle fiber never had the protein it needed, so the damage accumulates predictably over years as an inevitable consequence of ordinary muscle use, following a fairly consistent pattern tied to which muscle groups place the heaviest mechanical demand on the missing protein.

Inflammatory myopathies work through an entirely different mechanism. In polymyositis, for instance, the immune system -- through a process related to the same loss of self-tolerance seen in other autoimmune diseases -- directly attacks muscle fibers, with immune cells infiltrating muscle tissue and causing inflammation-driven fiber damage. There is no missing structural protein here; the muscle fiber is built normally, but an active immune process is damaging it from outside.

This distinction is exactly why the two disease categories respond so differently to treatment. Suppressing the immune system can reduce or halt the ongoing attack in an inflammatory myopathy, because there is an active immune process to suppress. In Duchenne muscular dystrophy, immunosuppression has nothing to act on: the muscle fiber's problem isn't an immune attack, it's the complete inherited absence of a protein the cell was never able to make, so no amount of dampening immune activity addresses the actual mechanism driving the disease.
