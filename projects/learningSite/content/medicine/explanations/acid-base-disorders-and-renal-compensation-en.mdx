Blood pH depends on a ratio between two quantities controlled by two entirely different organ systems: carbon dioxide, an acid whose blood level the lungs control by adjusting ventilation, and bicarbonate, a base whose blood level the kidneys control by adjusting how much they reabsorb versus excrete. This division is exactly what defines the two broad categories of acid-base disorder.

A respiratory acid-base disorder begins with a problem on the ventilation side of this equation, the same territory already covered in this subject's respiratory-failure-and-gas-exchange-abnormalities concept. Inadequate overall ventilation lets carbon dioxide accumulate, producing respiratory acidosis; excessive ventilation clears carbon dioxide too aggressively, producing respiratory alkalosis. A metabolic acid-base disorder instead begins with a problem entirely outside the lungs -- an abnormal bicarbonate level, or an abnormal load of some other (non-carbonic) acid, arising from causes like kidney dysfunction, severe gastrointestinal fluid loss, or a metabolic condition generating excess acid.

Whichever side is disrupted first, the organ system that was not originally the problem attempts to compensate, using whatever mechanism it actually has available. Facing a primary metabolic disorder, the lungs compensate by adjusting the rate and depth of breathing -- hyperventilating to blow off more carbon dioxide and help correct a metabolic acidosis, or hypoventilating to retain more carbon dioxide in response to a metabolic alkalosis. Because this only requires adjusting an already-continuous, moment-to-moment process, respiratory compensation begins within minutes of the primary disturbance.

Facing a primary respiratory disorder, the kidneys compensate by adjusting bicarbonate handling -- reabsorbing more bicarbonate (or generating more through cellular processes) to help correct a respiratory acidosis, or excreting more to help correct a respiratory alkalosis. This compensation depends on gradually altering the activity of tubular transport processes rather than adjusting something already in continuous motion, which is why renal compensation for a respiratory disorder characteristically takes hours to days to fully develop rather than happening immediately.

This asymmetry in compensatory speed is not just a mechanistic curiosity -- it carries real diagnostic information. Because renal compensation is slow, the degree to which it has actually developed in a given patient with a respiratory disorder reflects how long that disorder has been present: full compensation indicates a chronic, long-standing problem that has had time for the kidneys to catch up, while an incompletely developed compensatory response points toward a more acute, recently developed disturbance that the slower renal mechanism simply hasn't had time to fully address yet.
