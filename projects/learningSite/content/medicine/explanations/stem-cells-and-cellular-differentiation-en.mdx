Every cell in an adult human body traces back to the same single fertilized egg, yet a neuron, a red blood cell, and a liver cell look and behave nothing alike. The resolution to that puzzle is that differentiation does not change a cell's genome; it changes which parts of that shared genome stay accessible and active. Genes not needed for a given cell type are silenced through stable, heritable chemical modifications to the DNA and the proteins packaging it (collectively called epigenetic marks) rather than being physically removed, which is why virtually every cell in the body still carries a complete copy of the genome even though each cell type reads only a fraction of it.

Stem cells are the cells capable of both maintaining themselves and generating this diversity, and they are defined by two properties that have to be present together: self-renewal, the ability to divide and produce daughter cells that are themselves still stem cells, and potency, the range of specialized cell types their other daughter cells can still become. Potency is not one fixed category but a spectrum. A fertilized egg and the handful of cells produced by its first few divisions are totipotent, able to generate every cell type in the resulting organism plus the extra-embryonic structures (like the placenta) that support development but aren't part of the body itself. A few days later, the inner cell mass of the developing embryo consists of pluripotent cells, still able to become essentially any cell type of the body proper but no longer able to form those extra-embryonic tissues. Later still, most stem cells that persist into adulthood are multipotent, restricted to producing a related family of cell types -- hematopoietic stem cells in bone marrow, for instance, continuously generate the full range of blood cell types (red cells, the various white cell lineages, platelets) but nothing outside that blood lineage.

Under normal conditions, differentiation is a one-way trip: once a cell has committed to a specialized fate, its descendants inherit that same restricted gene-expression pattern indefinitely, which is exactly why a skin cell's progeny stay skin cells and a liver cell's progeny stay liver cells. This made the 2006 discovery that a fully differentiated adult cell could be experimentally reprogrammed back into a pluripotent state -- by forcing expression of a small set of transcription factor genes, producing what are called induced pluripotent stem cells -- such a significant result. It demonstrated directly that the restricted, silenced state of a differentiated cell's genome, while stable under ordinary circumstances, is a maintained regulatory state rather than a permanent, irreversible change to the DNA itself, opening an entire field of regenerative-medicine research aimed at generating patient-specific cell types from a patient's own already-differentiated cells.
