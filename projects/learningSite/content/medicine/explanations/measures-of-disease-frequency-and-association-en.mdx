Incidence and prevalence sound similar but answer genuinely different questions, and confusing them leads directly to misreading how serious a disease actually is. Incidence is forward-looking: it counts new cases arising in a population that starts out disease-free, over some defined stretch of time, and it is what you want to know if you're asking "how fast is this disease spreading" or "how risky is this exposure going forward." Prevalence is a snapshot: it counts everyone who currently has the disease, whether they got it yesterday or ten years ago, at one moment in time. A disease with low incidence but long duration -- something people are diagnosed with rarely but then live with for decades -- accumulates a high prevalence precisely because existing cases keep piling up faster than they resolve, while a disease with high incidence but short duration (people get it often but recover or die quickly) can have surprisingly low prevalence at any given moment, since cases don't stick around to be counted.

Relative risk turns incidence into a direct comparison. Once you know the incidence in an exposed group and the incidence in an unexposed group, dividing one by the other gives a single number that says, immediately, how many times more (or less) often the exposed group develops the disease. A relative risk of 1 is the null result -- exposure and outcome appear unrelated in this data -- while numbers above or below 1 indicate increased or decreased risk respectively, in direct proportion to how far from 1 they sit.

Odds ratio measures something related but subtly different: instead of comparing risks (proportions of a group who develop the disease), it compares odds (the ratio of people who develop the disease to people who don't, within each group). For rare diseases, these two calculations converge, because when very few people in either group develop the disease, "risk" and "odds" are numerically almost the same thing. As the disease becomes more common, though, the two measures pull apart, with the odds ratio typically overstating the relative risk. This isn't just a mathematical curiosity -- it directly explains why case-control studies, which structurally can only compute an odds ratio (since they select participants by disease status, not by exposure, so true incidence in each group can't be measured), give a number that needs to be interpreted differently than the relative risk a cohort study reports for the same exposure and disease.
