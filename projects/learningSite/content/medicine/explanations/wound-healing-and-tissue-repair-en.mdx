Which of two very different repair strategies a tissue uses after injury comes down to one property of its cells: can they still divide? Tissues built from cells that retain the capacity to proliferate -- the liver's hepatocytes, the skin's basal epithelial cells, the lining of the gut -- can achieve true regeneration, replacing lost cells with new ones of the identical type, fully restoring both the tissue's structure and its original specialized function. The liver's capacity to regrow a substantial fraction of its mass after surgical removal is the most dramatic example, but the same principle is at work every time a shallow skin scrape heals without leaving a mark. Tissues built from cells that have permanently exited the cell cycle -- mature cardiac muscle, skeletal muscle to a large degree, and neurons -- have no such option. When these cells die, in numbers, the tissue's only available repair strategy is fibrosis: replacing the gap with new, dense, collagen-rich connective tissue laid down by fibroblasts. A fibrous scar is a genuine repair in the sense that it restores mechanical integrity and seals the defect, but it cannot replicate the specialized function of the tissue it replaces -- scar tissue in a heart wall does not contract, and a large enough scar can measurably impair the heart's overall pumping function even once the "wound" itself has fully healed by every structural measure.

Whichever strategy a given tissue ultimately uses, the process that gets it there follows a broadly conserved sequence of overlapping phases. Hemostasis is essentially immediate: within minutes of injury, platelets aggregate at the site and the clotting cascade forms a fibrin clot that stops bleeding and provides a provisional scaffold. Inflammation follows over the next several days, recruiting the immune cells that clear debris, dead cells, and any invading pathogens -- the same vascular and cellular events covered by inflammation as its own topic, here specifically in service of clearing the way for repair. Proliferation then dominates over roughly the following one to three weeks: fibroblasts migrate in and begin laying down new collagen, new blood vessels sprout to supply the regenerating or repairing tissue (a process called angiogenesis), and in skin wounds specifically, epithelial cells at the wound edge migrate across the surface to re-establish a continuous covering. Finally, remodeling unfolds over months: the initially disorganized, weak collagen deposited during proliferation is progressively broken down and re-laid in a stronger, more organized, cross-linked pattern -- though even a fully remodeled scar typically reaches only about 70-80% of the tensile strength of the original, uninjured tissue, never quite matching it.

This sequence is not automatic or unstoppable, and several well-recognized factors can derail it -- almost always by stalling the process in the inflammatory phase rather than letting it progress on schedule. Persistently poor blood supply starves the healing tissue of the oxygen and nutrients proliferation requires. An unresolved infection keeps the immune system locked in an ongoing inflammatory response rather than allowing the transition to proliferation. Retained foreign material (a suture, a splinter, debris) gives the immune system something it can never fully clear. And systemic conditions like diabetes impair both blood supply (through vascular damage) and immune cell function simultaneously, which is why diabetic patients are especially prone to chronic, non-healing wounds -- a wound stuck indefinitely in inflammation, rather than a wound that is simply healing more slowly than usual.
