Bacteria are prokaryotes, and that single word carries a lot of structural weight: no nucleus, so their single circular chromosome sits directly in the cytoplasm, physically accessible in a way a eukaryotic cell's chromosomes never are; no membrane-bound organelles, so there is no mitochondrion, no endoplasmic reticulum, no Golgi apparatus dividing the cell into separate compartments the way a eukaryotic cell is divided. Everything a bacterium needs to do -- generate energy, build proteins, replicate its DNA -- happens in one open cytoplasmic space, using machinery that is often structurally different enough from its eukaryotic equivalent that a drug can target the bacterial version specifically.

The cell wall is where bacterial classification does its most practical work. Peptidoglycan, a mesh-like polymer of sugars and amino acids, gives the bacterial cell wall its rigidity and protects the cell from bursting under its own internal osmotic pressure. Gram-positive bacteria build a thick wall almost entirely out of peptidoglycan, layered many molecules deep, with no additional membrane outside it. Gram-negative bacteria build a much thinner peptidoglycan layer, but wrap it in a second lipid membrane on the outside -- the outer membrane -- studded with pore-forming proteins that tightly control which molecules can get through. This is not a cosmetic difference: the Gram stain test (crystal violet dye, an iodine fixative, an alcohol wash, then a pink safranin counterstain) works precisely because a thick peptidoglycan layer traps the violet dye complex inside during the alcohol wash, while a thin peptidoglycan layer with an outer membrane lets it wash straight out, leaving only the pink counterstain visible. That same outer membrane is also a real pharmacological barrier -- many antibiotics that diffuse easily through a Gram-positive cell wall are blocked or slowed by the Gram-negative outer membrane, which is why a bacterium's Gram-stain category is one of the very first pieces of information used to narrow down an appropriate antibiotic.

Beyond the wall, bacteria carry a set of optional structures deployed only when useful to a particular species or strain. Flagella are long, rotating protein filaments used for propulsion, letting a bacterium swim toward more favorable conditions. Pili are shorter, hair-like projections used for attaching to surfaces (including host tissue, in a pathogen) and, in some bacteria, for direct DNA transfer to another bacterial cell during conjugation. Some species also produce a polysaccharide capsule surrounding the whole cell, which can interfere with a host immune cell's ability to physically engulf the bacterium. Plasmids, small circular DNA molecules entirely separate from the main chromosome, round out the toolkit: a bacterium can carry several plasmids at once, replicate them independently of its own cell division, and transfer copies directly to other bacteria via conjugation -- a mechanism that lets a useful trait like antibiotic resistance move sideways between bacteria, including across species boundaries, far faster than ordinary vertical inheritance from parent to offspring ever could.
