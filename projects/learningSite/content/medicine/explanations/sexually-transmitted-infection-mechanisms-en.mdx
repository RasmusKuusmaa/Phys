Sexually transmitted infections aren't a single biological category -- they're a transmission route (genital, anal, or oral sexual contact) shared by pathogens from all three major categories covered elsewhere in this subject's microbiology content, and each category establishes infection in the reproductive tract through a genuinely different mechanism.

Bacterial STIs, such as Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae, typically begin as a mucosal infection of the urethra, cervix, or rectum. The immediate local infection is often what gets diagnosed and treated, but if left untreated, both organisms can ascend from the lower reproductive tract into the upper tract -- the uterus and fallopian tubes -- where the resulting inflammatory response, not the bacteria themselves directly, causes scarring. That scarring is what can physically block the fallopian tubes, a mechanistic link from an initially minor or even asymptomatic infection to later infertility, entirely separate from whatever symptoms (if any) the original infection produced.

Viral STIs illustrate just how different 'viral infection' can look mechanistically from one virus to the next. HIV specifically targets CD4+ T cells, a category of immune cell central to coordinating the whole adaptive immune response, and progressively destroys them over years, which is why HIV infection itself isn't the direct cause of illness so much as the resulting collapse of immune function is. Herpes simplex virus takes a completely different path: after an initial infection, it establishes lifelong latency inside the cell bodies of sensory neurons, essentially hiding from ongoing immune surveillance, and periodically reactivates to cause recurrent outbreaks throughout a person's life -- it is never cleared the way many other viral infections are. HPV (human papillomavirus) takes yet another path: it infects basal epithelial cells and, for a subset of high-risk strains, disrupts the normal controls on cell proliferation, driving progressively abnormal cell growth that, over years, can progress to cervical or other reproductive-tract cancers if the abnormal cells aren't detected and removed.

Protozoal STIs, such as Trichomonas vaginalis, work through a more direct mechanism than either of the above: the organism itself causes mucosal irritation and inflammation through its motility and attachment to epithelial cells, without the latency herpes shows or the systemic immune involvement HIV shows.

Across all three categories, a critically important and counterintuitive fact holds: a large proportion of infected people, for many of the most common STIs, have no noticeable symptoms at all, especially in the earlier stages of infection. This is precisely why routine screening (rather than symptom-triggered testing alone) matters for sexually active people, and why an absence of symptoms in oneself or a partner cannot be used as evidence of an absence of infection or transmission risk.
