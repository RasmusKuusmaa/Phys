Metabolic and nutritional diseases fall into two mechanistically opposite categories that can nonetheless converge on an identical clinical picture, which makes distinguishing them one of the more important diagnostic distinctions in this area of medicine. A classic nutrient deficiency arises when an essential nutrient -- something the body cannot make itself and must obtain from the diet -- is simply not present in sufficient quantity, so any biochemical pathway that depends on it as a cofactor or building block stalls for lack of raw material. Vitamin C is the textbook case: it is a required cofactor for the enzymes that stabilize collagen's triple-helix structure, and without it, existing collagen degrades faster than it can be replaced, producing scurvy's connective-tissue breakdown, bleeding gums, and poor wound healing.

An inborn error of metabolism produces the same kind of pathway failure through the opposite mechanism. The substrate itself -- the nutrient the pathway needs to process -- is often present in completely normal, even abundant, quantities in the diet. What is missing is a functioning enzyme: a mutation disables one specific catalytic step, so the pathway backs up precisely at that point, and the substance that should have been processed accumulates instead, often to toxic levels. Phenylketonuria is the classic illustration: a mutation disables the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase, so the essential amino acid phenylalanine -- present in completely ordinary amounts in a normal diet -- cannot be converted to tyrosine as it normally would be, and instead builds up to concentrations that damage the developing brain.

This mechanistic difference is exactly why the two conditions are managed in opposite ways. A true deficiency disease responds to simply supplying more of the missing nutrient -- vitamin C cures scurvy directly. An inborn error of metabolism, by contrast, is made worse by supplying more of the substance the broken pathway cannot process; the actual treatment is to restrict dietary intake of that specific substance (a low-phenylalanine diet, in PKU's case), reducing the raw material reaching the blocked step so it cannot accumulate to harmful levels, rather than trying to force a broken enzyme to keep up with an unrestricted supply.
