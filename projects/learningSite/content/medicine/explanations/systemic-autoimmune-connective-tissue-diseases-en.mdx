Autoimmune disease, as a category, is defined by a shared origin: self-reactive lymphocytes that should have been eliminated or suppressed instead escape control and attack the body's own tissue. But sharing that origin says nothing about what happens next, and lupus and systemic sclerosis are a clean illustration of how differently two diseases in the same category can actually behave once the underlying immune target and downstream mechanism diverge.

In lupus, the immune system produces autoantibodies against components of the cell nucleus -- DNA, histones, and other nuclear proteins. Because every nucleated cell in the body contains these targets, the resulting autoantibody-antigen immune complexes can form and deposit essentially anywhere immune complexes tend to settle: in the kidneys' filtering structures, in joint tissue, in small blood vessels throughout the skin, even in the nervous system. Wherever a complex deposits, it triggers local inflammation, which is exactly why lupus's presentation varies so much from one patient to the next -- the specific organs affected depend on where, by chance, complexes happened to accumulate in that particular patient, not on some fixed, predictable disease pattern.

Systemic sclerosis starts from the same category of broken self-tolerance but produces damage through an entirely different downstream process. Here, autoimmune activity triggers chronic injury to small blood vessels, and the tissue's response to that ongoing vascular injury is to lay down excessive collagen -- a fibrotic, scar-tissue-like response that, if it happened only once in one place, would simply be normal wound healing. But because the injury is chronic and driven by an ongoing immune process rather than a single wound, the fibrotic response never stops and never stays localized: it progressively stiffens and thickens the skin (the most visible sign) but also affects the lungs, the GI tract's smooth muscle, and the kidneys' vasculature, wherever the underlying chronic vascular injury is occurring.

The two diseases therefore end up looking similar in one respect -- both are genuinely systemic, multi-organ conditions -- for two mechanistically unrelated reasons: lupus because its molecular target is ubiquitous, systemic sclerosis because its fibrotic response follows wherever chronic vascular injury occurs. Treating either disease effectively requires acting on its own specific downstream mechanism, not a generic "autoimmune disease" treatment that assumes the two conditions work the same way just because both started from the same category of immune failure.
