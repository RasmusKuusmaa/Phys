The functional unit that does all of the kidney's actual work is the nephron, and roughly a million of them operate in parallel within each kidney, each one running the same three-step process on the blood flowing through it.

Filtration happens first, at the glomerulus, a specialized tuft of capillaries under unusually high pressure. That pressure forces water and small dissolved substances -- glucose, amino acids, electrolytes, urea, and other small molecules -- out of the blood and into the nephron's tubule, while blood cells and large proteins are too big to cross the filtration barrier and stay in the blood. This produces a large volume of filtrate that is, at this stage, essentially blood plasma minus its cells and proteins.

If the story ended there, the kidneys would be discarding enormous quantities of water and useful solutes continuously, which is why reabsorption is just as essential as filtration. As filtrate travels along the tubule, specialized transport mechanisms in the tubule wall reclaim the large majority of the filtered water, essentially all of the filtered glucose and amino acids under normal conditions, and most of the filtered electrolytes, moving them back into the blood. Only a small fraction of what was originally filtered survives this reabsorption process to continue toward becoming urine.

Secretion adds one further layer of control: the tubule can also actively transport a handful of additional substances directly from the blood into the forming urine, substances that were not necessarily filtered at the glomerulus in the first place. What ultimately remains after reabsorption has reclaimed nearly everything useful and secretion has added its final contributions is urine, which drains into the renal pelvis, travels down the ureter to the bladder for temporary storage, and exits the body through the urethra.

The reason this three-step system matters for far more than waste elimination is that reabsorption and secretion are not fixed, all-or-nothing processes -- they are continuously and precisely adjustable. By varying exactly how much of each filtered substance gets reabsorbed versus allowed to pass into the urine, the same nephrons that eliminate metabolic waste also perform the kidney's other essential regulatory roles: maintaining the body's overall fluid volume, keeping individual electrolyte concentrations (sodium, potassium, calcium, and others) within their normal ranges, and, as later concepts in this module will build on, participating directly in regulating the blood's acid-base balance.
