A mass balance starts from one accounting statement, applied to whatever boundary an engineer draws around a piece of equipment or an entire process: accumulation inside the boundary equals everything entering minus everything leaving. For a continuous process running at steady state -- the condition almost every industrial unit is designed to operate at during normal production -- nothing is building up or draining away inside the boundary, so accumulation is exactly zero, and the balance simplifies to "in equals out." That simplification is what makes steady-state process analysis tractable: an engineer can write one equation per stream and solve a whole flowsheet's worth of unknown flow rates and compositions.

The balance becomes genuinely useful once it's applied per component rather than just to total mass. Total mass in a non-reacting mixing process is conserved almost by definition, but the actual engineering question is rarely "how much total mass is in this stream" -- it's "how much of this specific reactant, product, or contaminant is here," and each component needs its own conservation equation, since a component can be created or destroyed by chemical reaction even while total mass across the whole system stays fixed.

A mixing point is the simplest possible illustration of both the balance and the mistake people most often make with it. When two streams of different concentration combine, the combined flow rate is simply the sum of the two inlet flow rates -- straightforward total-mass conservation. But the combined concentration is not the simple average of the two inlet concentrations; it is a flow-weighted average, where each stream's concentration is weighted by how much of the total combined flow that stream actually contributes. A small flow of concentrated solution mixed into a much larger flow of dilute solution barely moves the result away from the dilute stream's own concentration -- exactly the outcome component-by-component mass conservation predicts, and exactly what a naive unweighted average gets wrong.
