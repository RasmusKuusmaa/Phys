A catalyst's defining feature is that it participates in a reaction's mechanism without being consumed by it: it provides an alternative reaction pathway, typically through forming a temporary intermediate with one of the reactants, and that alternative pathway has a lower activation energy than the uncatalyzed route. Because reaction rate depends steeply on activation energy, even a modest reduction can speed up a reaction by many orders of magnitude, and the catalyst is regenerated at the end of the cycle, ready to repeat the process with the next reactant molecule. It's worth being precise about what this does and doesn't change: a catalyst lowers the activation energy for both the forward and reverse reactions equally, so it speeds up how quickly a reversible reaction reaches equilibrium without shifting the equilibrium position itself -- equilibrium yield is a thermodynamic quantity, set by the relative stability of reactants and products, and completely untouched by how the reaction gets there kinetically.

Catalysts split into two categories based on phase, and the split represents a genuine engineering tradeoff rather than an arbitrary classification. A homogeneous catalyst exists in the same phase as the reactants -- most often dissolved in the same liquid -- which gives it excellent, uniform contact with every reactant molecule and typically very high activity and selectivity. The cost is separation: once the reaction is done, pulling the dissolved catalyst back out of the product stream is often difficult and expensive, and any catalyst lost in that separation is catalyst that has to be replaced. A heterogeneous catalyst instead exists in a different phase from the reactants -- almost always a solid catalyzing a gas-phase or liquid-phase reaction -- which confines the reaction to the solid's surface (only surface atoms can participate, not the catalyst's full volume, generally reducing per-mass activity), but makes separation trivial: the solid catalyst simply stays behind as gas or liquid product flows past or through it, and can be reused for a long service life.

The Haber-Bosch process is the field's landmark illustration of catalysis mattering at civilizational scale, not merely industrial scale. Atmospheric nitrogen's triple bond is extraordinarily strong, and the uncatalyzed reaction between nitrogen and hydrogen to form ammonia is negligibly slow at any temperature an industrial reactor could economically operate at. An iron-based heterogeneous catalyst provides the alternative pathway that makes the reaction proceed at a usable rate under achievable industrial conditions, and that single catalytic discovery is what made synthetic nitrogen fertilizer -- and the roughly half of current global food production that depends on it -- possible at all.
