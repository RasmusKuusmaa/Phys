Reaction engineering takes the rate law chemistry already establishes and asks a design question chemistry alone doesn't answer: what physical reactor configuration turns that intrinsic rate into the highest possible conversion for a given size and feed rate. Three idealized reactor types anchor the field, and comparing them reveals exactly what a reactor's internal flow pattern is actually doing to the reacting mixture's concentration.

A batch reactor is conceptually the simplest: a fixed charge of reactants goes in, the reaction runs for a chosen time with no material entering or leaving, and every molecule in the vessel experiences the same reaction history. Conversion is controlled entirely by how long the batch is left to react, and the concentration inside falls over time exactly the way a chemist's rate law predicts for a closed system.

A continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR) trades that simplicity for continuous operation, at a real cost. Feed enters and product leaves continuously, and the vessel is mixed thoroughly enough that the concentration is uniform throughout -- which means the concentration everywhere inside the tank, and therefore in the outlet stream itself, is already the low, reacted-down concentration a batch reactor would only reach near the end of its run. Because reaction rate typically depends on concentration, a CSTR's entire volume reacts at this comparatively slow, diluted rate.

A plug flow reactor (PFR) avoids that penalty by preventing back-mixing entirely: reactants move through a tube as a series of thin "plugs" that don't mix with fluid ahead of or behind them, so concentration falls continuously along the tube's length the same way it falls over time in a batch reactor. Near the inlet, concentration -- and therefore rate -- is high; only near the outlet does it fall to the low value a CSTR imposes everywhere at once. For any reaction whose rate increases with concentration, this means a PFR reaches a higher conversion than a CSTR of identical volume and feed rate, simply because more of the PFR's volume gets to react at a faster rate before the concentration drops. This single comparison -- what fraction of a reactor's volume operates at high vs. low concentration -- is the central design tradeoff reaction engineering is built around.
