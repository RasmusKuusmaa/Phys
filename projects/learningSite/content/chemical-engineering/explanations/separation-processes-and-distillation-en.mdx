A chemical reactor almost never produces a pure output, which is exactly why separation processes exist as their own major branch of chemical engineering, distinct from reaction engineering: the job is to take a mixture and split it into purified streams by exploiting some genuine physical or chemical difference between the components, rather than any single universal method that works on everything.

Distillation is the field's default workhorse because volatility differences are common and the underlying physics is simple: when a liquid mixture is partially vaporized, the vapor produced is richer in whichever component boils at a lower temperature, since that component preferentially escapes into the vapor phase. A single vaporization step only shifts the composition modestly, though -- nowhere near pure. A real distillation column instead stacks many internal stages (trays, or packing material that serves the same purpose), and at each stage the rising vapor and falling liquid contact each other and re-equilibrate, repeating the same modest volatility-driven enrichment over and over. Stacking enough of these stages compounds a small per-stage effect into a sharp separation, concentrating the more volatile component almost entirely at the top of the column and the less volatile one almost entirely at the bottom.

Extraction and absorption both work on the same underlying logic -- solubility differences rather than volatility -- but apply to different phase combinations. Extraction introduces a second liquid solvent, chosen because one component of the original mixture is far more soluble in it than in the original liquid, so that component transfers preferentially into the new solvent phase, which can then be physically separated (decanted) from the original liquid. Absorption applies the identical solubility-difference idea to a gas mixture instead: a liquid is chosen because one specific gas component dissolves into it far more readily than the rest of the gas does, pulling that component out of the gas stream and into the liquid. Recognizing which physical property (volatility vs. solubility) and which phase pairing (liquid-vapor, liquid-liquid, or gas-liquid) actually distinguishes the components you need to separate is the first and most important design decision in any real separation problem.
