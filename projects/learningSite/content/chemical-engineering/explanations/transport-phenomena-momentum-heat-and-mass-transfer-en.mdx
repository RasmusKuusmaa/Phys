Three transport laws, developed in three different historical contexts for three apparently different purposes, turn out on close inspection to be the same equation. Newton's law of viscosity says the shear stress between two fluid layers is proportional to the velocity gradient between them, with viscosity as the proportionality constant. Fourier's law of conduction says the heat flux through a material is proportional to the temperature gradient across it, with thermal conductivity as the constant. Fick's law of diffusion says the flux of a chemical species is proportional to its concentration gradient, with diffusivity as the constant. Strip away the different names for the quantities involved, and all three read: flux equals a material property times a gradient.

This isn't a superficial notational coincidence -- it reflects the same underlying molecular mechanism at work in all three cases. Momentum, thermal energy, and chemical species are all transported by the same basic process: molecules undergoing random thermal motion, which on average carries whichever property is more concentrated in one region toward the region where it's less concentrated, until the gradient driving that net transport disappears. Viscosity, thermal conductivity, and diffusivity all measure the same kind of thing from this molecular perspective -- how effectively random molecular motion in a given material transports momentum, energy, or a chemical species, respectively.

The payoff of recognizing this shared structure is genuinely practical, not just conceptually elegant. Boundary-layer theory -- how a transported quantity's gradient concentrates into a thin layer near a solid surface, whether that's velocity near a surface in a moving fluid, temperature near a hot surface, or concentration near a dissolving surface -- was worked out mathematically for momentum transfer first, and the same mathematics, the same dimensionless groups, and often the same correlations transfer directly to heat and mass transfer problems with only the relevant material property and driving gradient swapped in. A chemical engineer designing a mass-transfer operation can therefore often start from momentum- or heat-transfer solutions already worked out in the literature, rather than solving the mass-transfer problem entirely from scratch.
