Process safety as an engineering discipline starts from a blunt observation: a chemical plant's most serious accidents unfold too fast, and release too much energy, for personal caution or reaction time to reliably stop once they've begun. The discipline's actual center of gravity is therefore not protective equipment or training, useful as those are for smaller-scale risks -- it's designing the process itself so that major hazards have as few pathways as possible to ever become accidents in the first place.

The HAZOP (hazard and operability) study is the field's core systematic tool for finding those pathways before a plant is ever built or before a design change is made. A team examines the process design one section at a time, and for each section, applies a fixed set of guide words -- more, less, none, reverse, other than -- to each relevant parameter (flow rate, temperature, pressure, composition). "More flow," "less temperature," "reverse flow," and so on force the team to consider every plausible deviation from intended operation and trace out its consequences systematically, rather than relying on whichever failure modes happen to occur to the team unprompted. The guide-word structure is precisely what makes HAZOP different from an unstructured safety brainstorm: it guarantees a systematic sweep across the entire design rather than depending on which risks the team happens to think of.

The runaway exothermic reaction is the field's paradigm chemical-specific hazard, and understanding why requires seeing it as a feedback loop rather than a simple temperature rise. Reaction rate increases steeply with temperature (the same relationship the Arrhenius equation captures). In an exothermic reaction, that reaction generates heat as a byproduct. Under normal operation, a cooling system removes that heat as fast as it's produced, holding temperature (and therefore reaction rate) steady. But if heat generation ever exceeds the cooling system's removal capacity -- because of a cooling failure, an unusually concentrated feed, or simply too much reactant charged at once -- temperature begins to rise. Rising temperature increases the reaction rate, which increases heat generation further, which raises temperature further still: a self-reinforcing loop with no natural ceiling once it starts, capable of escalating from a controlled reaction to a violent, uncontrolled one within minutes. Because that escalation genuinely outpaces what a human operator can reliably notice and respond to in time, process designs build in cooling-capacity margins well beyond the reaction's normal heat load, and automatic shutdown systems that trigger on temperature or pressure thresholds long before the feedback loop can gain real momentum -- engineering the hazard out of the design itself, rather than trusting anyone to catch it once it starts.
