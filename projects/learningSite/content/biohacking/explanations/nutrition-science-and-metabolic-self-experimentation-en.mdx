Body weight change is governed by energy balance: if calories consumed exceed calories expended over time, body mass increases, and if the reverse holds, it decreases. This identity is true by the laws of thermodynamics and not seriously disputed, but treating it as the whole story badly understates how hard "just eat less" actually is to sustain, because both sides of that equation are not fixed numbers a person simply chooses -- appetite, hunger hormones, and even resting energy expenditure all respond adaptively to diet composition, sleep quality, stress, and how much body fat has already been lost, which is why sustained calorie restriction reliably becomes harder over time rather than staying equally easy throughout a diet.

Intermittent fasting -- confining eating to a shorter daily window, or fasting on alternating days -- produces real, measurable changes: markers of cellular stress response and some metabolic markers shift during extended fasting periods. But the key question for weight loss specifically is whether it produces an advantage beyond simple calorie reduction, and here the controlled evidence is clear: when trials carefully match total daily calorie intake between an intermittent-fasting group and a group eating the same calories on a normal schedule, weight loss outcomes are typically similar between the groups. In uncontrolled, real-world settings, intermittent fasting often does produce weight loss, but the most parsimonious explanation is that restricting the eating window makes it easier for many people to spontaneously eat less overall, not that fasting triggers an independent fat-burning mechanism that operates regardless of total intake.

Ketogenic diets tell a structurally similar story. Very-low-carbohydrate eating shifts the body's primary fuel source from glucose to fat-derived ketone bodies, a genuine and measurable metabolic shift (ketosis) with real effects on hunger signaling and, for some people, blood sugar control. But head-to-head trials that hold total calories constant between a ketogenic diet and a higher-carbohydrate diet generally find comparable weight loss between them, not the dramatically superior fat-burning advantage claimed by some of the diet's strongest proponents. Ketosis is real; a unique metabolic advantage for fat loss beyond what the same calorie deficit produces on any diet is not well supported once calories are actually controlled for in the comparison.

None of this makes either approach worthless -- a dietary pattern that makes calorie reduction easier to sustain for a particular person is genuinely useful, precisely because sustaining a deficit is the hard part in practice, not the arithmetic. And the adaptive response to sustained restriction is itself real and worth understanding on its own terms: resting energy expenditure and hunger hormones shift measurably during a sustained diet, which is a normal physiological adaptation happening in essentially everyone who diets, not evidence of a uniquely "broken" metabolism requiring a special fix. What the evidence doesn't support is the specific claim that either intermittent fasting or ketogenic eating provides a metabolic shortcut around the calorie balance that governs weight change for everyone else.
