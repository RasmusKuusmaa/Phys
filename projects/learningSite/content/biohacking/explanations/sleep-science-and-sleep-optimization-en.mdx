A night of sleep is not a uniform block but a repeating cycle, roughly 90 minutes long, through several distinct stages. Light non-REM sleep dominates the transition into and out of deeper stages; slow-wave (deep) sleep is when the pituitary gland releases most of its daily growth hormone pulse and the body does the bulk of its physical tissue repair; and REM sleep is when most vivid dreaming occurs and a substantial share of memory consolidation -- particularly procedural and emotional memory -- takes place. Critically, these stages are not evenly distributed across the night: slow-wave sleep is heavily concentrated in the first half of a normal night, while REM sleep episodes lengthen and become more frequent toward morning. This is exactly why cutting sleep short at different ends of the night has different consequences -- going to bed late but waking at a fixed time disproportionately cuts into REM sleep, while an early wake time after a normal bedtime cuts less into slow-wave sleep specifically, since most of it already happened earlier.

Sleep debt is a real, cumulative physiological state, not a loose figure of speech. Controlled sleep-restriction studies show that losing even a modest amount of sleep per night, sustained over a week, produces cognitive performance deficits (reaction time, attention, working memory) comparable to a single night of total sleep deprivation -- and, notably, participants in these studies often underestimate how impaired they've become, since sleep deprivation degrades the very self-monitoring ability that would otherwise flag the problem. That debt is also not repaid in full by a single long recovery night: performance deficits from a week of restricted sleep can take several nights of adequate sleep to fully resolve, which undercuts the common assumption that a weekend of sleeping in "fixes" a week of short weeknights.

This combination -- stage-specific timing within the night, and the reality of cumulative, slowly-repaid sleep debt -- is exactly why polyphasic sleep schedules fail under rigorous scrutiny. Schedules like Uberman (multiple 20-minute naps spread through the day, no core sleep block) or Everyman (one shortened core sleep plus a few naps) aim to reduce total daily sleep to a few hours by claiming the brain adapts to enter deep and REM sleep faster within short naps. When tested with objective measures -- actigraphy, EEG-based sleep staging, and standardized cognitive performance batteries rather than self-report alone -- practitioners of these schedules generally show persistent, measurable cognitive impairment consistent with chronic partial sleep deprivation, even after weeks of "adaptation." The subjective sense of having adapted is itself suspect: judgment and self-assessment are among the cognitive functions sleep deprivation degrades most, meaning the very faculty someone would use to notice they're still impaired is compromised by the deprivation itself.

None of this means sleep needs are perfectly uniform across people -- there is genuine individual variation, and rare documented short-sleeper phenotypes exist -- but the well-supported range for adults clusters around 7-9 hours, and claims of reliably functioning well on a small fraction of that, achieved by schedule restructuring alone, have not held up when measured by anything more rigorous than how the person says they feel.
