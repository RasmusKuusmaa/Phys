Direct-to-consumer genetic testing is, at its core, an accurate laboratory measurement: a saliva sample gets genotyped at somewhere between a few hundred thousand and a million specific positions in the genome, and that raw measurement is generally reliable. Nearly all of the interpretive risk in personal genomics sits downstream of that measurement, in the leap from "here is your genotype at these positions" to "here is what that means for your health" -- and that leap looks very different depending on what kind of genetic variant is involved.

For a small number of well-characterized single-gene conditions, the leap is short and the information is genuinely strong. Certain BRCA1 and BRCA2 variants, for example, are high-penetrance -- carrying one substantially raises lifetime risk of breast and ovarian cancer in a way that decades of family studies have characterized precisely. A positive result for one of these variants is real, individually actionable information, not a probabilistic hint.

Almost everything else people search their raw data hoping to learn about sits at the opposite end of the spectrum. Common conditions like heart disease, most cancers, and most psychiatric conditions are polygenic: risk comes from the combined small nudge of many hundreds or thousands of common genetic variants, each contributing a tiny effect, plus a large environmental and behavioral component layered on top. A polygenic risk score built from a consumer test's genotype data can meaningfully rank people's relative genetic risk within a population, but it typically explains only a modest fraction of any individual's actual disease risk -- most of the variance in whether a given person actually develops the condition remains open to lifestyle, environment, and chance, not fixed by the score.

A further, often-overlooked risk layer comes from what happens to the raw genotype file after a consumer receives it. Reputable direct-to-consumer companies release that raw file to the customer alongside their own curated report, and a cottage industry of unaffiliated third-party websites exists specifically to run that raw file against research-grade variant databases and hand back an alarming-sounding report of "your variants of interest." Many of these tools surface associations sourced from a single, small, unreplicated study -- a far weaker evidentiary standard than the well-replicated findings underlying an established result like the BRCA variants above. A scary third-party report deserves exactly the evidence-hierarchy scrutiny this subject's first concept introduced: what kind of study is the association based on, has it been replicated, and does the effect size reported actually translate into meaningful individual risk -- rather than being treated as a diagnosis simply because it came out of a genetic data file.
