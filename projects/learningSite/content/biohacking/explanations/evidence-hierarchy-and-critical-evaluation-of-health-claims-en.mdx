Evidence for a health claim comes in a rough hierarchy, and the position on that hierarchy is determined by one question: how well does the study design rule out explanations other than the treatment itself? At the bottom sits the anecdote or testimonial -- a single person's before-and-after story, with no comparison group of any kind. This is maximally vulnerable to the placebo effect (expecting an effect can genuinely change how a symptom feels), to regression to the mean (people tend to try a new intervention right when they feel unusually bad, and unusually bad periods tend to improve on their own regardless of what's done about them), and to confirmation bias (noticing and remembering the times it seemed to work, forgetting the times it didn't). None of these require dishonesty -- they're systematic features of how human perception and memory work, which is exactly why they can fool a careful, honest person just as easily as a careless one.

An observational study is a real improvement: researchers systematically track many people and compare outcomes between those who did and didn't do something. But because the people themselves chose whether to do it, the two groups can differ in countless other ways too -- people who choose to take a particular supplement, for instance, are often already more health-conscious in general, which alone could explain a better outcome with nothing to do with the supplement. This is confounding, and no amount of statistical adjustment can rule out an unmeasured confounder with certainty. A randomized controlled trial solves this specific problem by taking the choice out of participants' hands: assignment to treatment or control is made by chance, so on average the two groups start out similar in every way except the one thing being tested, and any resulting difference in outcome can be attributed to that one thing with far more confidence.

Even a well-run randomized trial is one study, though, not a final verdict -- which is where publication bias enters. Studies that find an exciting, positive effect are more likely to be written up, submitted, and accepted by journals than studies that find no effect, so the published literature on a popular topic can systematically overstate how well something works, even if every individual study was conducted honestly. This is exactly why a systematic review or meta-analysis -- which specifically tries to find and weigh every relevant study, published or not -- carries more weight than any single trial, however well-designed.

None of this is an argument against trying things yourself; it's an argument for calibrating confidence correctly. A personal experiment is genuinely useful information about your own body specifically, and a lower rung on the evidence hierarchy doesn't mean zero information -- it means the conclusion you can responsibly draw from it is much weaker than "this is proven to work," and the habit worth building is asking, for any claim, what kind of evidence actually supports it before deciding how much to trust it.
