A new medication reaching a pharmacy shelf has cleared a demanding burden of proof: a manufacturer has to demonstrate both safety and efficacy through controlled trials before a regulator approves it for sale. Dietary supplements, in most jurisdictions including the United States, sit on the opposite side of that burden of proof entirely -- a manufacturer can bring a supplement to market without first proving it's safe or that it does what the label claims, and the regulator's practical role is largely reactive, removing products from the market only after evidence of harm has accumulated. This isn't a minor technicality; it's the single biggest reason "it's legally sold" carries so much less safety information than people intuitively assume it does.

Two categories that biohacking communities frequently reach for sit right in the middle of this gap. Peptides -- short chains of amino acids marketed for muscle growth, fat loss, or anti-aging effects -- are commonly sold not even as supplements but as "research chemicals not for human consumption," a labeling fiction that lets grey-market suppliers skip essentially all purity and dosing quality control while still shipping product to people who intend to use it exactly that way. SARMs (selective androgen receptor modulators) are marketed as a side-effect-free alternative to anabolic steroids, a claim the actual evidence base doesn't support -- SARMs carry their own documented risk signals, including liver toxicity and adverse cardiovascular effects, on top of being sold through the same largely unregulated grey market as peptides.

A separate, frequently underweighted risk sits in drug interactions rather than any single substance's own safety profile. A supplement, peptide, or medication that looks safe in isolation can become genuinely dangerous when combined with a prescription medication someone is already taking, with a different supplement, or even with a common food -- grapefruit's well-documented interference with a range of drug-metabolizing enzymes is a classic example of an everyday food producing a real interaction. Checking for a specific interaction requires knowing a person's complete, actual medication list and how a given substance is metabolized, which is precisely the expertise a pharmacist or physician provides and precisely the step most self-experimentation protocols skip by never involving one.

None of this is an argument that self-experimentation is inherently reckless, or that every supplement is dangerous -- plenty of substances discussed elsewhere in this subject (caffeine, for instance) have a long track record and well-characterized risk profile. It's an argument that the size of the regulatory and safety gap for a given category is itself a piece of information worth actively seeking out, rather than something to infer backward from the mere fact that a product can be legally purchased.
