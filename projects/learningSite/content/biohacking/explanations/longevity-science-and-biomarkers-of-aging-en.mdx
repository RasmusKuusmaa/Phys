Chronological age and biological age measure different things: chronological age is simply time elapsed since birth, while biological age attempts to capture how much cumulative physiological wear a person's cells and tissues actually show, which can run ahead of or behind chronological age depending on genetics, environment, and disease history. Cellular senescence is one of the best-studied mechanisms linking the two. A senescent cell has permanently stopped dividing, usually in response to DNA damage or other stress, but rather than dying it persists and secretes a cocktail of inflammatory signaling molecules that damage nearby tissue. Senescent cells accumulate with age, and their burden correlates with age-related dysfunction in animal models -- but senescence isn't purely a design flaw either: the same permanent growth arrest also suppresses tumor formation in damaged cells and contributes to normal wound healing, which is why "eliminate all senescent cells" is not automatically a safe or even coherent goal.

Caloric restriction is the most extensively studied lifespan-extension intervention, and the core finding is genuinely robust: restricting calorie intake (without malnutrition) reliably extends lifespan in yeast, roundworms, and fruit flies, and does so to a smaller but still measurable degree in mice. What does not follow automatically is that the same intervention meaningfully extends healthy human lifespan. The effect size shrinks as organisms get longer-lived and more physiologically complex, and critically, no human trial has been positioned to answer the lifespan question directly -- a study large and long enough to measure human lifespan extension from a dietary intervention is not practically feasible to run. Some human trials have measured caloric restriction's effects on shorter-term biomarkers (metabolic health markers, for instance), which is real and useful data, but it's a different, narrower claim than "extends human lifespan."

Rapamycin and metformin occupy a similar position. Rapamycin, developed as an immunosuppressant for organ transplant patients, and metformin, a first-line type 2 diabetes medication, both extend lifespan in mice through mechanisms that overlap with caloric restriction's underlying biology (nutrient-sensing pathways in particular), which is exactly why they've become the two most-discussed "longevity drugs" in biohacking circles. Neither has completed the kind of long-term trial in healthy humans that would establish whether the mouse result actually generalizes, and both carry real, well-documented side-effect profiles from their approved clinical uses -- immunosuppression-related infection risk for rapamycin, gastrointestinal and rare but serious metabolic effects for metformin -- that don't disappear simply because someone is taking the drug off-label for a different purpose than it was approved for.

Set against all of this genuinely interesting but still-unsettled science, the most reliable, well-established levers for a longer healthspan remain comparatively unglamorous: not smoking, regular physical activity, and a generally healthy diet consistently produce the largest, most replicated effects on both lifespan and healthspan of anything discussed in longevity research, mouse-study excitement about specific molecules notwithstanding.
