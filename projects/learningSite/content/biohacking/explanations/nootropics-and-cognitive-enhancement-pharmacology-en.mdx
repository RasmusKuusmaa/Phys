"Nootropic" functions more as a marketing umbrella than a pharmacological category, and the substances gathered under it have wildly different evidence bases that get flattened together by the shared label. Caffeine sits at one end: it has a clear, well-characterized mechanism (blocking adenosine receptors, countering the drowsiness that accumulating adenosine produces across a waking day) and decades of well-controlled trials supporting real, if modest, effects on alertness and reaction time. L-theanine, an amino acid found in tea, has reasonable evidence for smoothing out caffeine's jittery, anxious edge when the two are combined, but much weaker evidence for producing a meaningful cognitive effect by itself.

Racetams occupy a very different position despite decades of enthusiastic use: piracetam and its chemical relatives have a large body of trials, but most are small, old, and methodologically weak by modern standards, and no clear, reproducible mechanism has been established in healthy (as opposed to cognitively impaired) adults. Community popularity and a pharmaceutical-sounding name are not the same thing as trial evidence, and it's worth noticing when a substance has accumulated decades of anecdote without ever producing the kind of large, well-controlled trial that would settle the question.

Modafinil sits closer to caffeine in having real, well-documented effects -- but the key qualifier is on whom. The strongest evidence comes from sleep-deprived or shift-working populations, where modafinil restores alertness that sleep loss took away; evidence for a comparable general cognitive boost in people who are already well-rested and healthy is considerably weaker and more mixed. Modafinil is also prescription-only for real reasons -- cardiovascular risk factors and drug interactions that a prescribing physician screens for -- safeguards a person sourcing it online bypasses entirely, on top of taking a drug for an effect the evidence doesn't clearly support in their situation.

The pattern across all of this is what matters more than any single verdict: nootropics are sold in a largely unregulated supplement market where a manufacturer faces no requirement to prove either efficacy or purity before selling a product, unlike the regulatory bar a prescription drug clears. That makes "is this specific compound's evidence good" a question that has to be asked compound by compound, using the evidence-hierarchy tools this subject's first concept introduced, rather than inferred from how enthusiastically a product is marketed or how large its online community is.
