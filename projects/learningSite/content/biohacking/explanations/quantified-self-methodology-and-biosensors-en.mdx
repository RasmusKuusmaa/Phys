Heart rate variability -- the small, beat-to-beat variation in the time interval between successive heartbeats -- is genuinely informative: a healthy autonomic nervous system continuously fine-tunes heart rate in response to breathing, physical demand, and stress, and higher HRV broadly correlates with better cardiovascular fitness and recovery status, while suppressed HRV often accompanies illness, poor sleep, or accumulated training stress. Consumer wearables that estimate HRV from a wrist- or finger-based optical sensor (rather than the electrical signal an ECG captures directly) are validated against that ECG gold standard, and the validation studies are the source of an important fact often left out of marketing copy: the correlation between wearable and gold-standard readings is generally good but not perfect, with real error margins that vary meaningfully between devices and between individuals depending on skin tone, motion, and sensor placement. Sleep-stage estimates from wearables (validated against polysomnography, the lab-based gold standard that directly monitors brain activity) show a similar pattern -- reasonably good agreement on total sleep time, weaker agreement on the precise boundaries between sleep stages.

This matters directly for how to interpret a reading. A single night's HRV number sits within a band of normal day-to-day biological variability that is often comparable in size to the change a real, meaningful intervention might produce, which means a single data point is a poor basis for concluding anything specific happened the night before. A multi-day or multi-week rolling average filters out much of that noise and is a far more reliable signal of a genuine trend -- which is exactly why professional sports science and sleep medicine both lean on trends and multi-night averages rather than single readings when they use this class of data at all.

Turning a wearable into a genuine self-experiment, rather than a source of daily over-interpreted noise, means borrowing the logic of small-scale research design. A baseline period of tracking before introducing any change establishes what normal variability actually looks like for that person specifically, which is the only way to judge whether a later change is unusual. The intervention needs to be plausible enough, and applied consistently enough, to produce an effect size larger than that baseline noise -- a genuinely tiny effect will simply be invisible against normal variability, however real it is. Alternating the intervention on and off across multiple periods (rather than a single before/after comparison) helps separate a real, repeatable effect from a one-off coincidence or a trend that was already happening for unrelated reasons.

Even a carefully designed n-of-1 experiment along these lines is still missing something a proper randomized, blinded, controlled trial has: a comparison group that didn't get the intervention, and ideally not knowing which condition you're in at a given time, both of which are what let a real trial rule out the placebo effect and regression to the mean as explanations. A self-experiment run this way is genuinely useful personal information -- it's simply a lower rung on the same evidence hierarchy every other health claim should be judged against, not an exception to it.
