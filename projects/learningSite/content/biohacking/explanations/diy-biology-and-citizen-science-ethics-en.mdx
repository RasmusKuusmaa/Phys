Community biology labs and garage-biology spaces have genuinely democratized access to real molecular-biology tools: PCR machines for amplifying DNA, gel electrophoresis for separating and visualizing molecules, and increasingly CRISPR reagents for gene editing are all available today outside university and industry settings in a way that would have been unimaginable a generation ago. Most of what actually happens in these spaces is legitimately useful, low-risk citizen science -- environmental DNA monitoring, open-source lab protocol development, and hands-on biology education for people who would otherwise never get access to this equipment.

Self-experimentation with CRISPR specifically -- a small number of highly publicized cases of individuals self-injecting homemade gene-editing constructs -- sits in a completely different risk category from that community-lab activity, and it's worth being precise about why, because the reason has nothing to do with whether CRISPR "works" as an editing technology. Three separate infrastructure gaps stand between a lab demonstration and a safe self-administered edit. First, verifying construct purity and correct formulation: a home-mixed batch needs quality control most self-experimenters have no way to perform. Second, delivery: getting an editing construct into the right cells, in the right amount, inside a living human body is a substantially harder unsolved problem than the editing chemistry itself, which is exactly why delivery -- not editing precision -- remains the central bottleneck in professional gene therapy research generally. Third, off-target detection: catching rare but serious edits that happen at unintended genome locations requires whole-genome sequencing before and after, infrastructure essentially unavailable to someone working alone.

The biosafety and biosecurity apparatus that governs institutional research -- containment levels matched to an organism's risk, institutional biosafety committees reviewing proposed experiments, oversight specifically targeting higher-risk categories like gain-of-function research -- exists because some biological risks don't stay contained to the person taking them; a poorly designed experiment can pose risk to lab workers, the environment, or the public, not just the experimenter. Whether, and how, that same oversight framework should extend to an individual experimenting on their own body entirely outside any institution is a genuinely open, actively debated policy question. Existing biosafety law was largely written with institutions in mind, and how it applies -- or fails to apply -- to a person acting alone is unsettled in a way this concept can't resolve for a learner; what it can do is make clear that the absence of a settled answer is not the same thing as the activity being safe or already adequately governed.
