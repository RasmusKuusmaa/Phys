Nearly every tissue in the body -- the liver, the heart, individual skin cells -- carries its own molecular clock, built from a feedback loop of clock genes whose protein products rise and fall on a roughly 24-hour cycle. Left in isolation, most of these peripheral clocks drift out of sync with each other and with the actual day. What keeps the whole system locked together is a master clock: a cluster of roughly 20,000 neurons in the hypothalamus called the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), which receives a direct neural projection from light-sensitive cells in the retina and uses that signal to reset itself daily, then relays timing information to every peripheral clock in the body.

The direction of that reset depends entirely on when the light arrives, described by what's called a phase-response curve. Light in the few hours after your body's natural wake time shifts the clock earlier (a phase advance); light in the few hours before your body's natural sleep time shifts it later (a phase delay); light in the middle of your normal waking day has little resetting effect either way, since the clock isn't expecting a light transition at that point. This is exactly why a traveler crossing time zones benefits from deliberately timed light exposure and avoidance (bright light at the destination's morning, avoiding light in what is still, by the old clock, evening) rather than simply maximizing total light exposure -- getting bright light at the wrong point in the cycle actively pushes the clock the wrong direction.

The SCN's other major output is control of the pineal gland's melatonin release. Melatonin isn't primarily a sedative in the way a sleep medication is; it's a timing signal that communicates "it is biologically night" to the rest of the body, and its release is actively suppressed by light and rises once light input drops. Because artificial light -- especially the blue-wavelength-heavy light common in phone and computer screens -- suppresses melatonin release just as effectively as it suppresses the SCN's own light-sensing pathway, bright screen use in the hours before bed measurably delays both melatonin onset and the sleep timing that follows it. Taking a supplemental dose of melatonin doesn't force sleep the way a sedative does; it nudges the timing signal, which is genuinely useful for shifting a misaligned schedule but not a substitute for consistent light-based entrainment.

The clock's own free-running period -- how long it would cycle if left with no light cues at all -- is close to but not exactly 24 hours, and for most people it runs slightly longer than 24 hours, meaning the SCN needs a daily light-based correction just to stay locked to the actual day, not merely to fix an occasional disruption. This is why circadian misalignment from shift work or chronic irregular sleep-wake timing is a real physiological state with measurable metabolic and cognitive consequences, not simply a subjective feeling of being tired that can be pushed through with enough willpower or caffeine -- caffeine changes how sleepy you feel by blocking adenosine signaling, but it does nothing to resynchronize the clock generating the misalignment in the first place.
