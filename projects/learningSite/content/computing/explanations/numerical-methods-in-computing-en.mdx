Many equations that arise in physics, chemistry, and engineering — most
differential equations describing real systems, for instance — simply have
no closed-form solution: no finite combination of elementary functions
(polynomials, exponentials, trigonometric functions, and so on) can be
written down that satisfies the equation exactly. Rather than giving up,
numerical methods solve such problems approximately, by breaking a
difficult continuous problem into a large number of small, simple, repeated
computational steps that a computer can execute very quickly, even though
no human could realistically carry out the same calculation by hand.

Euler's method, one of the simplest numerical techniques, illustrates the
general idea clearly. Given a differential equation describing how a
quantity changes (its derivative) as a function of its current value, and a
known starting point, Euler's method approximates the solution curve by
taking a small step forward, computing the derivative at the current point,
and using it to estimate the quantity's new value a short step later — as
if the true, generally curved solution were locally a straight line over
that short interval. This estimated new point becomes the starting point
for the next step, and the process repeats: compute the local slope,
step forward, repeat, building up an approximate solution curve step by
step, entirely through repeated arithmetic rather than symbolic
manipulation.

Because each step approximates a genuinely curved solution with a straight
line, Euler's method introduces error at every step, and these small errors
tend to accumulate over many steps. The step size controls this trade-off
directly: a smaller step size tracks the true curve more closely, since a
straight line is a better local approximation to a curve over a shorter
interval, reducing the error introduced at each step — but reaching the
same total distance along the curve then requires proportionally more
steps, so the total computation takes longer. Choosing an appropriate step
size, small enough to keep accumulated error acceptably small but large
enough to finish the computation in reasonable time, is itself a central
practical skill in applying any numerical method, and it is why numerical
methods, though only approximate, remain indispensable throughout physics
and chemistry, wherever a problem's exact solution is unknown or
impractical to compute directly.
