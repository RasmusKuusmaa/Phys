Environmental impact assessment and the ecological footprint operate at different scales -- one project, versus an entire population's aggregate consumption -- but share a common purpose: making an environmental tradeoff visible and quantifiable before it becomes an unmanaged crisis. An EIA works by systematically comparing a proposed project's predicted effects (on air quality, water quality, soil, noise, ecosystems, and more) against a documented baseline of pre-project conditions, and against the alternatives available, including not building the project at all. Crucially, an EIA is a disclosure and comparison process, not a pass/fail gate that guarantees zero impact -- it exists so decision-makers weigh real tradeoffs explicitly rather than discovering them after the fact.

The ecological footprint scales the same underlying idea up to an entire population. Instead of tracking one project's specific effects, it converts everything a population consumes -- food, timber, fiber, built infrastructure, and the carbon-absorbing land needed to offset its emissions -- into one common unit: the biologically productive land and water area required to supply that consumption and absorb that waste. That total demand, the footprint, can then be compared directly against biocapacity: the biologically productive area actually available (within a country's borders, or globally) to supply it.

When footprint exceeds biocapacity, the population is in ecological overshoot, and this is not an abstract accounting discrepancy -- it describes a real, physical process: the only way to sustain consumption above what current biocapacity can regenerate is to draw down natural capital that took much longer to accumulate than it is being consumed. That draw-down can look like depleting fish stocks faster than they reproduce, cutting forests faster than they regrow, or accumulating atmospheric CO2 faster than natural carbon sinks absorb it -- each one the same overshoot mechanism a single resource's sustainable-yield analysis identifies, just aggregated across everything a population consumes at once. A country can run this overshoot for a long time without an obvious acute crisis, especially if it imports resources or exports its waste (including carbon emissions) elsewhere, but the underlying physical draw-down of natural capital is real and continues regardless of how visible its consequences are in the short term.
