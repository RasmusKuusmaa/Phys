A warming climate's radiative-physics mechanism -- greenhouse gases absorbing and re-emitting outgoing infrared radiation -- is only the starting point; what happens next as that initial warming propagates through the physical climate system and the biosphere is this subject's own territory. Not every response to warming is stabilizing. The ice-albedo feedback is a clear example of an amplifying loop: bright, reflective sea ice reflects most incoming sunlight back to space, but as it melts, it exposes darker ocean water that absorbs far more of that same sunlight, converting it to heat that drives further local warming and further melting -- a self-reinforcing loop, not a self-correcting one. Permafrost thaw works similarly through a different pathway: organic matter frozen for millennia is, upon thawing, decomposed by microbes that release methane and CO2, both potent greenhouse gases, adding to the very warming that caused the thaw in the first place.

These physical amplifications cascade directly into ecological consequences. Sea-level rise has two genuinely independent physical drivers, not one: melting land ice (glaciers, the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets) adds new water to the ocean, while thermal expansion -- water simply occupying more volume as its temperature rises, with no ice melting required -- raises sea level even from ocean warming alone. Both mechanisms are eliminating coastal habitat and driving saltwater intrusion into coastal freshwater ecosystems.

Species range shifts illustrate a subtler ecological constraint: a species can only track a moving climate envelope as fast as its own dispersal, reproduction, and establishment processes allow, and when climate shifts outpace that realistic capacity -- especially when human land use has fragmented the corridors a species would need to move through -- the species cannot keep pace regardless of whether climatically suitable habitat theoretically exists somewhere else. Finally, ocean acidification is the ocean's own chemical response to absorbing a large share of humanity's excess atmospheric CO2: dissolved CO2 reacts with seawater to form carbonic acid, lowering ocean pH, which directly interferes with the calcium-carbonate-shell-and-skeleton-building chemistry that corals, many shellfish, and several plankton species depend on -- a biological consequence of atmospheric chemistry, working through ocean chemistry rather than through temperature at all.
