Real extinctions are rarely the product of one dramatic cause acting alone -- they are usually the cumulative end point of multiple mechanisms compounding on a population that has already been weakened by an earlier one. Habitat fragmentation is one of the most consequential of these mechanisms precisely because its damage isn't limited to the raw area lost: splitting a continuous habitat into smaller, isolated patches severs the movement of individuals (and therefore genes) between the resulting sub-populations. Each fragment's population becomes smaller than the original whole, loses genetic diversity through inbreeding within that smaller pool, and becomes far more vulnerable to a single bad event -- a disease outbreak, a drought, an unlucky run of reproductive failures -- that a larger, connected population could have absorbed without lasting harm.

Overexploitation, invasive species, and climate-driven range shifts are the other major mechanisms conservation biology tracks. Overexploitation is simply harvesting or hunting a population faster than its own reproduction can replace losses -- mechanistically identical to depleting a non-renewable resource, in that the population declines regardless of how the harvest is justified. Invasive species introduce a predator, competitor, or disease a native population never had evolutionary pressure to develop defenses against, so even a modest invasive threat can inflict disproportionate damage. Climate-driven range shifts move a species' suitable habitat geographically faster than the species itself can migrate, reproduce, and re-establish in new territory, especially when human land use has fragmented the path a species would need to travel through.

What makes real-world extinction risk particularly dangerous is that these mechanisms rarely act alone: a population already reduced and fragmented by habitat loss is far more vulnerable to a subsequent climate shift or a newly arrived invasive competitor than an intact population would have been, since it has already lost the genetic diversity and sheer numbers that would otherwise buffer against a new stressor. This is exactly why conservation strategies are built around the specific mechanism threatening a given population rather than one generic response: habitat corridors specifically restore the gene flow fragmentation severed, protected areas simultaneously address overexploitation and further habitat loss, and population viability analysis quantifies a distinct and often overlooked risk -- that below a certain population size, random demographic and genetic chance alone (independent of any external threat at all) becomes likely to drive a population to extinction, which is why conservation targets aim not just to remove threats but to keep populations above that threshold.
