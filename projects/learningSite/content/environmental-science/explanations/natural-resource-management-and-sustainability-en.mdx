The distinction between renewable and non-renewable resources is often taken to mean renewable resources are inherently immune to depletion, but that reading misses what "renewable" actually describes: a mechanism of regeneration, not a guarantee of unlimited or instantaneous regeneration. A fish population regenerates through reproduction at a specific, finite rate that depends on the size and age structure of the remaining breeding population; a forest regenerates through growth at a rate set by its species and climate; groundwater regenerates through recharge at a rate set by local precipitation and geology. Every one of these regeneration rates is a real, measurable number, not an open-ended capacity.

Sustainable yield formalizes exactly this: it is the harvest rate that can be sustained indefinitely without the underlying stock declining, and it is set entirely by the resource's own regeneration rate. Harvest above that rate and the stock declines, exactly the way a bank balance declines when withdrawals exceed deposits -- the withdrawal doesn't care how much anyone wants to spend, and a resource's regeneration doesn't care how much a market wants to buy or how much extraction capacity exists to supply it. This is precisely why overfishing, overlogging, or over-pumping groundwater are mechanistically identical processes to depleting a non-renewable resource like oil: in every case, an extraction rate exceeds what the stock (fixed in oil's case, regenerating in the others) can supply indefinitely, and the stock shrinks.

The genuine difference between the two resource categories is not whether depletion is possible -- it's whether depletion is reversible. A non-renewable resource extracted past a certain point is gone permanently; there is no regeneration process to restart. A renewable resource driven below a critical threshold can, in principle, recover if harvest pressure is reduced or eliminated in time -- a fish population with enough remaining breeding stock can rebuild over subsequent seasons, a forest can regrow, an aquifer can recharge -- but only if the intervention comes before the population, ecosystem, or aquifer has been damaged past the point where recovery itself becomes impossible (a fish population too small to find mates, a forest ecosystem too degraded to naturally regenerate, an aquifer that has permanently compacted once emptied). Sustainable resource management is precisely the discipline of keeping harvest rates within the bounds a resource's actual regeneration rate can sustain, before that reversibility window closes.
