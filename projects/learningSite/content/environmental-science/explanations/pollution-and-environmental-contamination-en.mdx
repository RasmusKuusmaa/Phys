Pollution is fundamentally a mismatch between the rate a substance or energy enters air, water, or soil and the rate the environment can safely absorb, dilute, or break it down -- exceed that capacity and harm follows, through a mechanism that depends entirely on which medium and substance is involved. Airborne particulate matter and ground-level ozone act directly on respiratory and cardiovascular tissue on contact. Excess nutrients entering water trigger eutrophication: an algal bloom fed by the nutrient surplus eventually dies and is decomposed by bacteria, and that decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen faster than it can be replenished from the atmosphere, leaving fish and other aquatic organisms to suffocate in oxygen-depleted water -- a mechanism that has nothing to do with the nutrients themselves being toxic and everything to do with the downstream oxygen crisis their excess creates.

Bioaccumulation is a distinct and especially consequential mechanism: certain pollutants -- notably fat-soluble, slowly metabolized ones like many organochlorine pesticides and heavy metals such as mercury -- are absorbed by an organism faster than that organism can excrete or break them down, so the substance's concentration in the organism's tissue climbs steadily above the concentration in its surrounding water, air, or soil, simply as a consequence of that absorption/elimination imbalance over the organism's lifetime.

Biomagnification then compounds bioaccumulation across an entire food chain: each predator doesn't just accumulate the pollutant from its direct environment, it accumulates the pollutant already concentrated in every prey item it eats over its lifetime. Because a predator typically eats many times its own body mass in prey over that lifetime, and each of those prey items already carries its own bioaccumulated burden, the concentration compounds at each trophic step -- which is exactly why a top predator (a large predatory fish, a bird of prey) can carry a body burden many thousands of times higher than the concentration measured in the water or soil at the very base of that food web, even when that base-level concentration looks negligible in isolation. This is the core reason a pollutant's real-world danger cannot be judged from its release-point concentration alone: the concentration that matters for a top predator (or a human eating that predator) is the one at the end of the food chain, not the one at its beginning.
