A biogeochemical cycle is best understood as a set of reservoirs -- places matter can sit for a while -- connected by fluxes, the rates at which matter actually moves between them. The carbon cycle has reservoirs ranging enormously in size and turnover time: the atmosphere and living biomass exchange carbon on a timescale of years through photosynthesis and respiration, the surface ocean exchanges with the atmosphere on a timescale of years to centuries, and carbonate rock and fossil fuels store carbon on a timescale of hundreds of millions of years, released back to the surface only through slow geological processes like volcanism and weathering, or now, through human extraction and combustion.

This reservoir/flux framing is exactly why burning fossil fuels causes atmospheric CO2 to rise: it takes carbon that had been locked in the slowest reservoir on the planet and injects it directly into the fastest one, at a rate that vastly outpaces the slow geological processes (rock weathering, carbonate formation, burial) that would eventually draw it back down. The atmosphere-ocean-biomass system can absorb some of this excess (the ocean, in particular, absorbs a substantial fraction), but not fast enough to prevent atmospheric concentration from rising, because the fluxes capable of truly removing carbon from the fast surface system operate on geological, not human, timescales.

The nitrogen cycle illustrates a different but equally important principle: unlike carbon, whose atmospheric form (CO2) is already directly usable by photosynthesis, nitrogen's atmospheric form (N2 gas) is essentially useless to almost every organism because of its exceptionally strong triple bond. The entire cycle therefore depends on a narrow set of specialized bacteria: nitrogen-fixing bacteria break that bond and convert N2 into ammonia, nitrifying bacteria convert that into nitrate that plants can actually take up, and denitrifying bacteria eventually convert nitrate back into N2, completing the loop. Remove or disrupt any one of these bacterial steps and the nitrogen cycle simply stops functioning at that point -- a fragility carbon's cycle doesn't share, since its atmospheric and biologically usable forms are the same molecule.

Because every cycle ultimately depends on the same organisms and the same physical environment, they are coupled rather than independent: how much carbon a photosynthetic organism can fix is directly limited by how much nitrogen and phosphorus it has access to, so a change in nutrient availability changes carbon uptake, and vice versa -- which is exactly why interventions like synthetic nitrogen fertilizer, aimed at the nitrogen cycle, produce measurable downstream effects (algal blooms, oxygen depletion) that are really carbon- and water-cycle consequences.
