An ADC samples a continuous analog voltage at regular instants and rounds each sample to the nearest of its available digital levels. The sampling theorem sets a clean condition for when this process can, in principle, fully preserve a signal: the sampling rate must be at least twice the highest frequency present in the signal. The trouble is the word "present" -- real-world signals routinely contain noise, interference, or genuine high-frequency content above whatever the sampling rate implies as its safe limit, and any such content aliases: it folds back down and reappears in the sampled data as if it were a genuine, lower-frequency signal, with absolutely no way to tell the two apart after the fact.

This is exactly why anti-aliasing has to happen before sampling, in the analog domain, using an actual analog low-pass filter that physically removes frequency content above the sampling rate's safe limit before the ADC ever touches the signal. Raising the sampling rate helps by raising that safe limit, giving the anti-aliasing filter more room to work, but it never substitutes for the filter itself -- some frequency content will always exist above any finite limit in a real signal, so the filter's job never goes away.

Quantization error is a separate concern entirely, arising even in a perfectly anti-aliased, perfectly sampled signal: an ADC has a finite number of discrete output levels (its resolution), and every analog value gets rounded to the nearest one, introducing a small, unavoidable error independent of sampling rate. On the way back to analog, a DAC's output is a staircase of discrete voltage steps, and a reconstruction filter smooths that staircase into a continuous waveform again -- the mirror-image step to the anti-aliasing filter on the way in.
