An operational amplifier's raw open-loop gain is enormous by design -- often on the order of 100,000 or more -- which sounds useless at first (the tiniest input imbalance would swing the output fully to one supply rail), but that enormity is exactly the resource negative feedback consumes to produce something far more useful: a precisely, externally controllable gain.

In the standard non-inverting configuration, a fraction of the output voltage, set by a resistor divider (the feedback resistor Rf and the input resistor Rin), is fed back into the inverting input. Negative feedback drives the circuit toward whatever output makes the two input terminals equal, and working through that condition algebraically gives the closed-loop gain as 1 + Rf/Rin -- a relationship depending only on the ratio of two resistors an engineer picks, not on the op-amp's own gain figure at all, as long as that raw open-loop gain is large enough to be treated as effectively infinite for the calculation.

That "large enough" caveat is where the ideal formula meets real hardware. Every op-amp has a finite gain-bandwidth product: its open-loop gain is enormous at very low frequency but falls off as frequency rises, and it also falls off if an engineer demands too high a closed-loop gain relative to what's actually available at that frequency. Push either variable too far, and the assumption behind the ideal formula -- that open-loop gain vastly exceeds the requested closed-loop gain -- breaks down, and the real amplifier's gain falls short of what the simple resistor-ratio formula predicts. This tradeoff (bandwidth against gain) is one of the first practical limits every analog circuit designer learns to work around.
