A transmission line's own resistance dissipates power as heat according to P = I^2 R -- power loss scales with the square of the current flowing through the line, not with the voltage. This is the entire engineering reason power grids transmit at extremely high voltage: for a fixed amount of power to be delivered, doubling the voltage halves the current, and since loss depends on current squared, halving the current cuts transmission losses to a quarter of what they were. Voltages are then stepped back down for safe distribution and end use, since high voltage is far more hazardous and harder to insulate for at the point of actual use.

A transformer performs this stepping through electromagnetic induction rather than any moving parts. An alternating current in the primary coil produces a continuously changing magnetic flux in the shared iron core; that changing flux, in turn, induces a voltage in the secondary coil wound around the same core. The induced secondary voltage scales directly with how many turns of wire the secondary coil has relative to the primary -- twice the turns, twice the voltage -- which is the origin of the transformer turns-ratio relationship: the ratio of primary to secondary voltage equals the ratio of primary to secondary turns.

Because an ideal transformer has no internal losses, it must conserve power: whatever power enters the primary coil must leave the secondary coil (voltage times current, on each side, equal). A voltage step-up by some ratio therefore necessarily comes with a current step-down by that exact same ratio -- there is no way to get more voltage without paying for it in reduced current, which is precisely the physical tradeoff, not a free bonus.

Three-phase power's advantage sits on top of this same transmission-efficiency logic: three sinusoidal voltages offset by 120 degrees from each other sum to deliver constant instantaneous power to a balanced three-phase load, whereas single-phase power delivers power that oscillates and drops to zero twice every cycle. That steadiness is why nearly all bulk power generation and transmission -- and every large industrial motor -- runs three-phase rather than single-phase.
