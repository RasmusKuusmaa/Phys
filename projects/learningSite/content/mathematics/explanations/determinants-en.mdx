The determinant compresses a whole square matrix into one number, and the
formulas for computing it — the 2×2 cross pattern, cofactor expansion — give no
hint why anyone would want it. The geometric meaning does: the determinant is
the factor by which the transformation scales volume. A determinant of 3 means
every region comes out three times larger; a determinant of 1 means volume is
preserved, as a rotation does.

Read that way, a zero determinant is immediately meaningful. Scaling volume by
zero means the transformation has flattened space — squashed a cube onto a
plane, or a plane onto a line. Information is destroyed, distinct inputs now
share an output, and no inverse can exist. This is why "determinant zero" and
"not invertible" are the same statement, and why the determinant is the standard
test for whether a system of linear equations has a unique solution.

Two clarifications. A zero determinant says nothing about the entries being
zero: the matrix with rows (1, 2) and (2, 4) has determinant zero purely because
its rows are proportional and its transformation collapses the plane onto a
line. And a negative determinant is not an error — the sign records orientation.
A reflection scales volume by 1 in size but flips handedness, giving −1. Only
the magnitude is a volume factor; the sign says whether the transformation turns
a right-handed frame into a left-handed one. Determinants exist only for square
matrices, since only those map a space back to one of the same dimension.
