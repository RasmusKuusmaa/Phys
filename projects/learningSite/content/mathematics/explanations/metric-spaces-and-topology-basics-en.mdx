The epsilon-delta definition of a limit relies fundamentally on measuring how far apart two real numbers are. A metric space generalizes this by equipping an arbitrary set with a distance function, called a metric, satisfying a short list of natural axioms: the distance from a point to itself is zero (and only zero), distance is symmetric, and distances satisfy the triangle inequality (going directly between two points is never longer than going via a third). Once a set has such a metric, every concept built on epsilon-delta reasoning -- open sets, continuity, convergence, limits -- carries over essentially unchanged, with the metric simply substituted wherever ordinary absolute-value distance appeared before. This lets these concepts apply meaningfully to far more exotic settings than real numbers: spaces of functions, sequences, or points in an abstract configuration space can all be metric spaces in their own right.

Working through this generalization reveals that open sets are the truly essential ingredient -- continuity, for instance, can be restated entirely in terms of open sets, without any direct reference to distance at all (a function is continuous exactly when the preimage of every open set is open). Topology takes this observation to its logical conclusion and dispenses with distance altogether: a topological space is defined purely by declaring which subsets count as open, subject only to a short list of closure axioms (the whole space and the empty set are open; arbitrary unions of open sets are open; finite intersections of open sets are open). Every metric space is automatically a topological space (its open sets, defined via the metric, satisfy these axioms), but topology is strictly more general, since not every topological space arises from any metric at all.

This more abstract setting lets two everyday intuitions be made rigorous. "Closed and bounded," the informal description used in a first calculus course, is replaced by compactness, defined precisely as the property that every open cover of the space has a finite subcover; in ordinary Euclidean space, the two notions coincide exactly (a specific, named result called the Heine-Borel theorem), but this equivalence genuinely breaks down in more general spaces, where compactness -- not the more restrictive Euclidean-specific "closed and bounded" -- is the property that does the actual mathematical work, guaranteeing, for example, that a continuous function on a compact space always attains a maximum and minimum value. "In one piece" is likewise made rigorous as connectedness, the property that a space cannot be split into two disjoint nonempty open pieces. Together, metric spaces and topology supply the rigorous foundation underlying essentially all of modern real analysis and differential geometry.
