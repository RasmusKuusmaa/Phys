An ordinary finite-dimensional vector space equipped with a dot product comes with a full Pythagorean-style geometry: lengths, angles, orthogonality, and the ability to project one vector onto another or expand any vector in terms of an orthonormal basis. Extending this structure to infinitely many dimensions works perfectly well in most respects -- an infinite-dimensional inner product space still has all the same algebraic structure -- but one additional technical condition becomes essential once infinitely many dimensions are in play: completeness. A space is complete if every Cauchy sequence within it (a sequence whose terms bunch closer and closer together as it progresses) actually converges to a limit that lies within the space itself, rather than seeming to converge while pointing toward a limit that is simply missing.

In finite dimensions, completeness holds automatically and is easy to take for granted; in infinite dimensions, it does not come for free, and an inner product space that fails to satisfy it is called only a pre-Hilbert space, missing limit points that genuinely belong to its own natural completion. A Hilbert space is specifically an infinite-dimensional inner product space that does satisfy completeness, and this property is not a dispensable technicality: it is precisely what guarantees that the standard tools of linear algebra -- orthogonal projections onto a subspace, convergent expansions of a vector in an orthonormal basis, the existence of a genuinely closest-point approximation within a subspace -- actually work as expected in infinite dimensions. Without completeness, a sequence of successively better approximations that ought to converge to an actual solution could instead "converge" toward a limit that the space simply does not contain.

This structure is exactly what makes quantum mechanics's mathematical formalism work: the space of physically allowed wavefunctions is a Hilbert space, and it is precisely the completeness property, largely invisible in an introductory physics course, that guarantees the orthogonality relations, basis expansions, and projection operations quantum mechanics relies on constantly (expanding a state in terms of energy eigenstates, projecting onto a measurement outcome) behave exactly as ordinary finite-dimensional linear-algebra intuition would suggest, extended now to infinitely many dimensions rather than requiring an entirely separate mathematical framework.
