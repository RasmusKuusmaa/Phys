An algebraic equation asks which *number* satisfies a relation. A differential
equation asks which *function* satisfies a relation involving its own rate of
change. That shift is the whole difficulty and the whole power: nature is full
of statements of the form "the rate at which this changes depends on how much of
it there is", and those are differential equations rather than algebraic ones.

The simplest useful type is separable: get all the y's on one side with dy and
all the x's on the other with dx, then integrate both sides. dy/dx = 2y becomes
dy/y = 2 dx, integrates to ln|y| = 2x + C, and rearranges to y = Ce^(2x). Notice
what came out — not a number, but a family of functions, one for each C.
Exponential growth and decay, Newton's law of cooling and radioactive decay all
arrive this way. The linear first-order equation dy/dx + P(x)y = Q(x) is the
other standard type, solved by multiplying through by an integrating factor
chosen to make the left side a single derivative.

The constant is where the physics enters. A rate of change cannot know where
the process started, so the equation alone gives the whole family; an initial
condition such as y(0) = 5 picks out one member. Without it the answer is
genuinely incomplete rather than merely untidy. And a warning worth carrying
forward: separable and linear equations are the well-behaved minority. Most
differential equations have no solution expressible in elementary functions at
all, which is why numerical methods and qualitative analysis matter as much as
solving techniques.
