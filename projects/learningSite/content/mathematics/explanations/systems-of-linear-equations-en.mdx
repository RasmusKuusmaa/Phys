Each linear equation in three unknowns describes a plane. Solving a system
means finding the points common to all of them, and that geometric picture
explains the whole answer. Three planes can meet at a single point, or in a
common line, or share a whole plane, or have no common point at all — and there
is no arrangement giving exactly two intersection points. That is why the count
of solutions is always zero, one, or infinity, never anything else.

Counting equations does not settle which case you are in. Three equations in
three unknowns look decisive, but if one equation is just the sum of the other
two it adds no information and the solution set is a line. Conversely, a system
with more equations than unknowns is usually inconsistent but need not be — the
extra equations may simply repeat what is already known. The reliable test is
the determinant of the coefficient matrix: non-zero means exactly one solution,
zero means the transformation collapses space and the answer is either none or
infinitely many.

Gaussian elimination is the method that settles it in practice. Add multiples of
rows to other rows to introduce zeros below the diagonal, then read the answer
back by substitution. Its virtue is that it does not need to know in advance
which case applies: a row of zeros equalling zero reveals a redundant equation
and infinitely many solutions, while a row of zeros equalling a non-zero number
is the contradiction 0 = 1 and proves there is no solution.
