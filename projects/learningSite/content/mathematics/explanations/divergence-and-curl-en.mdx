Divergence and curl both take a vector field and produce something else, but
they answer completely different questions and are easy to conflate only by
their similar-looking definitions. Divergence, ∇·F, is a scalar at each point,
measuring the net rate at which the field flows outward from that point per
unit volume. Picture a tiny sphere: if more field lines leave it than enter,
divergence is positive there — the point behaves like a source. If more enter
than leave, divergence is negative — a sink. Water flowing at constant velocity
through empty pipe has zero divergence everywhere: whatever flows in one side
flows out the other, with nothing created or destroyed.

Curl, ∇×F, is a vector at each point, measuring local rotation. Its direction is
the axis a tiny paddle wheel placed there would spin about, by the right-hand
rule, and its magnitude is how fast the wheel would turn. Crucially, curl
measures *local* spin, not global motion: water flowing dead straight at uniform
speed has zero curl everywhere, even though the water is clearly moving fast —
a paddle wheel dropped into it would translate downstream without turning.
Curl only picks up where nearby streamlines move at different rates or bend,
producing the twist that would set the wheel spinning.

Both quantities feed the two integral theorems that generalise the fundamental
theorem of calculus to vector fields: the divergence theorem relates a volume
integral of ∇·F to a flux through the enclosing surface, and Stokes' theorem
relates a surface integral of ∇×F to a circulation around the boundary curve.
These are not curiosities — they are how Maxwell's equations are written in
their differential form, translating "no magnetic monopoles" into ∇·B = 0 and
Faraday's law into a statement about ∇×E.
