Two conditional probabilities that sound similar -- the probability of a hypothesis given some evidence, and the probability of that evidence given the hypothesis -- are, in general, very different numbers, and confusing them is common enough to have its own name in legal contexts, the prosecutor's fallacy. Bayes' theorem is the exact identity that correctly converts between them: it says the probability of a hypothesis given evidence equals the probability of that evidence given the hypothesis, multiplied by the hypothesis's prior probability (how likely it was believed to be before seeing this evidence), divided by the evidence's overall probability (how likely this evidence was to occur at all, across every possible hypothesis).

The classic illustration is a medical test for a rare disease. Even a test that is 99% accurate can produce a surprisingly low probability of actually having the disease given a positive result, if the disease itself is rare enough: among a large population, the number of healthy people who receive a false positive (a small percentage of a very large healthy majority) can vastly outnumber the number of genuinely sick people who receive a (mostly) true positive (a large percentage of a very small affected minority). Bayes' theorem correctly accounts for this by weighting the test's accuracy against the disease's low prior probability, producing the actual answer rather than the naive, misleadingly high guess of "the test's accuracy."

Bayesian inference builds a full framework for reasoning under uncertainty on top of this one identity, treating probability itself as a quantified degree of belief rather than only a long-run frequency, in contrast to frequentist statistics (hypothesis testing, confidence intervals), which defines probability strictly as long-run frequency and refuses, on principle, to assign a probability to a single fixed hypothesis at all. In the Bayesian framework, an explicit prior belief is updated by each new piece of evidence into a posterior belief, and crucially, that posterior becomes the prior for incorporating the next piece of evidence -- the framework is inherently sequential and cumulative, letting belief update incrementally and consistently as more data accumulates over time, rather than being a single calculation performed once and never revisited.
