Most differential equations encountered in real physical, biological, and engineering models have no solution expressible in closed form using elementary functions at all -- exact analytic solutions, of the kind taught first, are the exception among real-world equations, not the rule. Solving such an equation numerically means starting from a known initial condition and stepping forward through time in small increments, using the differential equation itself (which specifies the slope at any point) to estimate each next value from the current one. This is not a workaround for unusually hard problems; it is the standard, everyday method by which the overwhelming majority of real differential equations are actually solved.

The simplest such scheme is Euler's method: at each step, estimate the solution's slope using only the differential equation evaluated at the current point, then move forward by that slope times the step size. This is simple to implement but crude, and its accumulated error shrinks only linearly as the step size shrinks -- halving the step size roughly halves the total error, so achieving high accuracy this way requires an enormous number of very small steps, at correspondingly large computational cost.

The Runge-Kutta family of methods improves on this dramatically by sampling the slope not just once but several times within each step, at cleverly chosen intermediate points, and combining these samples with carefully chosen weights into a single, far more accurate estimate of how the solution actually changes over that step. The most widely used member of this family, the classic fourth-order Runge-Kutta method, evaluates the slope four times per step and combines the results so that the accumulated error shrinks with the fourth power of the step size -- halving the step size reduces the error by roughly a factor of sixteen, not merely a factor of two. This is a genuinely higher order of convergence, not simply a better constant multiplying the same linear improvement, and the four intermediate evaluations are specifically weighted to cancel out lower-order error terms that repeating Euler's method four times over would not cancel. This dramatically better accuracy, for only a modest increase in computational cost per step, is why fourth-order Runge-Kutta (and its many refined descendants) remains one of the workhorse tools for numerically solving differential equations across essentially every quantitative scientific discipline.
