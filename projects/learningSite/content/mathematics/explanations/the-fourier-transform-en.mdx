A Fourier series writes a periodic function as a discrete sum of sines and cosines at frequencies that are integer multiples of one fundamental frequency, set by the function's period. Now imagine stretching that period out longer and longer, toward infinity: the fundamental frequency shrinks toward zero, the allowed frequencies pack closer and closer together, and in the limit, the discrete sum turns into a continuous integral over every real frequency. This limiting object is the Fourier transform, and it applies to functions that are not periodic at all -- a single isolated pulse, a decaying signal, any function that does not repeat -- representing such a function as a continuous superposition of oscillations at every frequency rather than a countable list of specific ones.

The Fourier transform has a deep structural property: it relates a function's behavior in its own variable to its transform's behavior in a conjugate frequency variable, and these two behaviors trade off against each other in a precise, unavoidable way. A function tightly concentrated in its own variable (a short, sharp pulse) necessarily has a Fourier transform spread out over a wide range of frequencies, while a function spread out broadly in its own variable (a slowly-varying signal) has a transform concentrated near a narrow range of frequencies. This is not a limitation of any particular measurement technique or numerical method -- it is a theorem about the mathematics of the transform itself, true for every function and its transform without exception, and it can be quantified precisely as an inequality relating the two functions' respective spreads.

This general mathematical fact takes on direct physical significance in quantum mechanics. A particle's wavefunction in position space and its wavefunction in momentum space are related to each other by exactly this same Fourier transform -- position and momentum are Fourier-conjugate variables in quantum theory. The abstract Fourier trade-off between a function's spread and its transform's spread therefore becomes, in this specific application, precisely the Heisenberg uncertainty principle: a particle's position and momentum cannot both be made arbitrarily well-defined simultaneously, for exactly the same underlying mathematical reason a signal cannot be simultaneously localized in both time and frequency. One general theorem about the Fourier transform thus underlies two results that look, at first glance, like they belong to entirely different fields.
