On the surface a matrix is just numbers in a grid. What makes it worth studying
is what it *does*: an m×n matrix encodes a linear transformation taking
n-dimensional vectors to m-dimensional ones. Multiplying a matrix by a vector
applies the transformation; the grid is the bookkeeping.

That view explains the multiplication rule, which otherwise looks arbitrary.
The entry in row i, column j of AB is the dot product of A's row i with B's
column j — not the product of corresponding entries, which is what addition
does and what most people expect. The rule is what it is because AB has to mean
"do B, then do A", and composition of transformations forces exactly this
arithmetic. It also explains the shape requirement: A's columns must match B's
rows, or the composition is meaningless.

Order therefore matters. Rotating then reflecting is not the same as reflecting
then rotating, so AB ≠ BA in general — an inequality that has no analogue in
ordinary arithmetic and that catches almost everyone at least once. Division has
no analogue either. Instead there is the inverse matrix A⁻¹ undoing A, and it
exists only when the transformation is reversible. A matrix that flattens space
onto a line has destroyed information no inverse can recover, and its
determinant is zero — which is exactly what the determinant is for.
