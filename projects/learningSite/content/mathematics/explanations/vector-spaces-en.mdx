Linear algebra starts with arrows in space, then throws the arrows away. What
actually matters is that you can add two objects and multiply one by a number,
and that these operations obey the familiar rules. Any set with that structure
is a vector space, and the theory applies to it unchanged.

That abstraction is not decoration — it is the whole payoff. Polynomials of
degree at most 3 form a vector space: add two and you get another, scale one and
you get another. So do continuous functions on an interval, solutions of a
linear differential equation, and signals in a Fourier analysis. Every theorem
proved about arrows transfers to all of them, which is why "the space of
solutions is two-dimensional" is a meaningful and useful statement about a
differential equation.

Independence is the organising idea. A set of vectors is linearly dependent if
one of them can be built from the others, in which case it carries no
information the rest do not already provide. An independent set that also spans
the space — every element is some combination of them — is a basis, and every
basis of a given space has the same number of elements: the dimension. Two
warnings follow. Having the right *number* of vectors is not enough: three
vectors in space that happen to lie in a common plane are dependent and span
only that plane. And a basis is never unique — there are infinitely many, and
choosing a convenient one is a large part of what makes linear algebra useful,
since the right basis can turn a hard problem into an obvious one.
