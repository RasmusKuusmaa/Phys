A real function is differentiable at a point if the difference quotient
approaches the same limit from the left and from the right. A complex function
has a whole plane of directions to approach from, not just two, and
differentiability demands the limit agree along *every* one of them — straight
in, straight out, spiralling in, any path at all. That single strengthened
requirement is the entire difference between real and complex calculus, and it
turns out to be enormously restrictive.

Write f(z) = u(x, y) + iv(x, y), splitting the function into its real and
imaginary parts as functions of the real and imaginary parts of z. Demanding
that the derivative limit agree from the x-direction and from the
y-direction — just two of the infinitely many directions — already forces
∂u/∂x = ∂v/∂y and ∂u/∂y = −∂v/∂x, the Cauchy–Riemann equations. A function
satisfying them (with continuous partials) turns out to be differentiable from
every direction at once: two conditions buy infinitely many for free. This is
why u and v cannot be chosen independently — pick u and the equations determine
v up to a constant, coupling the two completely.

A function differentiable everywhere on an open set is called holomorphic, and
the payoff for this rigidity is extraordinary: a holomorphic function is
automatically infinitely differentiable, equals its own Taylor series wherever
that series converges, and is fully determined by its values on any small arc,
however short. None of this holds for real functions, where differentiable does
not imply twice differentiable, let alone analytic. This is also the direct
reason complex methods solve physical problems — Laplace's equation in two
dimensions, describing electrostatic potentials and steady fluid flow alike, is
exactly the condition that u and v (or v and u) satisfy, so every holomorphic
function hands you a ready-made solution.
