Plot a complex number as the point (a, b) and you can describe it two ways: by
its coordinates, or by how far it is from the origin and in which direction.
The second pair — modulus r and argument θ — gives the polar form
r(cos θ + i sin θ), and Euler's formula compresses that to re^(iθ).

The payoff is multiplication. In a + bi form, multiplying two complex numbers
is a four-term expansion. In polar form it is: multiply the moduli, *add* the
arguments. Multiplying by a complex number of modulus 1 is therefore a pure
rotation, which is why complex numbers are the natural language for anything
periodic — alternating current, oscillations, waves. Raising to a power follows
immediately as de Moivre's formula, r^n(cos nθ + i sin nθ), turning a messy
repeated multiplication into one step.

Two details catch people out. The argument is not unique: adding 2π to it names
the same number, so every complex number has infinitely many arguments and one
must pick a principal value when a single answer is needed. And going the other
way — taking an nth root — is where the multiplicity becomes visible: dividing
the argument by n and stepping round by 2π/n gives exactly n distinct roots,
evenly spaced on a circle. It is *roots* that come in n copies, not powers.
