Ordinary calculus solves for the numerical input that makes a function's output as large or small as possible, by finding where the function's derivative vanishes. The calculus of variations asks a structurally similar but genuinely more ambitious question about a different kind of object: a functional, which takes an entire function or curve as its input and returns a single number -- the curve's total arc length, say, or the value of some integral computed along it. Which specific function or curve makes that functional's output as large or small as possible?

The answer is obtained by the same underlying logic ordinary calculus uses: perturb the candidate curve slightly and require that this perturbation produce no first-order change in the functional's value at an extremum, exactly as an ordinary function's derivative must vanish at its extremum. Carrying this out rigorously (varying the function and integrating by parts to isolate the condition on the function itself) produces a differential equation the extremizing function must satisfy, called the Euler-Lagrange equation. This single result unifies a striking range of specific problems that look, individually, quite different. Applied to the arc-length functional in ordinary flat space, it reduces to the statement that the extremizing curve has zero curvature -- recovering the elementary fact that a straight line is the shortest path between two points, now understood as one instance of a far more general principle. Applied to travel time in a medium where light's speed varies with position, it recovers Fermat's principle that light follows the path of least (stationary) time, from which the ordinary laws of reflection and refraction can themselves be derived.

Most significantly for physics, applying the Euler-Lagrange equation to the action functional -- the time-integral of a mechanical system's kinetic energy minus its potential energy -- produces exactly Newton's equations of motion as the condition satisfied by the system's actual trajectory through time. This is not a coincidental agreement between two separately-derived theories; it is the same variational machinery, applied to one more specific functional, producing Newtonian mechanics itself as one of its outputs, alongside the shortest-path problem and Fermat's principle. The Euler-Lagrange equation is, in this sense, one of the genuinely unifying results in all of mathematical physics, showing that an enormous range of physical laws share the single common structure of extremizing some appropriately chosen functional.
