Every holomorphic function with a nonzero derivative at a point is, at that point, locally just a rotation and a uniform scaling — that's what having a single complex derivative actually means geometrically, since multiplying by a complex number f′(z₀) rotates by its argument and scales by its modulus, the same amount in every direction. A rotation-plus-uniform-scaling can distort distances freely but can never change the angle between two lines crossing at that point, which is exactly the definition of conformal: angle-preserving.

This breaks down at any point where f′(z) = 0, however holomorphic f still is there — the local behaviour is no longer a simple rotation-and-scale, and angles genuinely do distort. The classic example is w = z², which doubles angles at the origin (where the derivative vanishes) while still preserving them everywhere else.

The practical payoff is solving boundary value problems on awkward regions. Laplace's equation is conformally invariant: if u(x, y) is harmonic on a region and w = f(z) is a conformal map to a nicer region (a disk, a half-plane, a strip), then u composed with f⁻¹ is harmonic on the new region too. So the standard trick is to map an ugly domain to a standard one, solve the easy problem there (often by inspection or a known formula), and map the solution back — the angle preservation guarantees the boundary conditions carry over correctly, since the geometry that determined them locally hasn't been twisted, only reshaped.
