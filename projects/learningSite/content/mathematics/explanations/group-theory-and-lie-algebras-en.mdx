A group is one of the simplest and most powerful structures in mathematics: a set of
elements together with a single binary operation satisfying four properties — closure
(combining two elements gives another element of the set), associativity, the existence of
an identity element that leaves other elements unchanged, and the existence of an inverse
for every element. The elements need not be numbers at all: the set of rotations of a
square, the set of invertible matrices under matrix multiplication, and the set of ways to
permute a deck of cards are all groups. What makes group theory physically important is that
it is the precise language of symmetry — any transformation that leaves some system
unchanged, combined with the requirement that undoing a transformation and combining
transformations both make sense, automatically forms a group.

A Lie group is a group that is also a smooth, continuous manifold, so its elements can be
labelled by continuously varying parameters (a rotation by any angle, for instance) rather
than a discrete list. Near its identity element, a Lie group can be studied by looking at
elements infinitesimally close to "do nothing," and this local structure defines the Lie
algebra: a vector space equipped with a bracket operation (the commutator) that captures how
group elements fail to commute with each other to leading order. The Lie algebra is a purely
linear object, far simpler to compute with than the full nonlinear group, and for most
physical purposes the group's essential structure can be recovered from its algebra together
with global (topological) information the algebra alone doesn't capture.

This machinery underlies the entire Standard Model of particle physics: electromagnetism is
built on the Lie group U(1), the weak force on SU(2), and the strong force on SU(3), and the
particles that carry each force (the photon, the W and Z bosons, the eight gluons) correspond
directly to the generators of the associated Lie algebra. The specific particles that can
exist, and the conserved quantities associated with each symmetry (via Noether's theorem),
follow from the representation theory of these groups — how the group's elements act on
vector spaces of physical states — making group theory and Lie algebras not an abstract
mathematical curiosity but the actual scaffolding on which the fundamental interactions of
nature are built.
