Group theory captures the algebraic structure of a single operation acting on a set, but many of the most familiar number systems -- the integers, the rational numbers, polynomials -- naturally carry two interacting operations at once: addition and multiplication, related by the distributive law (multiplication distributes over addition, exactly as ordinary arithmetic requires). A ring is built specifically to capture this two-operation structure: it is an abelian group under addition, equipped with a second operation, multiplication, that is associative and distributes over the addition, but critically, a ring's definition does not require multiplicative inverses to exist. The integers are the standard example of a ring that is not a field: they satisfy every ring axiom, but most nonzero integers (2, for instance) have no multiplicative inverse within the integers themselves, since there is no integer that, multiplied by 2, produces 1.

A field is the more restrictive special case of a ring in which every nonzero element does have a multiplicative inverse, making division by any nonzero element always possible. This single additional requirement is precisely what separates rings like the integers or polynomials from fields like the rational numbers, the real numbers, and the complex numbers. Every field is automatically a ring (it satisfies every ring axiom and then some), but not every ring is a field -- the extra requirement of universal multiplicative invertibility is a genuinely stronger condition, one that many important and useful rings simply do not satisfy.

This distinction matters well beyond pure algebra: it is specifically the field structure, not the weaker ring structure, that linear algebra's vector spaces require for their scalars. A vector space's standard operations genuinely need to divide by nonzero scalars -- normalizing a vector by dividing by its own length, or solving a system of linear equations by dividing through by a nonzero coefficient -- and this is only guaranteed to be well-defined when the scalars are drawn from a field. Scalars drawn only from a ring (say, restricting vector components to integers) would leave many routine linear-algebra operations undefined, which is exactly why the standard definition of a vector space specifies a field of scalars rather than merely a ring.
