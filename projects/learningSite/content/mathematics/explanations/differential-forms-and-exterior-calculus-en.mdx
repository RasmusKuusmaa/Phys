Vector calculus presents the gradient, curl, and divergence as three separate operations, each with its own formula, and pairs each with its own integral theorem (the fundamental theorem of calculus for line integrals, Stokes' theorem, and the divergence theorem respectively). Differential forms reveal that all three operations, and all three theorems, are special cases of a single underlying structure. A differential form is an object specifically designed to be integrated over a curve, surface, or volume of matching dimension: a 0-form is an ordinary scalar function (integrated over a point, i.e., simply evaluated), a 1-form is the kind of object integrated along a curve, a 2-form is integrated over a surface, and so on. The exterior derivative is a single operation that turns any k-form into a (k+1)-form, and applying it to forms of different starting degree reproduces the classical operations exactly: applied to a 0-form (an ordinary function), the exterior derivative is the gradient; applied to a 1-form, it is the curl; applied to a 2-form, it is the divergence.

This same unification carries over to the integral theorems. The generalized Stokes' theorem for differential forms states, in one compact statement, that the integral of a form's exterior derivative over some region equals the integral of the form itself over that region's boundary. Specializing this one theorem to different dimensions and different starting degrees of form reproduces the fundamental theorem of calculus, Green's theorem, the classical Stokes' theorem, and the divergence theorem as five instances of one single result, rather than five separately-proved facts that happen to resemble each other.

Beyond its elegance, this reformulation genuinely extends what the classical vector formulas can do. The classical curl, in particular, relies on a cross-product-like construction that is only naturally defined in exactly three spatial dimensions, and the classical formulas as usually written assume flat Euclidean space. Differential forms and the exterior derivative are defined without reference to any particular dimension or any particular choice of coordinates, and they extend cleanly to curved spaces and to any number of dimensions with no modification to the underlying definitions -- which is exactly why this is the natural mathematical language used in general relativity (where spacetime is a curved four-dimensional manifold) and in higher-dimensional geometry generally, contexts where the original three-dimensional vector calculus formulas simply do not apply as written.
