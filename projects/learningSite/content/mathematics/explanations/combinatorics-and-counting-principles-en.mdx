Counting how many ways something can happen is the foundation almost every probability calculation eventually rests on, and the two most basic counting questions are permutations and combinations. A permutation counts ordered arrangements: how many distinct ways can a given number of objects be selected from a larger set and arranged in order, where selecting the same objects in a different order counts as a genuinely different outcome. A combination counts unordered selections instead: how many distinct subsets of a given size can be chosen from a larger set, where the same objects in any order count as one single outcome. These differ by exactly one thing -- whether order matters -- and the permutation count is always equal to the combination count multiplied by the number of ways the chosen objects could themselves be internally rearranged (that factor being the factorial of the subset size), since every unordered selection corresponds to exactly that many distinct ordered arrangements.

The binomial coefficient formalizes the combination count precisely: it is the permutation count divided by the factorial that removes the redundant internal orderings, giving the number of ways to choose an unordered subset of a given size from a larger set. These binomial coefficients arrange themselves into the rows of Pascal's triangle, where each entry is the sum of the two entries diagonally above it in the previous row -- a direct consequence of a subset either including or excluding one particular element, splitting the count into two complementary cases.

These same binomial coefficients reappear, seemingly independently, in algebra: the binomial theorem states that raising a sum of two terms to a power produces an expansion whose coefficients are exactly these binomial coefficients. This is not a coincidence but a direct combinatorial fact: expanding the product means choosing, from each of the multiplied copies of the sum, whether to take the first term or the second, and the coefficient attached to a particular resulting combination of terms counts exactly the number of ways to choose which copies contributed the first term -- precisely the same unordered-subset-counting problem the binomial coefficient was built to solve in the first place. One counting idea threads through combinatorics, probability, and algebra alike.
