"Adding infinitely many numbers" is not an operation anyone can perform, so the
definition sidesteps it. Add the first n terms — a partial sum, perfectly
ordinary — and then ask what those partial sums approach as n grows. If they
approach a limit, the series converges and that limit is called its sum. If they
do not, the series diverges. Everything about series is really about that
sequence of partial sums.

Framed this way, the first surprise dissolves. 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + … has partial
sums 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, … creeping towards 1 without ever passing it. Infinitely
many positive terms, finite total. The reason is that the terms shrink fast
enough that what remains to add always fits in the gap left.

The second surprise is sharper. Terms *must* shrink to zero for convergence —
but that is not enough. The harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … has terms going
to zero and diverges anyway: group them as 1/3 + 1/4 > 1/2, then
1/5 + … + 1/8 > 1/2, and so on forever, so the sum grows without bound however
slowly. Deciding convergence therefore needs real tests — comparison, ratio,
integral — rather than a glance at the terms. And convergent series are not all
alike: conditionally convergent ones like 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − … can be rearranged to
sum to *any* value you choose, so even the commutativity of addition stops being
safe once infinitely many terms are involved.
