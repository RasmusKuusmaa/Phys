A function's first and second derivatives are a complete toolkit for sketching its graph without plotting dozens of points. The sign of f'(x) tells you where the curve is rising (f' > 0) or falling (f' < 0); where f'(x) = 0 you have a *candidate* extremum, but only a genuine sign change of f' across that point confirms a maximum or minimum — a flat spot where the sign doesn't change, like x³ at the origin, is neither.

The second derivative answers a different question: not whether the curve is going up or down, but how it is bending. Where f''(x) > 0 the curve bends upward (convex, holding water); where f''(x) < 0 it bends downward (concave). A point where f''(x) = 0 is only an inflection point if the concavity actually flips sign there — otherwise it is a false alarm, just like a stationary point that isn't an extremum.

Combining both derivatives — rising/falling from f', convex/concave from f'' — lets you sketch a curve's overall shape (its "story": where it climbs, where it turns, where it bends) directly from the algebra, which is the whole point of curve sketching as opposed to blind point-plotting.
