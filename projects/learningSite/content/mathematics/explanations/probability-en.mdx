Probability assigns each event a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain), with
the probabilities of all possible outcomes summing to 1. For equally likely
outcomes it is a counting exercise: favourable cases over total cases. The
difficulty is never the definition — it is combining events, where two distinct
questions get confused with each other.

The first is whether events are *mutually exclusive*: can they both happen? If
not, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). The second is whether they are *independent*: does
one happening change the chance of the other? If not, P(A and B) = P(A)·P(B).
These are separate properties, and it is worth noticing that mutually exclusive
events are strongly *dependent* — learning that A occurred tells you B did not,
which is the opposite of no information. Treating the two words as synonyms is
the most common structural error in the subject.

The other persistent error is the gambler's fallacy. A fair coin that has landed
heads five times running is still 50/50 on the next toss, because the coin
retains no record of what it did. Nothing "evens out" by compensation. What is
true is the law of large numbers: over many tosses the *proportion* of heads
approaches 1/2, and it does so by swamping the early imbalance with new data
rather than by correcting it. The absolute difference between heads and tails
can and typically does keep growing even as the proportion settles.
