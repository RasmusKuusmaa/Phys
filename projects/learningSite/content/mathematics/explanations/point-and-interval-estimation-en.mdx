A point estimate — a sample mean, a sample proportion — is the best single guess at an unknown population parameter, but a single number carries no sense of how much to trust it. An interval estimate fixes that by reporting a range built from the point estimate plus and minus a margin of error, E = z·s/√n: the sample's own standard deviation s and sample size n set how much the estimate could plausibly wander, and the quantile z sets how much of that plausible range to actually cover.

The interpretation is the part that trips almost everyone up on first contact: a 95% confidence interval does **not** mean "there is a 95% chance the true value is in this particular interval" — the true value either is or isn't in it, with no probability left over once the sample is drawn. It means that if you repeated the whole sampling procedure many times, about 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the true value. The randomness lives in the *procedure*, not in the fixed number you're trying to pin down.

Two knobs control the interval's width, and they trade off against each other in opposite directions: raising the confidence level (say from 95% to 99%) widens the interval, because covering the true value more often demands more room; increasing the sample size narrows it, but only as 1/√n — quadrupling n, not doubling it, is what actually halves the width.
