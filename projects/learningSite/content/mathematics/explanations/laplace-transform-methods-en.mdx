The Laplace transform takes a function of time, f(t), and produces a new function of a complex variable s: F(s) = the integral from 0 to ∞ of f(t)e^(−st) dt. What makes it useful isn't the integral itself — it's the algebra it enables. Differentiation in the time domain becomes multiplication by s in the transform domain, and integration becomes division by s. A linear differential equation, which is genuinely hard to solve directly, becomes an algebraic equation in F(s), which is easy.

The initial conditions aren't lost in this process — they fall directly out of the transform of a derivative: the Laplace transform of f'(t) is s·F(s) − f(0). This is the feature that makes the Laplace transform, rather than the closely related Fourier transform, the standard tool for initial value problems: the initial state of the system is baked into the algebra from the first step, rather than needing to be fitted in afterwards.

Solving the algebraic equation for F(s) is only the middle of the process, not the end. F(s) describes the solution in the frequency domain — to read off what actually happens over time, it has to be transformed back with the inverse Laplace transform, typically by breaking F(s) into partial fractions that match known table entries. This three-step rhythm — transform, solve algebraically, transform back — is the whole method, and skipping the last step is the most common way to stop short of an actual answer.
