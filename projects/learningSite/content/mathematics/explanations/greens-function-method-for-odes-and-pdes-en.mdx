A Green's function answers one question once and for all: if a linear operator L acts on a system with fixed boundary conditions, what does the system do in response to a single, infinitely sharp poke at one point ξ? That response, as a function of position x, is G(x, ξ) — defined by L[G] = δ(x − ξ) together with the boundary conditions.

The payoff is that any source can be built out of point impulses. A real source term f(x) is just a continuum of tiny impulses, each of strength f(ξ)dξ at position ξ. Because L is linear, the response to the whole source is the sum — really the integral — of the responses to each piece: y(x) = the integral of G(x, ξ)f(ξ) dξ. Solve for G once, and every subsequent problem with the same operator and boundary conditions becomes an integral, not a fresh derivation.

G(x, ξ) is genuinely a two-argument, piecewise object: it typically has one formula for x less than ξ and another for x greater than ξ, joined at x = ξ so that G itself is continuous but its derivative jumps — that jump is exactly what's needed to produce a delta function when L (which involves a second derivative) is applied. This piecewise structure isn't a complication to work around; it's the mathematical signature of an impulse response, and it's what makes the method work.
