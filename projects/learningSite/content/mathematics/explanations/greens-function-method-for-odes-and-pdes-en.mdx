A Green's function answers one question once and for all: if a linear operator $L$ acts on a system with fixed boundary conditions, what does the system do in response to a single, infinitely sharp poke at one point $\xi$? That response, as a function of position $x$, is $G(x, \xi)$ — defined by $L[G] = \delta(x - \xi)$ together with the boundary conditions.

The payoff is that any source can be built out of point impulses. A real source term $f(x)$ is just a continuum of tiny impulses, each of strength $f(\xi)\,d\xi$ at position $\xi$. Because $L$ is linear, the response to the whole source is the sum — really the integral — of the responses to each piece: $y(x) = \int G(x, \xi)f(\xi)\,d\xi$. Solve for $G$ once, and every subsequent problem with the same operator and boundary conditions becomes an integral, not a fresh derivation.

$G(x, \xi)$ is genuinely a two-argument, piecewise object: it typically has one formula for $x < \xi$ and another for $x > \xi$, joined at $x = \xi$ so that $G$ itself is continuous but its derivative jumps — that jump is exactly what's needed to produce a delta function when $L$ (which involves a second derivative) is applied. This piecewise structure isn't a complication to work around; it's the mathematical signature of an impulse response, and it's what makes the method work.
