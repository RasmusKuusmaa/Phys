Write the most general second-order linear PDE in two variables as Au_xx + Bu_xy + Cu_yy + (lower-order terms) = 0. The three coefficients A, B, C in front of the second-derivative terms determine everything about the equation's qualitative character, through the same discriminant that classifies a conic section: Δ = B² − 4AC. This isn't a coincidence — the characteristic curves of the PDE (directions along which information propagates) satisfy the same quadratic relation that defines an ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola, which is exactly where the names come from.

Δ < 0 is elliptic — Laplace's and Poisson's equations are the standard examples, and they describe equilibrium states with no preferred direction of propagation. An elliptic equation is well-posed with boundary conditions specified on the entire boundary of a closed region (a Dirichlet or Neumann problem), never with initial conditions on an open curve. Δ = 0 is parabolic — the heat equation is the standard example, describing diffusion, and it's well-posed with an initial condition at one time plus boundary conditions on the spatial edges, evolving forward. Δ > 0 is hyperbolic — the wave equation is the standard example, describing propagation at a finite speed, and it's well-posed with initial position and initial velocity at one time, with information travelling outward along characteristic curves.

Because A, B, C are the equation's own coefficients (not the solution), the type is a property of the equation itself and doesn't change from one solution to another. But if those coefficients depend on x and y (rather than being constants), the equation can genuinely change type from region to region — the Tricomi equation, elliptic in one region and hyperbolic in another, is the textbook example of exactly this.
