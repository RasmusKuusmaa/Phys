Bayes' theorem gives an exact formula for a posterior distribution, but for most realistic statistical models, that posterior is a complicated, high-dimensional object with no closed form and no direct way to generate samples from it. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods sidestep this by constructing something indirect but computationally tractable: a Markov chain -- a random process whose next state depends only on its current state, not on how it got there -- specifically engineered so that, after running long enough, the distribution of states it visits converges to exactly the target posterior distribution, no matter where the chain started. Once the chain has run long enough to "forget" its arbitrary starting point (a phase called burn-in), its subsequent visited states can be treated as samples from the posterior, even though the posterior itself was never written down explicitly and could never be sampled from directly.

The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm is one standard way to build such a chain. At each step, it proposes a random candidate move from the current state, then accepts or rejects that proposal with a carefully calculated probability -- one specifically chosen so that, over the long run, the chain spends time in each region of the state space in exact proportion to that region's target posterior probability. A rejected proposal is not a wasted step; it means the chain simply stays at its current state for that step, and this deliberate accept-reject mechanism, not unconditional acceptance, is precisely what guarantees the chain's long-run behavior converges to the correct target distribution.

Gibbs sampling is a different, often simpler construction available when the model has multiple variables: rather than proposing a move in the full high-dimensional space at once, it updates one variable at a time, drawing each new value directly from that variable's own conditional distribution given the current values of all the others. Cycling through all the variables this way, over many iterations, also produces a chain whose long-run visited states converge to the full joint posterior distribution. Because closed-form posteriors are the exception rather than the rule for realistic Bayesian models, MCMC methods like these are not a rarely-needed specialized tool but the standard, routine computational engine behind the overwhelming majority of applied Bayesian statistics in practice.
