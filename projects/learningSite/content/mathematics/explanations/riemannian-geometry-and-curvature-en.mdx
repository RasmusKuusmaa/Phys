A Riemannian manifold is a smooth space equipped with a metric — a rule for measuring the
length of any small displacement at every point, which in turn determines distances, angles,
and volumes throughout the space. Curvilinear coordinates already show that basis vectors can
change direction from point to point; the Christoffel symbols organize exactly how much they
change, and are what's needed to differentiate vectors and tensors correctly on a curved or
curvilinear space (the covariant derivative), correcting the ordinary partial derivative for
the fact that the coordinate basis itself is twisting underneath it. Despite carrying indices,
the Christoffel symbols are not themselves a tensor — they pick up an inhomogeneous term under
coordinate changes — which is precisely what allows a clever choice of coordinates to make
them vanish at any single point (locally flat coordinates), even on a genuinely curved space.

True, coordinate-independent curvature is captured by the Riemann curvature tensor, built from
derivatives and products of the Christoffel symbols, which measures the failure of parallel
transport around a closed loop to return a vector to its original direction — a vector
transported around a small loop on a curved surface comes back rotated, and the Riemann tensor
quantifies exactly how much, in a way that cannot be removed by any coordinate choice. Because
the full Riemann tensor has many independent components, it is often summarized through
contractions: the Ricci tensor (a partial trace of the Riemann tensor) and the Ricci scalar (a
further trace of that), both of which discard some of the full curvature information (carried
separately by the Weyl tensor) but are exactly the objects that appear in the Einstein field
equations, since they are precisely the pieces of curvature that couple to the local
distribution of mass and energy.

This is why general relativity's description of gravity as spacetime curvature is a
mathematically complete, self-contained statement, not a metaphor requiring some higher-
dimensional space to curve "into": every geometric quantity — distances, angles, geodesics,
and curvature itself — is defined purely from the metric and its derivatives, intrinsically,
without any embedding. It also explains a subtlety around black holes: the vacuum region
outside a mass has zero Ricci curvature, satisfying the vacuum Einstein field equations, and
yet is very much curved overall (nonzero Riemann curvature, carried entirely by the Weyl part)
— tidal forces and the bending of light near a black hole are real, geometric effects even
where the Ricci tensor identically vanishes.
