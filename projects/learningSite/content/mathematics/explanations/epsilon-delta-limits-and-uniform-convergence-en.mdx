Describing a limit as "the value a function's outputs approach as the input gets closer and closer" is intuitive but not itself a rigorous mathematical definition -- it cannot, by itself, be used to prove general theorems about limits. Real analysis replaces this description with the epsilon-delta definition: a function has a limit at a point if, for every positive margin of error epsilon someone might demand around that limit value, there exists a correspondingly small neighbourhood delta around the input point within which the function's output is guaranteed to stay within epsilon of the limit. This precise, checkable logical statement is what actually makes it possible to prove that limits behave the way calculus assumes they do -- that the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits, for instance -- rather than merely illustrating the idea informally.

The same need for rigor becomes essential, and considerably more subtle, once an entire sequence of functions is involved rather than a single function. Pointwise convergence means that at every individual input, the sequence of output values at that input converges to some limiting value -- but this says nothing about how the rate of convergence might vary from one input to another. It is entirely possible for a sequence of continuous functions to converge pointwise to a limit function that is not continuous at all; this is the standard counterexample showing that pointwise convergence, though a natural first notion of a sequence of functions "converging," is too weak to guarantee that structural properties like continuity survive the limiting process.

Uniform convergence is the strictly stronger condition that actually provides this guarantee: rather than allowing a different, possibly ever-worsening rate of convergence at each input, uniform convergence requires a single rate that works simultaneously for every input in the domain at once. With uniform convergence, continuity does transfer reliably from the individual functions in the sequence to their limit function, and, similarly, a limit can be legitimately swapped with an integral (integrating term-by-term) or, under suitable additional conditions, with a derivative -- operations that are simply not valid to perform under pointwise convergence alone. Distinguishing these two notions of convergence, and knowing which structural properties each one does and does not preserve, is one of the central technical concerns of rigorous real analysis.
