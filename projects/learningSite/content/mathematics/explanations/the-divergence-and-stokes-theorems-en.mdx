The fundamental theorem of calculus says that integrating a derivative over an
interval reduces to evaluating the original function at the two endpoints — the
interior detail cancels and only the boundary survives. The divergence theorem
and Stokes' theorem are the same idea, one dimension up, and recognising that
kinship is the fastest way to remember what each one does.

The divergence theorem says that integrating ∇·F over a solid volume equals the
total flux of F out through the surface enclosing it. Intuitively: add up every
infinitesimal source and sink inside the volume, and what is left over after
internal flows cancel is exactly what crosses the boundary. A volume integral
— a three-dimensional, "interior" calculation — collapses to a surface integral
— a two-dimensional, "boundary" calculation. That is the boundary pattern
repeating.

Stokes' theorem does the same thing one dimension down: integrating ∇×F over a
surface equals the circulation of F around the curve bounding that surface. Add
up every infinitesimal swirl across the surface, and the internal rotations
cancel pairwise except along the edge, leaving a single loop integral. Same
pattern again — a surface integral reducing to a boundary (line) integral.

These are not formal curiosities. Gauss's law for electric fields *is* the
divergence theorem applied to Coulomb's field, turning a hard volume calculation
into an easy surface one whenever symmetry allows a convenient surface to be
chosen. Faraday's law connects a changing magnetic flux through a surface to an
induced electric field circulating around its boundary — precisely Stokes'
theorem. Learning the vector identity and learning the physical law are, in
these cases, the same act.
